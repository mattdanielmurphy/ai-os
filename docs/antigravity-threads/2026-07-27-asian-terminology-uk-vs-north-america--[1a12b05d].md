## Assistant

Short answer: Yes, absolutely. In British English, "Asian" almost always refers to people of South Asian descent (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka), whereas in North America, "Asian" defaults to East or Southeast Asian.

This difference comes down to demographic history, colonial links, and immigration patterns.

The British Context

In the UK, people of South Asian heritage make up the largest non-white ethnic minority group.

**Colonial history & migration:** Following the end of British colonial rule in South Asia and post-WWII labor shortages in the 1950s and 60s, large waves of immigrants moved to the UK directly from India, Pakistan, and later Bangladesh. Another major wave arrived in the 1970s when South Asian families were expelled from East Africa (Uganda and Kenya).

**Demographics:** Because South Asians became the dominant visible Asian demographic in British cities, colloquial language adapted accordingly. In everyday speech, news reports, and census classifications in the UK, "British Asian" primarily refers to people of South Asian origin.

**East Asians in the UK:** People from China, Japan, Korea, or Southeast Asia are usually referred to specifically by their country of origin or collectively as "East Asian" or "Southeast Asian" to distinguish them from the broader everyday use of "Asian."

The North American Context

In Canada and the US, the immigration timeline and geography led to a completely different default.

**Pacific migration:** The earliest and largest historical waves of Asian immigration across the Pacific to North America came from China, Japan, the Philippines, and later Korea and Vietnam.

**Census and demographics:** In the US, the term "Asian American" was coined in the late 1960s as a political coalition, but East and Southeast Asian communities made up the overwhelming majority of that demographic for decades.

**Terminology:** North Americans generally reserve "Asian" for East and Southeast Asians, while referring to people from the Indian subcontinent as "South Asian" or "East Indian" (a distinction historically used in Canada to avoid confusion with Indigenous First Nations/American Indian populations).

It's one of those classic regional linguistic splits where a single geographic word developed two completely different everyday meanings based on who moved where over the last century.
