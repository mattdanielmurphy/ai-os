## Assistant

**Monty Python:** Yes, John Cleese was a founding member and key writer/performer of the **Monty Python** comedy troupe alongside Graham Chapman, Michael Palin, Terry Gilliam, Eric Idle, and Terry Jones. Beyond their flagship TV series *Monty Python's Flying Circus* (1969–1974), he starred in and co-wrote their major feature films:

**Monty Python and the Holy Grail** (1975)

**Life of Brian** (1979)

**The Meaning of Life** (1983)

**Pink Panther:** Yes, Cleese appeared in the reboot franchise, playing Chief Inspector Dreyfus in *The Pink Panther 2* (2009) opposite Steve Martin.

Key Work & Highlights

Beyond *Fawlty Towers* and *Monty Python*:

**A Fish Called Wanda (1988):** Often considered his finest standalone film. Cleese co-wrote the screenplay (earning an Academy Award nomination for Best Original Screenplay) and starred as a buttoned-up British barrister caught in a chaotic heist mix-up alongside Jamie Lee Curtis, Kevin Kline, and Michael Palin.

**Rat Race (2001):** Played eccentric casino mogul Donald Sinclair, orchestrating an ensemble cross-country race.

**James Bond Franchise:** Played "R" in *The World Is Not Enough* (1999) before stepping into the main role of "Q" in *Die Another Day* (2002).

**Voice & Supporting Roles:** Portrayed Nearly Headless Nick in the first two *Harry Potter* films and voiced King Harold in the *Shrek* sequel franchise (*Shrek 2*, *Shrek the Third*, *Shrek Forever After*).

If you loved the frantic energy and precision writing of *Fawlty Towers*, **A Fish Called Wanda** **(1988)** is the closest direct follow-up in terms of style and comic escalation.

## Assistant

The Original *The Pink Panther* (1963)

The original 1963 film was actually intended as a suave ensemble heist comedy centered around **David Niven**, who played the main character—Sir Charles Lytton, a high-society playboy and secret jewel thief known as "The Phantom".

The original cast featured:

**David Niven** as the smooth jewel thief.

**Peter Sellers** as the clumsy French police detective Inspector Jacques Clouseau.

**Claudia Cardinale**, **Capucine**, and **Robert Wagner** rounding out the main ensemble.

Although Sellers was only supposed to be a supporting player, his legendary physical comedy and absurd French accent completely stole the show. Director **Blake Edwards** realized where the real gold was and immediately pivoted the franchise to focus entirely on Clouseau.

*(Fun fact: "The Pink Panther" isn't a person or a cat—it's the name of a massive pink diamond featured in the first film that has a flaw resembling a leaping panther inside. The animated pink cat in the opening credits was created purely for the title sequence, but became so famous it spun off into its own cartoon empire.)*

Which Pink Panther Movies Are Worth Watching?

Out of the 11 films in the broader franchise, the original run directed by Blake Edwards and starring Peter Sellers is the gold standard.

Essential Viewing

**A Shot in the Dark (1964)** — **The Absolute Best Entry**
Released just months after the original, this film discarded the heist structure and put Sellers front and center. It established the essential series tropes: Clouseau's long-suffering, driven-to-insanity boss Chief Inspector Dreyfus (Herbert Lom) and his manservant Cato (Burt Kwouk), who is instructed to attack Clouseau randomly to keep his martial arts skills sharp.

**The Pink Panther (1963)**
Worth watching for its sleek 1960s aesthetic, Henry Mancini’s famous musical score, and seeing how Sellers originally built the character.

**The Pink Panther Strikes Again (1976)**
The peak of 1970s slapstick absurdity. Dreyfus completely loses his mind, becomes a James Bond–style supervillain with a laser weapon, and threatens to destroy the world unless someone kills Clouseau.

**The Return of the Pink Panther (1975)**
The movie that revived the franchise after a decade-long hiatus, featuring classic hotel-room destruction and disguises.

What to Skip

**Inspector Clouseau (1968):** A standalone attempt starring Alan Arkin without Sellers or Edwards.

**Trail (1982) & Curse (1983) of the Pink Panther:** Assembled after Sellers died in 1980 using leftover outtakes and body doubles.

**The Steve Martin Reboots (2006 & 2009):** Broad 2000s slapstick that rarely captures the timing of the originals.

Stanley Kubrick's *Dr. Strangelove* remains one of the greatest comedic flexes in cinema history. Sellers played three entirely distinct roles—RAF Group Captain Lionel Mandrake, US President Merkin Muffley, and the ex-Nazi scientist Dr. Strangelove—improvising huge chunks of his dialogue for all three.
Peter Sellers in Dr. Strangelove. Source: eBay

The Steve Martin reboots fundamentally misunderstood what made Sellers' Inspector Clouseau work. Martin played Clouseau as a self-aware, goofy cartoon. Sellers played him with deadpan dignity—Clouseau genuinely believed he was the most brilliant, suave, and razor-sharp detective on the planet, completely oblivious to the chaos he left in his wake.

Beyond *Dr. Strangelove* and the essential Pink Panther films (*A Shot in the Dark* and *The Pink Panther Strikes Again*), a few non-Panther Peter Sellers performances stand out:
A Shot in the Dark (1964). Source: Wikipedia

Essential Non-Panther Peter Sellers Movies

**Being There (1979)** — **His Masterpiece**
His final great role before his death in 1980. Sellers plays Chance, a simple-minded, sheltered gardener whose entire knowledge of the world comes from television. Through a series of misunderstandings, his naive remarks about plants are interpreted as profound political wisdom, launching him into the inner circle of Washington power players. It is a quiet, subtle, brilliant satire.

**The Party (1968)**
Reuniting with *Pink Panther* director Blake Edwards, Sellers plays Hrundi V. Bakshi, an accident-prone background actor who accidentally gets invited to an exclusive Hollywood producer's dinner party. It is almost pure physical comedy and slapstick, escalating into complete anarchy.

**The Ladykillers (1955)**
A classic Ealing Studios dark comedy. Sellers stars alongside Alec Guinness as part of an eccentric gang of criminals posing as a string quartet while planning a heist inside an elderly widow's house.

**The Mouse That Roared (1959)**
Similar to *Dr. Strangelove*, Sellers plays three roles here: the Prime Minister, the Grand Duchess, and the incompetent military leader of a tiny European duchy that declares war on the United States, expecting to lose and receive foreign aid, only to accidentally win.

Starting with **A Shot in the Dark** and then jumping to **Being There** offers a clear view of how completely he could shift between outrageous physical farce and total restraint.
