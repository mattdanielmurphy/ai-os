## Assistant

The short answer: you're not imagining it. Central Alberta is currently sitting right in the middle of its **wettest summer on record**. Edmonton and surrounding areas have racked up 19 thunderstorm days in July alone (after 15 in June), shattering rainfall records going back over 110 years.  
Global News

The relentless convective activity over the past month stems from a few converging atmospheric mechanisms:

1. The Persistent Jet Stream & Trough Setup

For most of June and July, the large-scale jet stream pattern has been "locked" in place across Western Canada. While a massive **heat dome** pushed into the US Plains and eastern Prairies, central Alberta sat right on the active western boundary—the gradient zone. Low-pressure troughs and disturbances coming off the Pacific continuously drag cool upper-level air over the mountains, where it crashes directly into hot, humid air sitting over the Plains. That boundary is essentially a severe-storm conveyor belt.  
Alberta Farmer Express
+ 1

2. Orographic Lift Meets Unstable Air

Cold, dense air pushes eastward from the Rockies and foothills. As it hits the terrain, it forces the warm, moist surface air to rise rapidly (*orographic lift*). In a normal summer, this happens in bursts, but the upper-level jet stream dynamics this year have provided constant, strong **wind shear** (changing wind speeds and directions with height). Shear keeps storm updrafts separate from their downdrafts, allowing storms to persist for hours and organize into squall lines or supercells instead of collapsing.

3. The "Moisture Feedback Loop" (Atmosphere on Gasoline)

This is what turned an active pattern into a relentless one. The initial massive dumps of rain in June completely saturated the soil and vegetation. Through **evapotranspiration**, all that standing water and lush plant life pumps massive amounts of moisture back into the boundary layer of the atmosphere on warm days.

+ 1

Environment Canada meteorologists essentially describe atmospheric moisture as fuel. Because the soil can't absorb any more water, every warm morning creates high dew points locally, generating massive convective energy by 3:00 PM without needing a major weather system to push moisture in from elsewhere. The storms feed on the humidity generated by the *previous* day's storms.  
Global News

**Where things stand right now:** Over 580 mm of rain has fallen in the region since June 1—nearly triple the typical summer average. Until a dry, broad ridge of high pressure shifts in to break the moisture loop and push the jet stream north, the afternoon convective cycle is going to keep firing off.

## Assistant

When weather stations report **580 mm of rain**, it means that if all the rainfall over that period didn't drain away, evaporate, or soak into the ground, it would form a standing layer of water **580 millimeters (about 58 centimeters, or 23 inches) deep** across every square meter of flat ground in the area.

To put 580 mm into perspective:

**It’s nearly waist-deep water:** 58 cm is about knee-to-thigh height on an adult.

**The sheer weight:** 1 millimeter of rain over 1 square meter equal 1 liter of water (weighing 1 kg). So 580 mm means **580 liters (1,280 lbs) of water** fell on every single square meter of land.

**Over a typical residential lot:** On a standard 500 m² property, 580 mm of rain translates to **290,000 liters (76,600 gallons)** of water dumped onto that single lot. That’s enough to fill about five average-sized backyard swimming pools.

In central Alberta, normal total rainfall for an *entire year* (including melted snow) is around 450 to 500 mm, with summer usually bringing about 200 mm. Cracking 580 mm in under two months means the region received more than a full year's worth of precipitation in a fraction of the time—on ground that was already saturated.
