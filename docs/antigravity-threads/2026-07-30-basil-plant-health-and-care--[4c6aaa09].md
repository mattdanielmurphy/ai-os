Health Overview of Bastille

Based on the provided photos and your description, Bastille is exhibiting signs of severe rootbound stress, erratic watering, and the early stages of bolting (flowering). Grocery store basil is typically sown with dozens of seeds in a single tiny pot to look lush on the shelf, meaning they quickly run out of space and nutrients.

Here is a breakdown of Bastille's current condition and answers to your questions.

Roots and Pot Size

Your suspicion about the densely packed roots is correct. Bastille is heavily rootbound.

**The Issue:** When roots run out of room, they circle the inside of the pot, displacing the soil. Without enough soil to hold water, the plant dries out incredibly fast—which explains why you find it bone dry so often.

**The Yellowing Leaves:** In `IMG_1713.jpg` and `IMG_1714.jpg`, the yellow and brown leaves at the base are a direct result of this root stress and nutrient depletion. The plant is cannibalizing its older, lower leaves to funnel energy to the new growth at the top.

**The Fix:** Bastille needs to be repotted into a larger container (at least 6 to 8 inches in diameter) with fresh, well-draining potting soil. Gently massage the root ball to loosen the densely packed roots before placing it in the new pot.

Buds and Pruning (Height Management)

You are entirely correct about the buds shown in `IMG_1715.jpg`, `IMG_1716.jpg`, and `IMG_1717.jpg`.

**Bolting:** These are flower buds. When a basil plant feels stressed (due to lack of water, space, or extreme heat) or simply reaches maturity, it attempts to reproduce by flowering.

**Flavor Impact:** Once basil flowers, its hormones change. The plant stops producing new leaves, and the existing leaves become tough, bitter, and lose their signature sweet, savory flavor.

**Action:** Pinch or cut off those buds immediately. You were right to target the one in `IMG_1715.jpg`, but you should remove all of them to force the plant back into a vegetative state.

**Height:** Basil should not grow as a single, tall, leggy stalk like Bastille has. When you prune a basil stem just above a pair of leaves (a node), the plant will sprout two new branches from that cut, making it bushier. Because Bastille hasn't been pruned down, he has grown straight up toward the light.

Watering and Automation

Erratic watering—letting it go bone dry and then soaking it—causes severe cellular stress, which can lead to the dark stippling and tissue damage seen on the leaves in `IMG_1718.jpg`.

Automating your watering is very easy and highly recommended if you forget frequently:

**Watering Globes/Bulbs:** You can buy glass or terracotta watering globes. You fill them with water and jam the spike into the soil; capillary action slowly draws the water into the dirt as it dries. However, **these will not work right now** because Bastille's pot is too full of roots to fit the spike or absorb water properly. You must repot him first.

**Self-Watering Pots:** When you repot, consider buying a self-watering planter. These pots have a reservoir at the bottom and a wicking mechanism that constantly pulls moisture up into the soil, keeping it perfectly damp without rotting the roots.

Quick Basil Rundown

If you want to keep basil thriving long-term, follow these fundamentals:

**Sunlight:** Basil requires high energy. It needs 6 to 8 hours of direct, bright light per day. If it doesn't get enough, it will stretch and become "leggy" to reach for the light.

**Water:** The soil should be consistently moist like a wrung-out sponge, never bone dry and never sitting in a puddle of standing water.

**Harvesting:** Never pluck the large leaves off the bottom of the stem. Always harvest from the top down by cutting the stem just above a leaf node. This removes the apical meristem (the top growth point) and forces the plant to grow wide rather than tall.

1. The Issue with Height

While growing tall does help the plant reach the light, it comes at a structural and productive cost. When basil is allowed to grow straight up without pruning, the main stem turns woody and rigid (you can see the lower stem starting to look brown and bark-like in `IMG_1713_2.jpg`). A woody stem stops producing side branches and drops its lower leaves, meaning you end up with a top-heavy plant and very few usable leaves. Pruning it forces the plant to stay compact and redirect its energy into generating a dense bush of foliage rather than a single, fragile stalk.

Keeping him inside is a smart move. Tossing an indoor-acclimated plant out into the unpredictable Alberta summer weather—especially if you might forget him out there during a scorching afternoon or a sudden storm—would definitely be a death sentence.

Aim for something that roughly doubles the current soil volume. If you use a taller pot, ensure it still has drainage holes at the bottom so water doesn't pool and cause root rot.

Pruning and Plant Shock

Your logic regarding plant stress and homeostasis is fundamentally correct. Slashing away half of a plant’s solar panels (leaves) causes trauma, and if you take too much, the plant won't have enough energy to recover and will die.

However, basil essentially evolved to be grazed. It thrives on being chopped. When a stalk grows tall, the tip produces hormones that suppress the growth of side branches (botanists call this "apical dominance"). By cutting off the top of the stalk, you remove that hormone, and the plant panics in a good way—it immediately flushes energy into the side nodes to create a wider, bushier canopy.

**The Golden Rule of Pruning:** Never remove more than **1/3 of the plant's total foliage** at one time.

How to Trim Bastille (Height Reduction)

Yes, you should definitely reduce his height. A tall, top-heavy plant is going to be highly annoying to transport and will likely snap, and dorm room windowsills are notoriously cramped.

Here is exactly how far down you can go and how to do it:

**Isolate a stalk:** Look at one of Bastille's tallest stems.

**Find a node:** Trace your way down from the top. You are looking for a "node"—the intersection where two leaves grow opposite each other out of the main stem.

**Make the cut:** Snip the stem about a quarter-inch *above* that pair of leaves.

**The Result:** Do not leave a bare stump. As long as the stalk still has a few healthy leaves left below your cut to catch sunlight, it will survive. Those two leaves right below your cut will turn into two brand new branches growing outward.
