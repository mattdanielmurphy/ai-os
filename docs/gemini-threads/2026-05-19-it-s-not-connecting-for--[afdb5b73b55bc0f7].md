## Gemini

To understand how indeterminism emerges from a deterministic foundation, it helps to look at the "many-to-one" relationship between levels of reality. This is often called coarse-graining.


1. The Many-to-One Relationship
In physics, a single "macro-state" (what we see and experience) can be realized by a near-infinite number of different "micro-states" (the exact positions of every atom).


Imagine you are looking at a digital photo of a person.



* The Micro-level: This is the individual pixels. Each pixel has one specific color value. If you know the state of every pixel, the image is fixed.

* The Macro-level: This is the person in the photo.


If you change a few pixels in the background, the macro-image—the "person"—remains exactly the same. This means one macro-state is consistent with many different micro-states.


2. Where the "Branching" Comes From
The "indeterminism" comes from the fact that the laws governing the macro-level (like psychology or biology) don't have access to the specific details of the micro-level.


Suppose a deterministic micro-level means that if you are in Micro-state A, you must end up in Micro-state B.
However, at the macro-level, you are in Macro-state 1. Macro-state 1 is made up of a "cloud" of millions of micro-states (A, C, D, E...).



* Micro-state A leads to a future where you drink coffee.

* Micro-state C leads to a future where you drink tea.


Because both Micro-state A and Micro-state C are identical "versions" of you at the macro-level, the macro-level description must branch. From the perspective of "you" as an agent, both futures are genuinely possible continuations of your current state.


3. Why this isn't just "ignorance"
A common rebuttal (like the one you noted from Sam Harris) is that this is just epistemic—meaning we are just too "ignorant" to see the underlying atoms.


Christian List’s argument is that this indeterminism is ontic (real) because the macro-level is an autonomous level of reality with its own laws. We don't say "pressure" or "temperature" are "fake" just because they are made of moving atoms. They are real properties that allow us to predict how a boiler works.


If our best scientific theories of human behavior (psychology, sociology, economics) require us to treat agents as having choices to make accurate predictions, then "choice" and "alternative possibilities" are as scientifically real as "electrons" or "gravity."


4. Summary: The source of the "Open" Future
The indeterminism "comes from" the loss of information that happens when you move from atoms to agents.



* At the bottom: The path is a single line.

* At the top: Because many different "lines" at the bottom look like the same "state" at the top, the path appears to—and effectively does—branch.


In this view, you could have "done otherwise" because the macro-state you were in was physically compatible with more than one outcome. The "you" that exists at the level of thoughts and intentions is a system that genuinely has multiple valid future paths.

## Gemini

The gap in understanding often comes from how we visualize the relationship between those levels. It isn’t that the brain "overrides" its programming; rather, it’s that "programming" looks different depending on the resolution at which you view the system.


The "Fork" in the Macro-Level
The argument isn't just that many micro-states lead to the same macro-state. The crucial part is what happens next.


If you are in Macro-state 1 (e.g., "deciding what to drink"), that state is supported by a massive collection of possible micro-states ($M_1, M_2, M_3...$).



* If the world is at micro-state $M_1$, the deterministic laws of physics might dictate that you will end up drinking coffee.

* If the world is at micro-state $M_2$, those same laws might dictate that you will end up drinking tea.


Because both $M_1$ and $M_2$ are "realizations" of the exact same macro-state (you, with your thoughts and intentions), the macro-level description genuinely branches. From the perspective of the laws of psychology or agency, both "choosing coffee" and "choosing tea" are real, open possibilities.


The Weather vs. The Agent
Your weather analogy is actually quite close to how List sees the world, but with one major distinction: Intentionality.


A cloud is a macro-state, and its behavior (raining or not) is determined by micro-states. However, a cloud doesn't have "goals" or "beliefs." We don't need to treat a cloud as an "agent" to explain what it does; we can just use fluid dynamics.


Humans are different because our best scientific descriptions of human behavior—the ones that actually work for predicting what people do—require us to treat people as intentional agents. We have to talk about "reasons," "intentions," and "choices." If these high-level descriptions are the only way to accurately describe the system, List argues that the properties they describe (like "having a choice") must be considered real.


Is the Mind a Machine?
List doesn't necessarily argue that the mind is fundamentally different from a machine. Instead, he argues that even a "machine" can have indeterministic properties if you look at it from the right level.


Think of a computer program designed to play a game with a "random" element. At the level of the hardware (the electrons moving through gates), it might be entirely deterministic. But at the level of the software (the game logic), the "choice" the AI makes is a real branching point in the game's state-space.


The "Could Have Done Otherwise" Problem
To the Sam Harris point: if "you" are defined only as the collection of neurons and atoms, then yes, there is only one path. But List’s point is that "you" are a macro-level entity.


"Could have done otherwise" means that the macro-state you were in was physically compatible with more than one future macro-state. If the laws of the agent-level (psychology/decision-making) allow for multiple outcomes, then "doing otherwise" is a scientifically legitimate description of what happened.


It isn't an "illusion" of free will caused by ignorance of the atoms; it is a real property of a complex system that exists specifically at the level of agents and their intentions. In this view, the "programming" isn't being overridden—the programming is the macro-level process of weighing options and making a choice between multiple real possibilities.

## Gemini

You’ve hit on the exact tension that makes this debate so difficult. It isn’t just a matter of "scaling up" physics; it’s a question of which level of description "owns" the truth.


To address your points directly:


1. The "Crossing Levels" Objection
You are absolutely right that the macro-state is reliant on the micro-state. In philosophy, this is called supervenience: there can be no change at the macro-level without a change at the micro-level.


However, List’s argument is that determinism is not a property that must transfer between levels.


Think of it like a computer game. At the micro-level (the code and transistors), every single electron movement is deterministic. But at the macro-level (the game's rules and the AI’s behavior), we might describe the AI as having "choices" or "branching strategies."


If the macro-level description is the only one that can explain the behavior (e.g., "The AI moved left because it wanted to avoid the fire"), then that macro-level property is "real" for that level. The fact that it's built on deterministic atoms doesn't mean the behavior at the high level is deterministic in the same way.


2. "Who is 'They'?" (The Self vs. The System)
This is where the Sam Harris/nondualism point comes in. Harris argues that because there is no "captain" at the center of the brain, there is no agency.


Christian List takes a functionalist view. He doesn't look for a "soul" or a "deity" inside the head. Instead, he defines an "agent" as a system that:



* Has goals and beliefs (Intentionality).

* Processes information to act on those goals.

* Could have acted differently given the rules of its own level.


In this view, the "They" isn't a mystical spark; the "They" is the entire biological system. You don't "override" your programming because you are the programming—but it's a high-level, flexible programming that operates through the weighing of reasons, not just the bumping of atoms.


3. Causal Control: "Along for the Ride" vs. "Driving"
You mentioned that we feel like we’re just observing our thoughts. List argues that for a decision to be "ours," our mental states must be the cause of the action.


If we try to explain why you drank coffee using only physics, we have to track trillions of atoms. This explanation is "correct" at the micro-level, but it misses the causal story. The real reason you drank coffee is that you were tired and believe coffee helps.


List argues that the macro-cause (the intention) is the proportionate cause. The physics is just the "machinery" that makes the intention possible. If the intention is the real cause, then the agent isn't just "along for the ride"—the agent's internal processing is what is actually directing the outcome.


4. "Could Have Done Otherwise" (The Weather Analogy)
Your weather analogy is perfect for a system without agency. A cloud "rains" because of physical necessity. It has no goals, no reasons, and no "intentional" level of description.


But humans are intentional systems. If we want to predict what a person will do, "he wants to win the game" is a better, more accurate scientific tool than "his neurons are firing in pattern X."


List’s core claim is that if the most accurate scientific description of a human (at the level of psychology) requires us to say they have "alternative possibilities," then those possibilities are real features of the macro-world.


It’s like saying "solidity" is a real feature of a table. At the atomic level, the table is mostly empty space. But it would be wrong to say "the table isn't really solid." Solidity is a real property that exists at the macro-level. List is saying "free will" (defined as branching possibilities for an intentional agent) is a real property that exists at the human level.


Summary of the "Agency" Source
Agency doesn't come from a "special" substance. It comes from the complexity of the system.



* At the bottom: One path (Deterministic).

* At the top: Many different micro-paths look like the same "choice" to the agent. Because the agent's "state" is compatible with more than one outcome, the agent genuinely "could have done otherwise" from their own perspective and the perspective of the laws governing agents.


You don't have to be a "deity" to have agency; you just have to be a system whose behavior is best explained by goals and choices rather than just particle collisions.

## Gemini

The "nonsensical" feeling often comes from trying to force two different, equally valid "maps" of the world to look exactly the same. The analogy of the table being both solid and a collection of atoms is actually the most scientifically accurate way to look at it, and it mirrors Christian List's point perfectly.


1. The Table Analogy: Reality is Layered
If you say, "The table isn't really solid; it’s just atoms," you are making a claim that only the bottom level of physics is "real." But if you want to understand why your coffee cup doesn't fall through to the floor, the "solid" map is much more useful—and more accurate—than a map of trillion-year-old subatomic collisions.


List argues that free will is like "solidity." It is a real, functional property that exists at the level of human beings. It isn't a "lie" told by our brains; it is a description of how a complex biological system interacts with its environment.


2. The "Author" Problem
The Sam Harris argument—that we aren't the "true authors" because we didn't choose our genes or upbringing—is a micro-level argument. From the perspective of atoms and history, you are a sequence of causes.


However, from the perspective of Agency (the macro-level), the "author" is the system that processes the information and makes the move. If a self-driving car avoids a pedestrian, we say the car’s software made a "decision" based on its sensors. We don't say "the car is just along for the ride while the code does the work," because the car is the code and the hardware acting as a unit.


List would argue that "You" are the high-level system. When that system weighs two options and picks one, "Authoring" is exactly what that system is doing at that level of reality.


3. The "Cope" and Responsibility
Taking solace in determinism when things go wrong isn't necessarily a "cope"; it’s a shift in perspective.



* The Macro Perspective: "I made a bad choice, and I am responsible for the consequences because I am an agent with goals." This is useful for learning and social function.

* The Micro Perspective: "My brain was in a state determined by my past, and I couldn't have done otherwise at the atomic level." This is useful for compassion and reducing the paralysis of guilt.


Both are "true" in their own domain. The conflict arises when we try to use the micro-level map (atoms/causality) to solve a macro-level problem (intentions/ethics).


4. Is it just a Definition Problem?
A lot of it is.



* The Hard Determinist defines "Free Will" as a mystical, god-like ability to break the laws of physics. Since that doesn't exist, they say free will is an illusion.

* The List/Carroll/Dennett Compatibilist defines "Free Will" as a high-level capacity of an agent to make choices between multiple real possibilities in their environment. Since that does exist, they say free will is real.


You aren't being "un-Bayesian" or fundamentalist by seeing both sides. In fact, accepting that there are multiple, non-contradictory levels of description is a key part of "Poetic Naturalism," the framework Sean Carroll often discusses.


5. Nondualism and the "Decision Maker"
The feeling that there is a "consciousness" observing a "decision maker" is likely a result of how our brains process internal monitoring. If you drop the idea that there is a "little man" inside the head (the homunculus) and instead see the entire organism as a single system processing data, the "who is making the decision?" question starts to dissolve.


The system—the entire "you"—is making the decision. The fact that you can observe yourself doing it doesn't mean you aren't the one doing it.


In short: you aren't "choosing but not choosing." You are a system that is indeterministic at the macro-level (you have real choices as an agent) but deterministic at the micro-level (your atoms follow physics). Neither level "overrides" the other; they are just different ways of describing the same reality.

## Gemini

The tension between the "useful fiction" view and the "ontic reality" view is the heart of the debate between hard determinists and level-relative compatibilists. The disagreement isn't about whether the atoms are deterministic; it’s about whether the "branching" we see at the human level is a real feature of the world or just a result of our blurry vision.


1. The "In Principle" Argument (The LLM Analogy)
The point about LLMs or computer programs is a strong one: if you had the seed and the weights, you could predict every token. You argue that even if it's too complicated for us to calculate, the "choice" is still just a row of dominoes.


Christian List’s counter-argument is that "in principle" is doing a lot of heavy lifting there. In science, we don't define what is "real" based on an imaginary, all-knowing observer (Laplace’s Demon) who can see every atom. We define what is real based on our best, most successful scientific theories.


If our best theory of an LLM requires us to speak about "parameters," "attention," and "probabilistic outputs" to understand what it’s doing, then those properties are the "real" ones for that system. If we tried to describe an LLM’s output using only the movement of electrons through silicon, we wouldn't just be doing "harder math"—we would lose the ability to explain why it wrote a poem instead of a shopping list. The "meaning" and the "choice" exist at the level of the software, not the hardware.


2. Is it a "Useful Fiction" or a "Fact"?
The "useful fiction" stance suggests that free will is a polite lie we tell ourselves. List argues it is more like "air pressure."



* At the micro-level, there is no such thing as "pressure"—there are only individual molecules moving at different speeds.

* However, pressure isn't a "fiction" or an "illusion." It is a real, measurable physical property that can pop a balloon.


List applies this to agency: if "making a choice" is a necessary part of the best scientific description of human behavior, then it is as real as air pressure. It isn't that the "program ended up that way"—it's that the program is a decision-making engine, and "deciding" is the accurate description of its function.


3. The "Row of Dominoes" vs. The "Branching Tree"
The "dominoes" visualization represents a single, linear chain of cause and effect. But List’s mathematical argument (the "nerdy" one) suggests that because one macro-state (the agent) is realized by many different micro-states, the "causal tree" for the agent genuinely branches.


Imagine a single macro-state (you) as a wide gate. Behind that gate are millions of different micro-level "domino tracks."



* Some tracks lead to the "Coffee" exit.

* Some tracks lead to the "Tea" exit.


Because the laws of psychology and agency only "see" the gate (the macro-state) and not the individual tracks, the future for that agent is truly open. Both exits are physically reachable from the current macro-state. In this view, "could have done otherwise" isn't a statement about the atoms; it's a statement about the agent's state-space.


4. Morality and Culpability
There is actually a significant overlap between these two views when it comes to the "real world."



* The Hard Determinist: We should focus on rehabilitation because people are the result of their "programming."

* The Level-Relative Compatibilist: We should focus on rehabilitation because we are treating the "agent" as a system that can process new information (punishment/reward) to change its future "choices."


The difference is mostly in the "why." For a determinist, the person is a "broken machine" that needs fixing. For List, the person is a "responsible agent" whose decision-making process was flawed but who genuinely occupies a level of reality where they have the capacity to steer the system.


Ultimately, if the "useful fiction" is so strong that it’s impossible to live without it, and it maps perfectly onto how we successfully navigate the world, the distinction between "useful fiction" and "real property" begins to blur. Whether you call it a "real property of a high-level system" or a "necessary illusion of a complex machine," the functional result is the same: we live in a world where choices matter.
