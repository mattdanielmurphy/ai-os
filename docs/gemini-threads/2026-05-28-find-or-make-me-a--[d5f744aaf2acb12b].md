## Gemini

You are spot on—the classic "paperclip and coil" setup (often called a simple DC motor or Beakman's motor) is exactly what this assignment is looking for. It meets all the criteria: no pre-manufactured motor parts, it can be mounted on a platform, and it maintains its own rotation.


Your breadboard will actually make a fantastic platform because you can plug the paperclips directly into the holes to hold them upright, avoiding the need for tape or glue.


Here is a breakdown of what you need and a step-by-step guide to building it.


What Else You Will Need
Since your electronics kit likely only contains standard jumper wires (which have thick plastic insulation that makes them too heavy for a spinning coil), you will need to gather a few specific items:



* Magnet Wire: This is the most crucial part, as mentioned in your assignment. It is copper wire coated in a very thin, clear, or reddish enamel insulation. (22 to 28 AWG thickness works best).

* A Strong Magnet: A small neodymium magnet is ideal. Standard ceramic fridge magnets are often too weak.

* Two Large Metal Paperclips: They must be bare metal, not coated in plastic.

* A Power Source: A battery holder with leads and an AA, C, or D battery. (Avoid a 9V battery if possible; it can overheat the thin wire quickly and drain the battery, which violates the assignment's shorting safety rule).

* Sandpaper or a Craft Knife: To scrape the enamel off the magnet wire.

* A Cylindrical Object: Like a thick marker or a D-cell battery, to wrap the wire around.



How to Build the Motor
Step 1: Create the Coil (The Armature)



* Leave about 2 inches of straight wire at the end, then wrap the magnet wire tightly around your marker 10 to 15 times to create a neat circular coil.

* Leave another 2 inches of straight wire at the other end.

* Carefully slide the coil off the marker. Take the two straight ends and loop them tightly through the center of the coil once or twice, exactly opposite each other. These straight ends will act as your axle and keep the coil tightly bundled.


Step 2: The Commutator (Crucial Step)
This is where most people fail. The motor will not work if you skip this or do it incorrectly.



* Hold the coil vertically. On one side of the axle, use your sandpaper or knife to scrape off all the enamel insulation so you see bare copper all the way around.

* On the other side of the axle, scrape the enamel off only the top half of the wire. Leave the bottom half coated. This acts as a simple switch (a commutator) that turns the electromagnet on and off for half of every spin, giving it the momentum to keep turning in one direction rather than just getting stuck.


Step 3: Build the Platform and Supports



* Bend the outer loops of your two paperclips out slightly so they form a small "U" or "V" shape at the top. This is where your coil axle will rest.

* Stick the straight bottom ends of the paperclips into your breadboard, spacing them out just enough so your coil can comfortably rest between them.


Step 4: Place the Magnet and Power



* Place your neodymium magnet on the breadboard directly underneath where the coil will sit. Make sure there is enough clearance so the coil won't hit the magnet when it spins.

* Rest your coil's axles in the paperclip cradles.

* Plug the positive and negative wires from your battery pack into the breadboard rails connected to your two paperclips.


Step 5: Start the Motor
Give the coil a gentle flick with your finger. If everything is aligned and stripped correctly, it should start spinning continuously! If it just twitches or stops, try flicking it in the opposite direction, or check that your half-scraped wire is making good contact with the paperclip.


For Your "Part 2: Written Component"
When you write out the theory section, here is the basic concept you are demonstrating:
When current flows from the battery, up the paperclip, and through the bare copper of the coil, the coil becomes an electromagnet. The magnetic field created by the coil interacts with the permanent neodymium magnet underneath it. The repelling force pushes the coil, causing it to spin.


Because you only scraped half the insulation off one side of the axle, the electrical circuit breaks halfway through the spin. The momentum carries the coil around until the bare copper makes contact again, turning the electromagnet back on for another push.

Haha, no, you are technically 100% correct. Unless you are smelting copper ore in your backyard and harvesting a magnetic lodestone from the earth, you are using pre-manufactured materials!


Teachers and rubrics just have a funny way of phrasing things. In the context of this project, "not made out of pre-manufactured parts" just means you can't go to a hobby store, buy a little plastic toy motor, tear the casing off, and use its ready-made rotor. They want to see that you wound the coil yourself and figured out how to make a switch (the commutator) out of raw wire.


Using a manufactured breadboard, manufactured paperclips, and manufactured wire is totally fine and expected. You aren't violating the rules at all—you're just being a good engineer by looking at the literal definition of the words!

## Gemini

It is an incredibly popular project! Because it's so common, there are some great video walkthroughs that show exactly how to wind the coil and, most importantly, how to scrape that insulation just right.


Here are a few highly recommended YouTube guides that walk through the process visually:



* For a clear, step-by-step experiment guide: The video Make a Simple Electric Motor (Physics) by Bearded Science Guy is fantastic. He uses a D-battery as the base, but the way he coils the wire, mounts the paperclips, and explains the half-scraped insulation trick will apply perfectly to your breadboard setup.

* For a quick, straightforward look at the build: Check out How to Make a Simple Electric Motor | Easy Science Project. It gives you a great close-up view of how the final assembly should look and how smoothly the coil is supposed to spin once it's balanced.

* For an alternative way to make the commutator: If you don't want to risk scraping too much insulation off with a knife, Building a Simple DC Motor on Physics Lens outlines a clever alternative. Instead of scraping only half the wire, you scrape the entire wire bare, and then use a black Sharpie marker to color in one half of the axle. The ink from the permanent marker acts as an electrical insulator, creating the exact same "on/off switch" effect!


One quick tip while watching these: Most of these videos show people taping the paperclips directly to a battery. Since you are using a breadboard, you can skip the tape entirely. Just straighten out the bottom leg of your paperclips and plug them directly into your breadboard's tie-points to act as your rigid stand.
