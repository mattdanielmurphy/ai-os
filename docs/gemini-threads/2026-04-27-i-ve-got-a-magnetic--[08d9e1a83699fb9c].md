## Gemini

You're likely right—3 minutes of active mixing with a magnetic stirrer is more than enough for most standard electrolyte powders. In fact, for a simple solution of salts and flavorings, the "95% value" mark probably happens even sooner.


When you're trying to figure out if something is "mixed enough," you're usually looking at three different physical states.


1. Dissolution vs. Suspension

* Dissolution: This is what's happening to the potassium (likely potassium chloride or citrate). The water molecules pull the ions apart until they are chemically bonded with the water. Once a salt is dissolved, it stays dissolved. It won't "settle" later unless the water evaporates.

* Suspension: The "cloudiness" you see initially is often caused by flavoring oils, anti-caking agents (like silicon dioxide), or certain vitamins that don't dissolve easily. These are tiny solids floating in the water.


2. The Absorption Myth
The good news is that your body is remarkably good at finishing the job. Even if the water still looks a bit cloudy or has "floaties" when you drink it, the hydrochloric acid in your stomach is a much more powerful solvent than your magnetic stirrer.


As long as the powder isn't sitting in a dry clump at the bottom of the glass, the bioavailability (how much your body absorbs) remains essentially the same. The difference between "3 minutes of mixing" and "30 minutes of mixing" is almost entirely aesthetic, not nutritional.


3. How to Tell It’s "Done"
You can use a few visual cues to stop early:



* The Vortex Test: While the stirrer is on, look at the vortex. If you no longer see white grains "dancing" in the whirlpool or sitting at the bottom edges, the solids are either dissolved or fully suspended.

* The Clarity Plateau: Most electrolyte mixes contain "clouding agents" to make them look like juice rather than plain water. If the liquid reaches a consistent level of opacity and stops changing for 30 seconds, it’s as mixed as it’s ever going to get.

* The "Shadow" Check: Hold the glass up to a light. If you don't see "shimmering" lines (convection streaks caused by different densities of salt and water mixing), the solution is likely homogenous.




Note on leaving it too long: If you leave a magnetic stirrer going for a very long time, the friction of the stir bar can actually slightly increase the water temperature. While minor, this can sometimes help dissolve stubborn minerals, which is why it looks "clearer" after you've forgotten about it—but again, this won't change how well your body processes the potassium.



Unless you're using a massive amount of powder in very little water (reaching "saturation"), a quick 60-second spin is usually the sweet spot where the effort-to-reward curve flattens out.

## Gemini

What you’re seeing is actually quite common with supplement powders. Even if the primary ingredient—the potassium—has fully dissolved into the water, those tiny visible particles are likely the "inactive" ingredients or complex flavor compounds.


Here is why those particles persist even with high-speed magnetic stirring:


1. Insoluble "Excipients"
Many electrolyte packets use flow agents like silica (silicon dioxide) or calcium silicate. These are added to the powder to prevent it from clumping in the packet.



* The Physics: These minerals are often technically insoluble in water. No matter how long you stir, they will never "disappear" because they aren't dissolving; they are simply being suspended.

* The Visual: Under a bright light, these act like tiny mirrors, reflecting the light back at you even when the rest of the liquid is clear.


2. The Tyndall Effect
If the particles are extremely small, you might be witnessing the Tyndall Effect. This happens when light scatters off particles in a colloid (a mixture where microscopic particles are dispersed through another substance).



* Even if a particle is too small to see individually with the naked eye, a bright, direct light source will catch them and make the entire liquid look "shimmery" or "dusty."



3. Flavor Oils and Natural Colors
If your electrolytes use natural flavors, they often contain essential oils or plant-derived pigments.



* Oils are hydrophobic (water-fearing). A magnetic stirrer is great at creating an emulsion—breaking those oils into such tiny droplets that they stay spread out—but it won't make them bond with the water.

* These droplets will catch the light and look like a fine mist of particles.



Should you care?
From a biological standpoint: No.


As soon as those particles hit your stomach, the environment changes from a neutral pH (water) to a highly acidic one (gastric acid).



* Most "stubborn" particles that refuse to dissolve in your glass will be broken down instantly in the stomach.

* Even the ones that don't dissolve (like silica) are biologically inert and simply pass through your system without affecting the absorption of the potassium.


The Verdict: If you’ve given it 3 minutes on the stirrer, the potassium ions are definitely in the solution. You’re essentially just looking at the "scaffolding" that kept the powder dry in the packet. You're good to drink it.

$$\text{Dissolution Rate} \propto \text{Surface Area} \times (C_s - C)$$


In this simplified version of the Noyes-Whitney equation, the rate of dissolution depends on the difference between the saturation concentration ($C_s$) and the current concentration ($C$).
