## Gemini

You actually deduced the exact reason for that weird behavior. The magnets aren’t oriented the way we naturally expect them to be (with one flat face being North and the opposite face being South).


Instead, they are likely magnetized longitudinally or use a multi-pole design inside the bracket. Here is exactly what is happening to create that strange, twisting force.


Why They Twist and Sit "Off to the Side"
If the magnets were simple bar magnets with North on the front and South on the back, they would behave exactly as you expected: clean attraction one way, and direct push-back when flipped.


Because they are fighting you perpendicularly and slipping sideways, it means the North and South poles are actually running parallel to the face you are looking at, or split right down the middle of that single exposed surface.


When you try to push two of these magnets together face-to-face:



* The Conflict: You are accidentally bringing a North pole close to a North pole, and a South pole close to a South pole simultaneously on the same face.

* The Lateral Shift: The magnets violently try to correct this. They push sideways so that the North half of Magnet A can line up with the South half of Magnet B. This causes that sudden "snap" to the side, leaving them sitting offset rather than flush.


Why Door Magnets Are Built This Way
It seems counterintuitive for a basic latch, but making the poles run sideways (or alternating them in a U-shape inside the bracket) is a deliberate engineering choice for two reasons:



* Completing the Circuit: A door magnet interacts with a flat steel strike plate on the door. Steel doesn't care about North or South; it just wants a magnetic field. By putting both North and South poles right on the front face, the magnetic field travels out of the North pole, loops directly through the steel plate, and immediately enters the South pole. This creates a closed, highly concentrated magnetic circuit.

* Maximum Holding Power: This layout drastically increases the grip strength when the magnet is touching the steel plate, while ensuring the magnetic pull drops off quickly once the door is pulled open a few millimeters.


Your intuition was spot on—you were trying to push poles together that were arranged like a pair of double-ended arrows lying sideways, forcing them to shift over to find their balance.

## Gemini

You just hit the nail on the head. Your description of the "two metal plates on either side" completely explains the layout.


What you are looking at is called a sandwich magnet or a channel magnet assembly.


How It Delivers "Both Sides" at Once
Your intuition is exactly right: a normal, standalone magnet has a magnetic field that loops all the way through the air from one end to the other. That massive loop through the air is incredibly inefficient because air is terrible at conducting magnetic fields.


By placing those two steel plates on either side of the magnet, the manufacturer is creating a shortcut.


   [ Steel Plate ]  <-- Becomes North Pole
   [=== Magnet ==]  <-- Magnetized side-to-side
   [ Steel Plate ]  <-- Becomes South Pole

The steel plates absorb the magnetic field from the hidden sides of the magnet and channel them straight to the front edge. When you close the door against the strike plate, you are placing a flat piece of steel across both of those plates. The magnetic field passes out of one plate, straight through the door's steel strike plate, and back into the other plate.


Because the field only has to travel a fraction of a millimeter through steel instead of looping through empty air, the holding force skyrockets. It doesn't waste any of the magnetic energy.


Can You Double the Strength by Putting a Second Magnet on the Door?
In theory, yes—putting two magnets together can increase holding force. In practice with these specific cabinet or door latches, it is incredibly difficult and usually counterproductive for a couple of reasons:



* The Alignment Nightmare: Because those steel plates are channeling the North and South poles right next to each other on a tiny surface, you would have to align the door magnet perfectly down to the millimeter. If it’s off by just a tiny bit, the North plate of the frame magnet will hit the North plate of the door magnet, and the door will violently push itself open or twist sideways, just like you experienced.

* Diminishing Returns: The steel strike plate on a normal door is already doing an almost perfect job of completing the magnetic circuit. Adding a second magnet doesn't double the strength because the limiting factor isn't the lack of a second magnet; it's the saturation point of the steel and the surface area of contact.


If you actually wanted a drastically stronger latching force, the easiest path isn't adding a second magnet to fight with—it's simply using a single, larger channel magnet with more surface area, or ensuring the steel strike plate on the door is thick enough that it doesn't "leak" any of the magnetic field out the back.

## Gemini

You have a great logical radar, and you are pulling the right pieces together, but the physics here is a bit subtle. Let's break down exactly where you are spot-on and where the math of magnetic forces does something unexpected.


The "Two Normal Magnets" Comparison: You're Right
Your intuition about the channel magnet is absolutely correct.


A standard bar magnet touching a piece of steel only uses one pole efficiently. The other pole's field loops out into space and is mostly wasted. By adding those steel plates to create a channel magnet, you redirect that wasted pole right to the front face.


In terms of utilizing the magnet's total available magnetic energy, one channel magnet effectively does the work of two exposed poles. It stops the waste.



Why Two Magnets Don't Equal Twice the Force
This is the part that feels completely counterintuitive. If you take two identical channel magnets, flip one so the poles line up perfectly (North to South, South to North), and stick them face-to-face, the holding force does not double. It stays almost exactly the same as a single magnet stuck to a thick piece of steel. Here is why:


Magnetic holding force depends on two primary things:



* The flux density (how many magnetic field lines are crammed into the contact area).

* The surface area of the contact.


When a single channel magnet sticks to a thick piece of steel, the steel becomes temporarily magnetized and acts like a mirror. It easily absorbs 100% of the magnetic field lines coming out of the magnet. The magnetic circuit is perfectly closed and saturated.


If you replace that piece of steel with a second identical magnet facing the right way, you haven't actually increased the number of field lines passing through that joint, nor have you increased the surface area. The second magnet is just pushing its own field lines into the same space where the first magnet's lines already were. Because the magnetic circuit was already "full" and perfectly closed by the steel, adding a second active engine to the same loop gives you almost zero extra pull.


When Does Adding a Second Magnet Double the Force?
To actually double the holding force, you have to change the setup in one of two ways:



* Double the Area (Parallel): If you place a second channel magnet next to the first one on the door frame, and double the size of the steel plate on the door, you have doubled the contact surface area. That will double your holding force.

* Magnetic Stack (Distance): If you stack two raw magnets on top of each other (making a single, thicker magnet) to pull on a piece of steel from a distance, the force increases because a thicker magnet can project its field farther through the air. But once they are already touching the steel flush, stacking them stops adding much power.


So, your conclusion is completely practical: using a single channel magnet gives you the maximum efficiency out of that magnet's material without any of the alignment headaches of trying to match two opposite multi-pole faces together.

## Gemini

You are exactly right on the metal: magnets do not stick to aluminum. If your door is cheap, thin, flexible, and magnetic, it is almost certainly made of thin sheets of galvanized steel.


And you hit on a massive variable here. The fact that the steel door is "cheap and thin" actually changes the calculus completely.



Part 1: How a Thin Door Changes the Math
Earlier, I mentioned that a piece of steel acts like a mirror, absorbing 100% of the magnetic field lines. But that only works if the steel is thick enough.


Think of magnetic field lines like water pouring through a pipe. A thick piece of steel is a massive pipe that can handle all the water. A thin piece of sheet metal is a tiny pipe. The magnetic field saturates the thin steel instantly, and the rest of the field lines "leak" right out the back of the door into the air.


Because the thin door is bottlenecking the magnet, you aren't getting the full holding force the channel magnet is capable of.


Does a second magnet help here?
Yes! In this specific scenario, if you put a second magnet on the thin door, it would increase the holding force. Not because of a magical doubling effect, but because the second magnet helps corral that leaking field and forces more of it into the loop.


However, the absolute easiest way to fix this without alignment headaches is just to glue a small, thick piece of scrap steel (like a heavy washer or a proper strike plate) to the inside of the door right where it meets the magnet. Giving the field a "thicker pipe" will instantly maximize the latch strength.



Part 2: Two Regular Magnets vs. One Channel Magnet
To answer your second question: if you took two regular, raw bar magnets (no steel channels) and stuck them face-to-face, no, it still wouldn’t double the holding force compared to one bar magnet stuck to a thick steel plate.


It feels like it should, because of the "wasted pole" logic, but here is how the physics plays out:



* One Bar Magnet + Thick Steel: The steel turns into an opposite pole instantly. The North pole of your magnet touches the steel, making that spot a South pole. The circuit loops through the steel and back to the magnet's own South pole in the air.

* Two Bar Magnets Face-to-Face: You bring a real South pole to meet the North pole.


In both setups, the connection point is identical: a North pole tightly meeting a South pole. The total "flux density" (the concentration of the magnetic field) right at the boundary line doesn't change.


The only difference is what happens to the "wasted" poles at the far ends. With two magnets stuck together, you've just made a single, longer magnet. A longer magnet is better at reaching out and pulling things from a distance, but its actual "holding force" once it is already touching flush isn't twice as strong.


The Ultimate Summary

* Two channel magnets face-to-face: A nightmare to align, and won't add power if the steel is thick.

* Two regular magnets face-to-face: Acts exactly like one longer magnet; doesn't double the flush grip.

* The bottleneck: Your thin door is the real culprit stealing your holding power. Bulk up the steel on the door side, and your single frame magnet will suddenly pull with its full, maximum strength.

## Gemini

You are absolutely right to feel skeptical, and your understanding of moving charges and electron alignment across magnetic domains is spot-on.


Those videos are usually leaning heavily into clickbait, but they are also playing on a philosophical and linguistic trick. When popular science videos claim "nobody understands magnetism," they are conflating how it works (which we understand deeply) with why it exists at all (which eventually hits a fundamental wall).


Here is the breakdown of why people say this, and why your unit on physics proves we actually do know what we are talking about.



1. The Linguistic Trick: Description vs. Explanation
We understand the rules of magnetism perfectly. Maxwell’s equations and quantum mechanics allow us to predict, calculate, and engineer magnetic fields down to the subatomic level with perfect precision.


But if you keep asking a scientist "Why?" like a persistent toddler, you eventually run out of answers.




* "Why do these two magnets pull together?"

* Scientist: Because of the magnetic field generated by the aligned spins of their electrons.

* "Why do aligned electron spins create a field?"

* Scientist: Because moving charges and intrinsic quantum spin generate a magnetic dipole.

* "Why does quantum spin create a magnetic dipole?"

* Scientist: Because that is a fundamental property of the electron. It just does.



At that final step, science stops explaining the cause and simply states the axiom. Magnetism is an intrinsic, fundamental property of the universe. When clickbait videos say "we don't understand it," what they really mean is "we cannot break it down into an even simpler, mechanical concept that makes sense to our human brains."



2. The Famous Richard Feynman Interview
Most of these videos are modern riffs on a famous 1983 BBC interview with physicist Richard Feynman.


The interviewer asked him to explain the feeling of force between two magnets. Feynman spent several minutes explaining that you can't describe magnetism using everyday concepts like "rubber bands pulling them together" or "springs pushing them apart," because the tension in rubber bands and springs is actually caused by electromagnetic forces at the atomic level anyway.


Feynman's point was that magnetism is so foundational that you cannot explain it using a analogy of a familiar object. You have to accept it as a fundamental rule of the universe. Video creators twist this deep philosophical point into a sensationalist headline: "Even Feynman says nobody gets it!"



3. The Special Relativity Mind-Bender
There is one genuinely mind-bending aspect of magnetism that makes it feel elusive, even though we understand the math perfectly: Magnetism is actually just electricity viewed through the lens of Einstein's Special Relativity.


If you have a static electric charge, it has an electric field. If that charge starts moving past you, length contraction occurs due to relativity, the charge density shifts, and that electric field transforms into a magnetic field from your perspective.


Because magnetism and electricity are two sides of the exact same coin (electromagnetism), a magnetic field is essentially a relativistic correction factor for a moving electric field. It's incredibly strange to wrap your head around intuitively, which gives science communicators endless fuel for dramatic scripts.


The Verdict
It is absolutely clickbait.


Saying "nobody understands magnetism" is like saying "nobody understands what a pixel is" because we can't explain why light exists. We understand the mechanics, the quantum states, the domains, and the mathematics well enough to build MRI machines, particle accelerators, and the hard drives that store those very videos. You shouldn't doubt your physics unit—you understand the "how" perfectly, and that's exactly what science is designed to do.
