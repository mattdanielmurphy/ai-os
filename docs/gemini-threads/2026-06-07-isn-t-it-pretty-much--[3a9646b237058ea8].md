## Gemini

That narrative is incredibly common—in fact, it’s practically a tradition of its own. People have been declaring that Saturday Night Live "isn't as funny as it used to be" since roughly 1976.


While it feels like a universal truth, the actual reality is a bit more nuanced. The perception of a permanent "downhill slide" usually comes down to a few specific factors:


The "Golden Age" Bias
There is a well-documented phenomenon with SNL where a viewer's personal "Golden Age" almost always aligns with when they were between the ages of 15 and 25. Whether that means the era of Dana Carvey and Phil Hartman, Will Ferrell and Cheri Oteri, or Tina Fey and Kristen Wiig, people tend to romanticize the cast they grew up with.


The Clip-Show Effect
When we look back at older seasons, we remember the iconic, culture-shifting sketches—the Coneheads, Celebrity Jeopardy, or the Lonely Island Digital Shorts. We completely forget that even in the most revered seasons, a standard 90-minute live show always included plenty of duds, bizarre late-night experiments, and sketches that fell completely flat. Modern viewers compare today's entire live episodes against the all-time greatest hits of the past.


Cultural Relevance vs. Consistent Quality
Even critics who argue the week-to-week sketch quality has dipped will concede that the show's cultural footprint remains massive, particularly during election years. As noted in industry reviews, impressions like Tina Fey’s Sarah Palin or the show's parodies of Donald Trump created massive spikes in ratings and drove the national political conversation.


Critical and Industry Reception
While internet commentary often skews negative, the entertainment industry hasn't entirely abandoned ship. In fact, Variety ranked Saturday Night Live at #15 on its list of the 100 Greatest TV Shows of All Time, and a Hollywood Reporter industry survey placed it as the #7 favorite show among Hollywood professionals.


Ultimately, SNL functions like a generational hand-off. The cohort currently complaining that the show has lost its touch will likely be replaced in ten years by a younger generation fiercely defending the current era as a classic.

No, not at all. If my last response implied that the actual reviews and ratings have been glowing and flawless, that’s a misdirection on my part.


To be direct: the week-to-week critical reviews are frequently harsh, and traditional TV viewership is a fraction of what it was decades ago.


When looking at the data from the past decade, the reality is a fascinating contradiction: SNL is simultaneously experiencing massive audience engagement and persistent critical panning.



1. The Ratings Reality (Linear vs. Digital)
If you look strictly at traditional broadcast TV ratings, SNL has shrunk significantly over the last decade, just like every other network show. However, executives aren't panicking because the show’s total reach has actually shifted and grown digitally:



* The Broadcast King: Even with lower traditional numbers, SNL frequently finishes its seasons as TV’s top-rated entertainment series in the coveted 18–49 demographic. It isn't necessarily drawing more viewers than of old; it's just dying much slower than prime-time sitcoms and dramas.

* The Streaming/Social Boom: The show has essentially transitioned into a digital-first property for a huge chunk of its audience. Industry data from TheWrap highlighted that SNL racked up over 7.8 billion social media views in 2025 alone. People aren't staying up until 1:00 AM on Saturdays; they are watching the three best sketches on YouTube or TikTok on Sunday morning.


2. The Review Rollercoaster
Critical reviews are absolutely not consistently positive. They generally fall into a predictable pattern:



* The "Trudge" Era: Critics often describe standard mid-season episodes as exhausting, repetitive, or toothless. Reviews from outlets like The Guardian regularly criticize the show's reliance on predictable political parodies and star-studded cameos over sharp writing.

* The Outlier Saved Grace: The reviews stay alive because of intermittent, highly praised episodes that remind everyone of what the show can do. When a highly capable host (like Ryan Gosling, John Mulaney, or Ariana Grande) gels perfectly with the cast, critics pivot to praising the show's enduring brilliance.




The Bottom Line: > If you read a review of a random episode in March, it's very likely to say the show is struggling. But if you look at the show's cultural footprint, Emmy nominations, and multi-platform viewership, it remains an incredibly dominant force. It hasn't stayed consistently excellent—it has stayed consistently relevant.

## Gemini

To stop nvm from defaulting to the Intel/Rosetta version (system) whenever you open a new terminal session, you need to set your default alias to an ARM-native version of Node.


Here is how to fix it in two quick steps.


1. Alias the Latest ARM Node as Default
Run the following command in your terminal. Using "node" tells nvm to always point the default alias to the latest installed version:


nvm alias default node

If you prefer to lock it to a specific major version that you know is ARM-native (for example, v22), you can use:


nvm alias default 22


2. Verify the Architecture
Open a new terminal window or tab to ensure the environment refreshes, then run these two commands to verify everything is running natively on ARM:


node -p "process.arch"


Expected Output: arm64



nvm current


Expected Output: v22.x.x (or whichever version you designated, instead of system)



Why this happens
On Apple Silicon, system typically points to the pre-installed macOS stub or an architecture resolved during your initial shell configuration. If your shell accidentally starts under Rosetta once, nvm can capture that path. Explicitly pointing the default alias to a specific version number or the "node" keyword forces nvm to look inside its own ARM-compiled binary folder instead of falling back to the system environment.

## Gemini

If nvm alias default node didn't stick, your shell configuration file is almost certainly resetting nvm or overwriting your PATH after the alias is loaded.


This usually happens because of the order of commands in your .zshrc (or .bash_profile). Here is how to track down and fix it.


1. Check for Duplicate or Hardcoded Initializations
Open your configuration file (assuming Zsh, which is the default on Mac):


nano ~/.zshrc

Scroll through the file and look for two things:



* Multiple NVM blocks: Sometimes there are two separate blocks loading nvm. The second one can stomp on the first.

* A hardcoded nvm use command: Look for a line that explicitly says nvm use system or nvm use default. If nvm use system is written at the bottom of your file, it will run every time you open a terminal, completely ignoring your default alias.


If you find a line like nvm use system, delete it or comment it out by adding a # in front of it.



2. Check Your PATH Export Order
If your .zshrc modifies the $PATH variable after the NVM script runs, it can accidentally put the system's default Intel binary folder ahead of NVM's ARM folder.


Make sure your NVM configuration block is at the very bottom of your .zshrc file so it gets the final word. It should look like this:


export NVM_DIR="$HOME/.nvm"
[ -s "$NVM_DIR/nvm.sh" ] && \. "$NVM_DIR/nvm.sh"  # This loads nvm
[ -s "$NVM_DIR/bash_completion" ] && \. "$NVM_DIR/bash_completion"  # This loads nvm bash_completion

If you made changes in nano, press Ctrl + O then Enter to save, and Ctrl + X to exit.



3. The Nuclear Debugging Step (Force it on load)
If you can't find what is overriding it and just want it fixed, you can manually force your shell to run your default alias at startup.


Open ~/.zshrc again, go to the very bottom of the file (below the NVM block), and add this single line:


nvm use default --silent

The --silent flag ensures it switches to your ARM-native default node quietly every time a new window opens, without printing a message to your screen. Save the file, open a brand new terminal tab, and run nvm current to verify.

## Gemini

It works because of how shells like Zsh build your environment from top to bottom when you open a new terminal window.


Whenever you run a command, your terminal looks through a list of folder paths stored in a variable called $PATH. It searches those folders from left to right and runs the very first match it finds.


Here is exactly what was happening behind the scenes:


The Problem Order
When the NVM block was at the top of your ~/.zshrc, this sequence occurred:



* NVM loaded first: It checked your default alias (node), located the ARM binary folder, and prepended it to your $PATH.

* Other tools loaded next: As the shell kept reading down your file, other configurations (like Homebrew, custom exports, or system paths) added their directories to the front of the $PATH.

* The conflict: If any of those subsequent tools or path modifications re-introduced a path containing an Intel/Rosetta binary directory or a system wrapper ahead of NVM's folder, it pushed NVM's ARM directory down the priority list.

* The result: When the shell finished loading, running node hit the system/Rosetta binary first, forcing NVM to look at your environment and report that you were using system.


The Fix
By moving the NVM block to the absolute bottom of your file, you guaranteed that NVM got the absolute last word.


It takes whatever $PATH your other tools built, puts its own ARM-native Node path at the very front of the line, and ensures that the terminal always hits the ARM binary before it can wander off and find the system default.

why is the system version rosetta in the first place? Why would the system want a version that's running on an emulation layer?

---

## Gemini

The system version of Node isn't running on Rosetta because macOS wants an emulated version; rather, it's usually the result of a chain reaction caused by how the tool that installed it was running at the time.


If nvm points to a system version that triggers Rosetta, it almost always comes down to one of two reasons:


1. The Global Python/Xcode Toolchain Dependency
macOS doesn't actually ship with a pre-installed, standalone copy of Node.js. However, it does ship with command-line tools (via Xcode or the standalone Command Line Tools package) that include stubs for various development environment behaviors.


If an older, non-native development tool, an explicit Python script wrapper, or a legacy global package manager invoked a basic build script during system setup, macOS may have cached or resolved a fallback executable using the Rosetta translation layer to ensure backward compatibility. To nvm, any executable found in the standard system paths (/usr/local/bin or /usr/bin) that isn't managed by nvm itself gets labeled as system.


2. The "Inherited Architecture" Chain
This is the most common culprit on Apple Silicon. When you install development tools, processes inherit the architecture of the parent process that launched them.



* If your terminal app (like Terminal.app or iTerm2) or an IDE terminal was ever opened with the "Open using Rosetta" option checked in its Finder Info panel, the shell inside it starts as an Intel (x86_64) process.

* If you installed a package manager like Homebrew or ran a global script while that shell was inadvertently running under Rosetta, it fetched and configured the Intel version of those tools.

* Consequently, when nvm looks at the Node executable located in your system's global paths, it finds a binary built for Intel chips.


The system doesn't actively prefer the emulation layer; it simply runs the binary exactly how it was compiled. Because Apple Silicon chips handle Rosetta translation seamlessly in the background, the Intel-compiled "system" binary runs without crashing, masking the fact that it is running on an emulation layer until you notice the performance drop or check process.arch.
