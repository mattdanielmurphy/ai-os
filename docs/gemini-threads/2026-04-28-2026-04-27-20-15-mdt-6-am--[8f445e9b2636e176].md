## Gemini

In physics problems like this, when they state "the rocket accelerates at $25\text{ m/s}^2$," they are giving you the net acceleration ($a_{net}$) of the object along that specific path.


You should assume the $25\text{ m/s}^2$ is the final acceleration value you use for your kinematic equations, with gravity already "baked in."



Why we treat it as the Net Acceleration
If the problem intended for you to calculate the acceleration yourself based on thrust, it would have provided the mass of the rocket and the Newtons of force produced by the engine. Since it simply gives you a single value, it’s telling you the observed result of all forces combined.


Think of it this way:



* Thrust is pushing it up the ramp.

* Gravity's parallel component ($g \sin 32^\circ$) is pulling it down the ramp.

* The result of that tug-of-war is exactly $25\text{ m/s}^2$ up the incline.


Strategy for Solving
Since you're looking for the range ($R$), you'll likely need to break this into two distinct phases:


Phase 1: The Incline (Linear Acceleration)



* Use the distance of the incline (which you can find using the $70\text{ m}$ height and the $32^\circ$ angle: $d = \frac{70}{\sin 32^\circ}$) and the $25\text{ m/s}^2$ acceleration to find the final velocity ($v_f$) at the moment it leaves the ramp.

* This $v_f$ becomes your initial velocity ($v_0$) for the projectile phase.


Phase 2: The Projectile Motion (Free Fall)



* Once it leaves the ramp, the rocket "stops firing." Now, the only acceleration is gravity ($9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ straight down).

* Your launch angle is $32^\circ$.

* Your starting height is $70\text{ m}$.


If you treated the $25\text{ m/s}^2$ as "vacuum acceleration" and then subtracted gravity again, you'd essentially be double-counting the effect of the incline.

## Gemini

You're right—having a non-zero starting height ($d_y = 70$ m) makes the standard "range equation" ($R = \frac{v^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}$) unusable because that formula assumes the launch and landing heights are the same.


When you have an initial height, isolating $t$ usually requires the quadratic formula, which can be a bit of a slog. Here is the cleanest way to step through it without getting lost in the algebra.


Step 1: The Launch Velocity
First, find your launch velocity ($v_L$) at the top of the ramp. Since you know the height is $70$ m and the angle is $32^\circ$, the distance along the ramp is $s = \frac{70}{\sin 32^\circ}$.
Using $v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2as$:
$$v_L = \sqrt{0 + 2(25)\left(\frac{70}{\sin 32^\circ}\right)}$$


Step 2: Component Breakdown
Break that $v_L$ into $x$ and $y$ components:



* $v_{ix} = v_L \cos 32^\circ$

* $v_{iy} = v_L \sin 32^\circ$


Step 3: Solving for Time ($t$)
This is the part you're dreading. Using the vertical displacement equation $\Delta y = v_{iy}t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2$:
$$-70 = (v_L \sin 32^\circ)t - 4.9t^2$$
(Note: $\Delta y$ is negative because it ends up 70m lower than it started).


To isolate $t$, rearrange it into standard quadratic form:
$$4.9t^2 - (v_L \sin 32^\circ)t - 70 = 0$$


Is there a shortcut?
If you want to avoid the quadratic formula, you can solve for the final vertical velocity ($v_{fy}$) first, which is often faster:



* Use $v_{fy}^2 = v_{iy}^2 + 2a_y\Delta y$.

* Once you have $v_{fy}$, use $v_{fy} = v_{iy} + a_y t$ to get $t$.


This "two-step" method usually feels less like an algebraic trap than the quadratic formula. Once you have $t$, the range is just the simple horizontal walk: $R = v_{ix}t$.


Does that "two-step" approach to finding $t$ feel a bit more manageable for your workflow?

## Gemini

Exactly. That is the beauty of using displacement ($\Delta y$) rather than total distance.


Because gravity is a constant acceleration, the kinematics equations don't care about the "path" the rocket takes (the rise and the fall); they only care about the state at $t = 0$ and the state at the final time $t$.


Why it works
The equation $v_{fy}^2 = v_{iy}^2 + 2a_y\Delta y$ is derived from energy principles (work-energy theorem). The vertical kinetic energy at the end depends only on:



* How much vertical kinetic energy you started with ($v_{iy}$).

* How much potential energy you "traded" for kinetic energy by dropping 70 meters ($\Delta y = -70$).


A small tip for the math
When you solve $v_{fy} = \sqrt{v_{iy}^2 + 2a_y\Delta y}$, remember that the square root technically gives you a $\pm$ result. Since the rocket is moving downward when it hits the ground, you'll want to use the negative value for $v_{fy}$ when you plug it into the next step:
$$t = \frac{v_{fy} - v_{iy}}{a_y}$$


If you use the positive version of $v_{fy}$ by mistake, you'll actually calculate the time it would have taken the rocket to reach that same speed while it was still ascending (which, in this case, would give you a nonsensical or negative time).
