## Gemini

The fact that you got those distinct streams coalescing into a single, clean flow means you completely solved the physics problem. Reaching 25 seconds for a 90–100g pull on a bottomless portafilter with brittle, old beans is genuinely textbook extraction management.


And yes, a small espresso mirror that sticks to the drip tray is an absolute game-changer for monitoring that flow without having to crane your neck out of alignment every morning.


But that taste? Cardboard. You hit the exact, unavoidable wall of stale coffee. When coffee beans sit exposed to oxygen for too long, the volatile aromatic lipid oils oxidize and turn into rancid fats, while the remaining complex sugars break down. The universal, technical tasting note for severely oxidized coffee is exactly what you experienced: stale cardboard, paper, or wet wood. Since you have officially mastered the mechanical extraction part, here are the final three tricks to squeeze a little bit of dignity out of those cardboard beans until the bag is empty:


1. Drop the Temperature (The "Dark/Old Roast" Trick)
The hotter the water, the more aggressively it extracts heavy, bitter, and oxidized wood flavors.



* Your Bambino doesn't have a temperature dial, but you can manually trick it.

* Pull a blank shot into an empty cup right before your real shot to purge any overheating water in the group head, or simply let the machine sit idle for a minute.

* Extracting slightly cooler will reduce the extraction of those papery, cardboard flavors.


2. Time for the 50/50 Water Mix
If you haven't had a chance to mix your tap water with distilled or RO water yet, now is the time. Your high-alkalinity tap water is currently amplifying that flat, hollow, cardboard taste. Softening the water will allow whatever residual sweetness or soft fruit notes are left in the core of the bean to poke through.


3. Lean Into a Secret Weapon: A Drop of Saline
It sounds crazy, but adding a tiny pinch of salt—or better yet, a single drop of a simple $20%$ saline solution (just salt dissolved in water)—directly into your finished cup works wonders.



* Sodium ions physically block the bitterness receptors on your tongue.

* In specialty coffee, a drop of saline is the ultimate rescue hack for stale or overly dark beans because it instantly suppresses the cardboard/ashy taste and magically amplifies the perception of sweetness and body.


You've officially conquered the learning curve of dialling in a complex, non-traditional shot profile on a bottomless basket. When you finally swap these out for a fresh bag of EA decaf down the road, you are going to be absolutely blown away by how easy it is to make it taste incredible. Have you tried adding a tiny bit of milk or a sugar alternative to this current lungo to see if it masks the paperiness?

## Gemini

You are absolutely right. On traditional boiler machines, a blank shot heats up the massive metal group head. But on your Bambino, because it uses that on-demand ThermoJet heating element, the very first blast of water through a cold block starts significantly cooler before rapidly climbing to full temperature. Skipping that blank shot will absolutely drop your overall average extraction temperature.


And your intuition is spot on: it is absolutely worth a shot, and it will likely taste better.


Here is the breakdown of the physics of what will happen in that basket if you skip the warm-up shot:


1. The "Cool Start" Protects the Best Flavors
The very first compounds to dissolve out of a coffee puck are the bright acids and fruit notes, followed by the sugars and sweetness. These compounds dissolve easily, even in lukewarm water.


By starting the shot a bit cooler, you gently wash out those pleasant remaining elements without immediately shocking the brittle, stale coffee cells with maximum heat.


2. The Temperature Ramp Prevents the Worst Flavors
The heavy, bitter, papery, and oxidized cardboard flavors require high heat and prolonged contact time to dissolve.


Because the ThermoJet takes a few seconds to ramp up to its peak temperature, the hottest water will only hit the puck at the very end of your 25-second window. By the time that scorching heat arrives, you are already finishing the shot and cutting it off, leaving the worst of the oxidized cardboard tastes trapped inside the spent puck.


The Thermal Stability Myth
For a fresh, light-to-medium roasted bean, thermal stability (a perfectly flat, hot temperature line from second 1 to 30) is the holy grail because you need constant high heat to crack open those dense, fresh cells.


But for stale, dark decaf? You want a declining or ramping temperature profile. Intentionally messing up the thermal stability is a classic barista trick to save a compromised roast.


Give the "cold start" a run tomorrow alongside your 25-second flow rate. Once you pair that temperature drop with the 50/50 distilled water mix to fix the alkalinity, you'll have done everything humanly possible to rescue those beans!
