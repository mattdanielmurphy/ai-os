## Gemini

It’s incredibly annoying, but the "logic" from their perspective basically boils down to Digital Rights Management (DRM) paranoia.


Here is why they pull this:


1. The "Hardware Root of Trust"
To stream in 4K, most studios (and the lawyers who represent them) require Widevine L1 or similar high-level hardware-backed DRM.



* The Problem: Most web browsers (especially Chrome and Firefox) use software-based DRM implementations.

* The Risk: These are easier to "hook" into or spoof, allowing people to rip high-quality 4K streams.

* The Result: HBO (Max) simply refuses to serve the 4K assets to any environment they can't fully lock down at the hardware level.


2. Browser Limitations vs. Dedicated Apps
Browsers like Chrome and Firefox are generally capped at 1080p (or even 720p) across almost all streaming services for this exact reason.



* Edge/Safari Exception: On Windows, Microsoft Edge can sometimes hit 4K because it integrates directly with Windows' PlayReady hardware DRM. On Mac, Safari can do it because it hooks into Apple's FairPlay DRM.

* The Catch: Even though the browsers can handle it, Max often doesn't bother enabling it for the web player because it's easier to just push people toward native apps (Apple TV, Roku, Shield, etc.) where they have total control over the environment.


3. Bandwidth Costs & Licensing
There is also a cynical financial layer:



* Tier Gating: They want you on the "Ultimate Ad-Free" tier to even see 4K, and forcing you onto a TV-connected device makes it much harder to share accounts or bypass their specific ecosystem limits.

* Data Savings: 4K streams are significantly more expensive to serve. By capping the web—where people are often multi-tasking or watching on smaller laptop screens—they save a massive amount on CDN (Content Delivery Network) costs.



Is there a workaround?

Not really a good one. Even if you use a 4K monitor on a PC, Max will usually stick to 1080p in the browser. Your best bet for 4K is a dedicated streaming box or, in some very specific cases, the native "Max" app from the Windows Store (though even that is notoriously buggy compared to a dedicated device like an Apple TV or Shield).
