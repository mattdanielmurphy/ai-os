## Gemini

You hit on a really common linguistic trap. Etymologically, it absolutely sounds like it should mean "inside an atom," but in physics, the prefix "sub-" actually means "smaller than" or "below the level of."


So the definition of a subatomic particle is simply any particle that is smaller than an entire atom.


Think of it like a hierarchy:



* Macroscopic: A grain of sand.

* Atomic: A single helium atom.

* Subatomic: Anything smaller than that helium atom.


Because a muon is a fundamental particle with a mass much smaller than any atom, it earns the "subatomic" label—even though it spends its entire brief life completely free, tearing through space completely solo.


Protons, neutrons, and electrons are subatomic particles that usually live inside atoms, but if you strip an electron away from an atom, it doesn't lose its status; it's still a free subatomic particle. The muon is just born free!

Here is how they actually intersect, and why the answer key is misleading:

When subatomic particles (like electrons or cosmic ray muons) move at speeds approaching the speed of light, physicists cannot use standard quantum mechanics. They have to use Relativistic Quantum Mechanics.

In 1928, physicist Paul Dirac wrote down the Dirac Equation. It was a brilliant mathematical marriage of Special Relativity ($E^2 = (pc)^2 + (m_0c^2)^2$) and Quantum Mechanics.

This led directly to the discovery of antimatter (like the positron, the antimatter twin of the electron). If SR weren't relevant to QM, we wouldn't have predicted antimatter.


3. Quantum Field Theory (QFT)
Today, the absolute cutting-edge framework for particle physics—the math used at the Large Hadron Collider to find things like the Higgs Boson—is Quantum Field Theory. QFT is, by definition, the fully realized union of Quantum Mechanics and Special Relativity applied to subatomic fields.

3. Charge and Interactions

* Electrons: Carry a negative electric charge ($-1e$). Because they are charged, they interact strongly with electromagnetic fields and repel each other.

Because both photons and moving electrons have momentum, they both propagate as waves through the slits and create interference. But the moment they hit the detector, the electron acts like a localized lump of matter, while the photon acts like a localized packet of pure energy.
