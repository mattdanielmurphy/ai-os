You are spot on—diagram D is technically incorrect because it misses the key physics of the situation.


If the object is moving at a constant speed, the acceleration is zero ($\vec{a} = 0$), which means the net force must be zero ($\sum \vec{F} = 0$). For the forces to "cancel out" and sum to zero, the free-body diagram (FBD) must account for all components acting on the block.


The Breakdown of Forces
To have a balanced system, you need three distinct force vectors:



* Gravity ($F_g$): This points straight down toward the center of the Earth.

* Normal Force ($F_N$): This points perpendicular to the surface of the inclined plane.

* Friction ($F_f$): Since the block is sliding down, friction must point parallel to the slope, upwards.


Why D is misleading (and which is likely "correct")

* Diagram D shows a force pointing straight down (Gravity), one pointing up and to the right (Normal), and one pointing up and to the left (Friction). While it looks balanced, the vectors are often drawn incorrectly in these types of multiple-choice questions.

* Gravity pointing straight down.

* Normal Force perpendicular to the slope.

* Friction acting opposite the direction of motion.





In C, if you resolve the gravity vector into its components—one perpendicular to the plane ($F_g \cos \theta$) and one parallel to the plane ($F_g \sin \theta$)—you can see how they are perfectly countered by the Normal force and Friction, respectively.


Mathematical Balance
For constant speed on an incline of angle $\theta$:



* Perpendicular axis: $F_N = F_g \cos \theta$

* Parallel axis: $F_f = F_g \sin \theta$


Diagram D fails because the "Friction" vector and "Normal" vector are not oriented correctly relative to the "Gravity" vector to represent a real-world inclined plane.
