## Gemini

To derive the formulas for terminal voltage ($V_{\text{terminal}} = \epsilon \pm Ir$) and back EMF ($V_{\text{back}} = \epsilon - Ir$), we look at how energy conservation and Kirchhoff's Loop Rule apply to real-world components like batteries and DC motors.


Here is the breakdown of how these equations are built from fundamental principles.



1. Deriving Terminal Voltage: $V_{\text{terminal}} = \epsilon \pm Ir$
A real battery consists of two things in series: an ideal source of electromotive force ($\epsilon$, the chemical potential energy) and an internal resistance ($r$). The terminal voltage $V_{\text{terminal}}$ is the actual voltage you measure across the physical battery leads.


Case A: Discharging (Battery Supplying Power)
When a battery runs a circuit, current $I$ leaves the positive terminal. As this current passes through the battery's own internal resistance $r$, a small amount of voltage drops inside the battery.


Applying Kirchhoff's Loop Rule starting from the negative terminal:



* Gain potential moving across the chemical EMF: $+\epsilon$

* Lose potential dropping across the internal resistance in the direction of current: $-Ir$


$$\text{Voltage leaving the battery} = \epsilon - Ir$$
$$V_{\text{terminal}} = \epsilon - Ir$$


Case B: Charging (Battery Receiving Power)
If you connect the battery to a higher external voltage source (like a car alternator or a wall charger), current is forced backwards into the positive terminal.


Because the current direction through the battery is reversed:



* You must overcome the internal EMF: $\epsilon$

* You must also push through the internal resistance in the opposite direction, requiring an additional voltage push: $+Ir$


$$V_{\text{terminal}} = \epsilon + Ir$$



2. Deriving Back EMF: $V_{\text{back}} = \epsilon - Ir$
A DC motor is structurally identical to a DC generator. When you supply a terminal voltage ($V$) to a motor, current flows through its armature windings, which have a resistance ($R$ or $r$). This current causes the motor shaft to spin.


However, because the armature coils are now spinning inside a magnetic field, Faraday's Law of Induction states that they will generate their own electromotive force. According to Lenz's Law, this induced EMF must oppose the external voltage that created it. This is why it is called counter EMF or back EMF ($V_{\text{back}}$ or $\epsilon_{\text{back}}$).


The Loop Equation
If we trace the loop of the motor circuit while it is running:



* The external line provides an input supply voltage: $+V$

* A voltage drop occurs across the internal armature resistance: $-Ir$

* The rotating armature induces an opposing voltage drop: $-V_{\text{back}}$


Setting the sum of potential changes around the closed loop to zero (Kirchhoff's Voltage Law):


$$V - Ir - V_{\text{back}} = 0$$


Rearranging this formula to solve for the back EMF gives:


$$V_{\text{back}} = V - Ir$$


(Note: On your formula sheet, the symbols are swapped to $V_{\text{back}} = \epsilon - Ir$, where $\epsilon$ is used as the external source voltage supply and $r$ represents the internal resistance of the motor windings).


Connecting to Your Assignment Questions

* At Startup (Question 4a): The motor speed is zero, so no magnetic flux is changing. Because it isn't spinning, $V_{\text{back}} = 0$. The equation simplifies directly to Ohm's Law: $V = Ir \implies I = \frac{V}{r}$.

* At Full Speed (Question 4b): The motor spins rapidly, generating a significant opposing $V_{\text{back}} = 108\text{ V}$. This leaves a much smaller net voltage ($V - V_{\text{back}} = 12\text{ V}$) to push current through the $5.0\ \Omega$ windings, which drops the current draw drastically.

The handwriting attempts to use the turns ratio ($N_p / N_s$) to find the secondary current ($I_s$) while scaling it by $75%$. However, efficiency ($\eta$) is strictly a relationship between output power and input power, not a direct multiplier for the turns ratio.


Furthermore, because the problem explicitly gives you the measured secondary voltage ($V_s = 40\text{ V}$), you do not need to use the turns ratio at all.


Here is the breakdown of why the current formula failed and how to solve it correctly.



The Flaw in the Current Formula
Efficiency is defined as:
$$\text{Efficiency } (\eta) = \frac{\text{Power Out}}{\text{Power In}} = \frac{P_s}{P_p}$$


Since Electric Power ($P$) equals Voltage ($V$) multiplied by Current ($I$):
$$\eta = \frac{V_s \cdot I_s}{V_p \cdot I_p}$$


Your handwritten formula assumed that $I_s$ scales down by $75%$ based on the turns ratio. In reality, because the transformer is inefficient, the secondary voltage dropped to $40\text{ V}$ (an ideal transformer with these turns would have a secondary voltage of $50\text{ V}$). The loss of efficiency affects both voltage and current simultaneously.



The Correct Solution
By using the power efficiency formula, we can solve for $I_s$ directly using the given values.


1. Identify the given variables:



* Efficiency ($\eta$) = $75% = 0.75$

* Primary Voltage ($V_p$) = $100\text{ V}$

* Primary Current ($I_p$) = $0.010\text{ A}$

* Secondary Voltage ($V_s$) = $40\text{ V}$


2. Rearrange the efficiency equation to solve for $I_s$:
$$I_s = \frac{\eta \cdot V_p \cdot I_p}{V_s}$$


3. Substitute the values:
$$I_s = \frac{0.75 \cdot 100\text{ V} \cdot 0.010\text{ A}}{40\text{ V}}$$


$$I_s = \frac{0.75\text{ W}}{40\text{ V}}$$


$$I_s = 0.01875\text{ A}$$



Final Answer
The correct secondary current is $0.01875\text{ A}$ (or $1.88 \times 10^{-2}\text{ A}$ if rounding to significant figures).
