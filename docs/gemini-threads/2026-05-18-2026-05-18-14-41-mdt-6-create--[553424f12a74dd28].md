### 1. Ensuring Equal Charge
To ensure both balloons acquire the exact same charge, the experimenter should use two identical balloons. Both balloons must be rubbed with the exact same material (such as a piece of wool or fur) for the same amount of time and with the identical number of strokes. For an even more precise equal charge distribution, the balloons could be coated in a conductive material (like graphite spray). The experimenter could then charge one balloon and physically touch it to the other. Because they are identical conducting spheres, the charge would distribute exactly equally between them.

### 2. Determining the Charge
To calculate the specific charge on each balloon, the following procedure could be executed:
1.  **Measure** the mass ($m$) of each identical balloon using a digital scale before hanging them.
2.  **Control** the length of the string ($L$) by measuring and cutting equal lengths of thread for both balloons.
3.  Charge both balloons equally using the method described above, and allow them to hang from a common point and repel each other until they reach a state of static equilibrium.
4.  **Measure** the linear distance ($r$) between the centers of the two balloons at equilibrium.
5.  **Vary** the initial amount of charge (by rubbing more or fewer times) to collect multiple sets of data ($r$ values) to ensure reliability.

By analyzing the free-body diagram of one balloon, the electrostatic force ($F_E$) is balanced by the horizontal component of the tension ($T_x$), while gravity ($mg$) is balanced by the vertical component ($T_y$). Using the measured string length $L$ and distance $r$, the angle $\theta$ from the vertical can be found using $\sin\theta = \frac{r/2}{L}$. Because $\tan\theta = \frac{F_E}{mg}$, the electrostatic force is $F_E = mg \tan\theta$. Finally, substituting this into Coulomb’s Law, $F_E = \frac{kQ^2}{r^2}$, allows the experimenter to solve for the charge $Q$ on each balloon.

### 3. Analyzing Asymmetrical Equilibrium
In some trials, the balloons may reach an asymmetrical equilibrium where one balloon hangs nearly straight down while the other is repelled further out to the side. The most physically accurate reason for this is that **the balloons have unequal masses**. 

According to Newton's Third Law, the electrostatic force ($F_E$) exerted by balloon A on balloon B is *exactly equal and opposite* to the force exerted by balloon B on balloon A, regardless of whether their charges are equal or unequal. Therefore, a simple mismatch in charge would *still* result in symmetrical repulsion.

However, the angle at which a balloon hangs depends on the ratio of the horizontal electrostatic force to the downward gravitational force ($\tan\theta = \frac{F_E}{mg}$). Because $F_E$ is identical for both balloons, the balloon with the **larger mass** (due to variations in the mass of the uninflated rubber itself or a differing mass of contained air if unequally inflated) will experience a stronger downward gravitational pull. This stronger downward force causes the heavier balloon to hang at a smaller angle (closer to the vertical), while the lighter balloon is pushed out to a larger angle.

## Gemini

You are exactly right. Tension is strictly a pulling force—a string cannot push an object.


In a Free Body Diagram, force vectors should always point in the direction of the force acting on the object. Because the strings are holding the balloons up and pulling them toward the center, the tension vectors ($T_1$ and $T_2$) must originate at the balloons and point away from them, tracing up the strings toward the pivot.


The downward-pointing arrows in your current diagrams imply the strings are pushing down and out into the balloons. To make the FBDs physically accurate, you just need to flip those green arrowheads at the bottom so they pull upward and inward.
