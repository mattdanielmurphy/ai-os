## Gemini

The terminal doesn't have eyes to natively see "smooth lines," so you have to measure this using mathematical proxies.


For vector-style graphics (logos, icons), the two best proxies for quality are pixel density (file size relative to dimensions, which weeds out heavily compressed, artifact-ridden images) and standard deviation (edge contrast and sharpness, which weeds out blurry, upscaled images).


Here are two ways to do this on macOS, ranging from native tools to the exact mathematical measurement.


Option 1: Native macOS (Dimensions + Compression Proxy)
You can use sips (built into macOS) and stat to calculate the total pixel count and file size, sorting the list so the largest, least-compressed images bubble to the top. A high pixel count with a high file size usually means fewer compression artifacts and smoother lines.


Run this directly in the directory containing your 96 images:


for img in *.{png,jpg,jpeg}; do
  # Grab dimensions using macOS native sips
  w=$(sips -g pixelWidth "$img" 2>/dev/null | awk '/pixelWidth/ {print $NF}')
  h=$(sips -g pixelHeight "$img" 2>/dev/null | awk '/pixelHeight/ {print $NF}')
  
  if [ -n "$w" ]; then
    pixels=$((w * h))
    size=$(stat -f %z "$img") # Get file size in bytes
    echo "$pixels|$size|${w}x${h}|$img"
  fi
done | sort -t'|' -k1,1nr -k2,2nr | column -t -s'|'

Outputs: Total Pixels | File Size (Bytes) | Dimensions | Filename


Option 2: ImageMagick (The Sharpness Metric)
If you want to actively detect crisp edges and smooth lines, you need to measure the image's standard deviation. A blurry, poorly upscaled image will have a low standard deviation, while a sharp, pristine logo will have a high one.


If you don't have it installed, grab it via Homebrew (brew install imagemagick), then run this one-liner:


magick identify -format "%[fx:w*h] | %[standard_deviation] | %f\n" *.{png,jpg,jpeg} 2>/dev/null | sort -t'|' -k1,1nr -k2,2nr | column -t -s'|'

Here is why the physics of bees doesn't fit inside a greenhouse, and the few clever ways growers actually do automate pollination without using a paintbrush.



The Problem with Honeybees in a Greenhouse
Honeybees are hardwired for the open sky. When you put them in an enclosed space, two major evolutionary design flaws kick in:



* The Glass Ceiling Trap: Honeybees navigate using the sun and UV light polarization. When they find themselves inside a structure with glass or polycarbonate panels, they fly straight up toward the light, bounce against the ceiling, and continuously try to fly through it until they exhaust themselves and die on the floor.

* The "Too Small" Factor: A single standard honeybee hive holds 20,000 to 50,000 bees. A 250 sq. ft. room has nowhere near enough forage to sustain them. They would strip the 500 strawberry flowers of pollen in an hour, realize there is a massive food shortage, grow highly stressed, and turn aggressive.



The Real-World Alternatives: Commercial Hacks
If you want to fire yourself from the morning makeup-brush routine, commercial greenhouse growers use two specific methods instead:


1. Bumblebees (The True Greenhouse Workers)
If you absolutely want a biological solution, you don't use honeybees; you use Bumblebees (Bombus impatiens). Commercial insect companies actually sell small, disposable greenhouse-specific bumblebee mini-hives (called "quads").



* Why they work: Bumblebees are much larger, visually navigate differently, and don't care about the glass ceiling effect. They are solitary-style colonial nesters, so the hives are tiny (only 50 to 100 bees).

* The "Buzz Pollination" Feature: Strawberries love bumblebees because of a behavior called sonication. The bee grabs the flower and vibrates its flight muscles at a specific frequency (around a middle C note). This explosive vibration literally blasts the pollen loose inside the flower, resulting in exceptionally perfect, massive fruit.

* The Scale Problem: Even a mini-hive is usually overkill for a tiny 250 sq. ft. space. They will often over-visit the same few hundred flowers, accidentally bruising them.


2. The Oscillating Fan Hack (The Simplest Automation)
Strawberry flowers are "perfect," meaning a single flower contains both the male parts (anthers with pollen) and female parts (pistils). They don't actually need an insect to carry pollen from another plant; they just need the pollen to move a fraction of a millimeter from the edge of the flower to the center.


Instead of buying bees, most small-scale indoor growers install a series of highly oscillating horizontal fans or automated air-pulsing blowers.



* By timing the fans to kick on a higher, turbulent setting for an hour every morning, you create enough artificial wind to shake the flower stems and drop the pollen directly onto the center receptors.

* While it isn't quite as flawless as a bumblebee or a meticulous human with a brush, it gets you about 90–95% of the way to perfect fruit geometry with zero maintenance and zero stings.
