## Gemini

Actually, heat (internal energy) usually is the heavyweight here, even if it feels less obvious than a loud "thud" or a squished piece of putty.


Here is the breakdown of the "energy hierarchy" in an inelastic collision:


1. Why Heat Wins
When you deform the putty, you are breaking and reforming molecular bonds. At a microscopic level, that mechanical work ends up as random kinetic energy of the molecules. In physics, "random molecular motion" is the literal definition of thermal energy. While the putty might only feel slightly warmer to the touch, that small temperature change across the entire mass usually accounts for the vast majority of the original kinetic energy.


2. The Deceptive Nature of Sound
Sound feels significant because our ears are incredibly sensitive to pressure waves. However, the actual amount of energy required to create a "thud" is remarkably small.



* To put it in perspective: if you yelled at a cup of coffee for eight years, you might barely have enough energy to heat it up for a sip.

* Most of the energy that creates the sound is actually dissipated into the wall and the putty as—you guessed it—heat.


3. Deformation (Work)
Deformation is the "permanent" part of the energy sink. In materials like putty or lead, the energy used to change their shape doesn't get "stored" like a spring; it gets "spent" to overcome internal friction as the molecules slide past each other. This process is almost entirely dissipative, meaning the work done to deform the object is ultimately converted into internal thermal energy.



The Reality Check
If you were to throw a 100g wad of putty at 20 m/s:



* Total KE: $20\text{ Joules}$.

* Sound: Might account for less than $0.01\text{ Joules}$.

* Heat/Internal Energy: The remaining $19.99\text{ Joules}$.
