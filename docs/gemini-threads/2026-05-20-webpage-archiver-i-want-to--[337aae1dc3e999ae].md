## Gemini

To save a webpage exactly as it appears on your screen—including dynamically loaded content, state changes, or pages behind login screens—standard browser saving often falls short because it tries to re-fetch assets publicly.


For Chrome, the most reliable way to bake everything (HTML, CSS, images, fonts) into a single, fully offline .html file is using a specialized browser extension designed for this exact purpose.


SingleFile (Recommended Extension)
SingleFile is an open-source extension that captures the current live state of the DOM (the page structure as it exists right now in your browser) and converts all external resources into embedded data URLs.



* How it handles auth/private data: Because it runs locally inside your active tab, it captures images and styles that require your current login session or cookie state, embedding them directly so they work completely offline.


How to use it:

* Install SingleFile from the Chrome Web Store.

* Navigate to the webpage and let it fully load (scroll down if it uses lazy-loading images).

* Click the SingleFile extension icon in your toolbar.

* It will process the page for a few seconds and automatically download a single .html file containing absolutely everything.



Chrome Native Alternative: MHTML
If you cannot or do not want to install an extension, Chrome has a built-in format called MHTML (Mime HTML), which archives the entire page into a single file.



* Press Ctrl + S (Windows) or Cmd + S (macOS) to open the Save dialog.

* In the Format or Save as type dropdown, look for Webpage, Single File (.mhtml or .mnt).

* If you do not see this option, it can be forced via Chrome's experimental flags:

* Navigate to chrome://flags/#save-page-as-mhtml

* Set it to Enabled and relaunch Chrome.






Note on Compatibility: While MHTML works well natively within Chrome and Chromium-based browsers, the resulting file is less portable than the standard .html file generated by SingleFile, which can open seamlessly in any browser or mobile device.

To address your deeper questions, you've actually stumbled onto some of the most interesting parts of particle physics and chemistry.



1. Why don't two neutral objects attract each other?
You’re right to wonder about this. If the charges could move, why don't they?
The problem is the "First Shove." * A charged object has an existing Electric Field. It reaches out and "forces" the electrons in a neutral object to move.



* Two neutral objects have no net electric field. Because their positive and negative charges are perfectly mixed, they don't exert any "push" or "pull" on each other from a distance.

* Since there is no "shove" to start the separation, the charges stay mixed, and the objects stay indifferent to each other.


The Exception (London Dispersion Forces): On a microscopic level (atoms and molecules), you are actually 100% correct. Electrons are constantly wobbling. By pure chance, for a tiny fraction of a second, more electrons might end up on one side of an atom than the other. This creates a tiny, temporary "charged" side that induces a shift in the neighbor atom. This is called a London Dispersion Force, and it’s why even neutral gases (like Neon) can eventually be turned into liquids.






2. How "free" are these charges?
This depends entirely on the Material.



* Conductors (Metals): Electrons are like water in a pipe. They aren't "owned" by any one atom and can flow across the entire object almost instantly.

* Insulators (Plastic/Glass): Electrons are "tethered" to their atoms. They can’t run to the other side of the object, but they can lean as far as their "leash" allows. This is why a balloon (insulator) can still be attracted to a wall—the atoms just stretch or rotate rather than the electrons flowing across the surface.



3. Do they travel at light speed?
This is a classic physics "gotcha." There are two different speeds at play here:



* The Signal Speed: When you bring a charged rod near a metal sphere, the "instruction" for electrons to move travels at nearly the speed of light. The electric field propagates through the material almost instantly.

* The Drift Velocity: The actual physical electrons are surprisingly slow. They bounce around like pinballs in a crowded machine. In a typical wire, an individual electron might only move at about 1 millimeter per second.


It’s like a long row of train cars: if the engine hits the first car, the "clunk" (the signal) reaches the back of the train very fast, even if the train itself is barely crawling.



4. Does the size matter?
Absolutely.



* Distance ($r^2$): As you noted, the further the "repelling" charges are pushed away, the less they can fight back against the "attracting" charges. This is why induction is much more noticeable in larger conductive objects.

* Surface Area: A larger object has more room for charges to spread out. If you have a massive neutral object, you can "hide" the repelling charges so far away that their effect on the original charged object becomes mathematically negligible.



Summary for your notes:
Attraction to a neutral object requires an external field to break the internal balance. Once that balance is broken (Polarization), the $1/r^2$ math ensures the "close" attraction is always stronger than the "far" repulsion.

When describing a vector direction, we typically start from the nearest cardinal axis (North, South, East, or West).

* If it’s closer to the East-West line, we use "___ of East" or "___ of West."

35° E of N is exactly the same direction as 55° N of E.



(Because $90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$). However, most teachers prefer the version with the smaller angle (anything under 45°) because it makes the vector's orientation easier to visualize at a glance.

How to decide on a test:
