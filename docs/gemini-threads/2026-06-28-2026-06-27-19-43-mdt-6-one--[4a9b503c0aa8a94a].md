[2026-06-27 19:43 MDT-6] one thing I hate about macos is the infinite dock bouncing apps can do sometimes. I don't think it's ever necessary and its super annoying. Can I disable it entirely or something?

---

## Gemini

It is incredibly distracting when an app sits there jumping up and down like a toddler who needs attention, especially when it is stuck in a loop.


While macOS allows you to turn off the animation for when you click an icon to launch an app, Apple does not provide a single checkbox in the System Settings to completely disable the continuous alerting bounce (the one that triggers when an app wants your attention in the background).


However, you can completely kill the bounce or heavily restrict it using a few different methods.



Option 1: Disable the "Alert" Bounce Globally (Terminal)
If you want to stop apps from bouncing to get your attention while they are running in the background, you can disable the no-bouncing setting via the Terminal.


Open your Terminal app and run the following command:


defaults write com.apple.dock no-bouncing -bool TRUE && killall Dock


What this does: This sets the Dock's hidden preference for alert-bouncing to false and restarts the Dock process to apply the change. Apps will still show a small dot underneath them to indicate they are open, but they will stop jumping.


If you ever want to undo this and bring the bounce back, change TRUE to FALSE:
defaults write com.apple.dock no-bouncing -bool FALSE && killall Dock




Option 2: The Core System Setting (Launch Animation Only)
If you also want to stop the bounce that happens specifically while an app is opening, you can toggle this off in the standard settings UI.



* Open System Settings.

* Navigate to Desktop & Dock in the sidebar.

* Find the toggle labeled Animate opening applications and turn it off.



Option 3: Address the "Infinite" Loop Bug
If you notice a specific app is bouncing indefinitely and won't stop until you force quit it, it is usually because the app is trying to display a hidden modal dialog box or permissions prompt that didn't render properly on screen.


If Option 1 is a bit too scorched-earth and you prefer to keep normal alerts but just hate the endless loop, third-party utilities like Hammerspoon can be scripted to automatically intercept and suppress persistent dock notifications, though the Terminal command in Option 1 is generally the cleanest way to reclaim your focus.

Here is a breakdown of why those claims hold up, along with a few practical nuances to keep in mind:


1. The Problem with Multi-Blade Cartridges

* The "Lift-and-Cut" Flaw: Mass-market multi-blade razors utilize a mechanical process called hysteresis. The first blade pulls the hair taut, and the subsequent blades cut it below the skin line. While this creates a temporary feeling of extreme smoothness, the hair frequently retracts beneath the surface, curling and piercing the follicle wall as it grows back—the primary cause of ingrown hairs and razor bumps.

* Skin Scraping: Passing three to five flexible blades over your face means you are essentially scraping the top layer of your skin multiple times in a single stroke, requiring lubricating strips to mask the friction and irritation.


2. The Henson Solution: Rigidity and Tight Tolerances

* Eliminating "Blade Chatter": Traditional plastic cartridge razors allow the blades to flex and vibrate as they hit dense hair. Henson addresses this by Aerospace CNC-machining their aluminum handles to a tolerance of $\pm 0.0005$ inches ($\approx 12.7\text{ \mu m}$). The top cap and base plate clamp a standard double-edge blade completely flat right up to the cutting edge. This eliminates deflection and vibration (chatter), resulting in a cleaner cut with less tugging.

* The 30-Degree "Training Wheels": Classic safety razors require you to manually maintain a perfect 30° wrist angle against your face. Henson engineered wide, flat facets directly onto the razor head. When you rest the flat surface against your skin, it automatically locks the blade into the optimal 30° shaving plane, removing the learning curve entirely.


3. Long-Term Economics

* The Cost Trap: The ad emphasizes that while the handle is an investment upfront, the blades cost pennies. This is true. Standard double-edge safety blades typically cost between $0.10 and $0.20 each, compared to mass-market multi-blade cartridges which can easily cost $4.00 to $6.00 per refill.



Nuances to Consider Before Switching
While the physics and build quality match the marketing, a single-blade precision razor does change the experience in two distinct ways:



* Closeness vs. Shadow: Because it shears the hair precisely at the skin level rather than pulling it out from underneath, individuals with dark, fast-growing hair may notice a "5 o'clock shadow" appear slightly sooner in the day compared to using a multi-blade system.

* Light Weight: The aircraft aluminum construction makes the razor exceptionally light (about 37 grams). If you are transitionining from traditional, heavy brass safety razors, you might miss the substantial heft that allows gravity to do the cutting work, meaning you have to guide it firmly with your fingertips without pushing into your skin.
