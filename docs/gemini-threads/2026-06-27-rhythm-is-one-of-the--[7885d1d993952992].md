## Gemini

It absolutely is. It’s essentially a linguistic obstacle course.


The main reason it feels so bizarre to English speakers is that it's an etymological import that kept its original Greek formatting.


Here is why the spelling is so wild:


1. The Greek "Rh" (rho)
In ancient Greek, the word was rhythmos (measured motion). When a Greek word started with the letter rho ($\rho$), it was often pronounced with a breathy, aspirated sound. When the Romans borrowed these words into Latin, they used rh to represent that sound. English inherited that spelling directly.


2. The Missing Traditional Vowels
It is one of the longest common English words to not contain any of the standard vowels (a, e, i, o, u). Instead, it relies entirely on y to act as the vowel, which is a direct relic of the Greek letter upsilon ($\upsilon$).


3. The Consonant Pile-up
Finishing a word with -thm forces your mouth to transition from a voiced th sound directly into a closed-lip m sound without any vocalic breathing room. It's an incredibly rare phonetic ending in English, which is why it looks so unnatural on the page.




Fun Fact: Because it's such a notorious misspelling trap, it's one of the most common words to rely on mnemonics just to get right, like: Rhythm Helps Your Two Hips Move.

Here is a breakdown of the unique style and mesmerizing vocal mechanics you are hearing.



The Genre: Qawwali
Qawwali is a form of Sufi devotional music originating from the Indian subcontinent. It is deeply rooted in Islamic mysticism, where the performance functions as a spiritual journey. The core objective is to guide both the performers and the audience into a state of religious ecstasy and heightened consciousness (sama).


The Group Dynamic
What looks like a duet or intense call-and-response is actually a tightly coordinated ensemble known as a Qawwali Party.



* The Leader: Nusrat sits at the center, steering the musical direction and improvising melodies on the fly.

* The Harmonium & Percussion: The driving force comes from the harmonium (the hand-pumped reed organ) providing a melodic drone, paired with the complex rhythms of the tabla.

* The Chorus: The surrounding ensemble provides the rhythmic handclapping and echoes the lead phrases, acting as a powerful sonic safety net that amplifies the emotional weight of the lead vocals.



Decoding the Vocal Technique
The mind-bending pitch jumps and rapid-fire textures that caught your attention are derived from centuries of rigorous training in classical Hindustani music.



* Sargam Improvisation: When you hear him rapidly singing microtonal sequences, he is often executing Sargam—singing the actual names of the musical notes (Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni) at breakneck speed.

* Taan (Acrobatic Melodic Runs): The lightning-fast ascending and descending patterns sung on open vowel sounds (like "Ah") are called Taan. It requires extraordinary breath control and abdominal support to hit every individual microtone precisely without sliding or blurring the pitches.

* Gamaka & Murki (The Vocal Oscillations): The "tongue wiggle" or rapid flutter effect is a technique involving swift, deliberate oscillations of the throat, soft palate, and jaw. By lifting the soft palate (similar to the internal mouth posture used by opera singers), he opens up a massive resonant cavity. This allows him to rapidly flutter between notes and execute complex rhythmic ornamentations while maintaining total clarity and volume.


It is an incredibly physically demanding art form, essentially blending elite athletic breath control with profound spiritual expression.

---

## Gemini

Those spikes are called chits or sprouts (and the process of them growing is known as chitting).


They grow from the "eyes" of the potato, which are actually dormant buds. When a potato is exposed to warmth and light, it triggers these buds to wake up and start growing into new stems, preparing the potato to reproduce.


Are they safe?

* Don't eat the sprouts: The sprouts contain high concentrations of solanine and chaconine, which are toxic glycoalkaloids that can cause digestive upset or headaches.

* The potato itself: If the potato is still firm and the sprouts are small, you can just snap or cut the sprouts off, peel the potato, and cook it safely. If the potato has gone soft, wrinkled, or turned distinctly green, it's best to toss it out, as the toxin levels have likely spread into the flesh.

## Gemini

That visceral reaction makes total sense. They look intensely alien and aggressive, almost like a parasitic fungus or something out of a horror movie rather than a clean, natural plant.


There's actually a biological reason why they look so creepy compared to normal houseplants:



* No light formatting: When plants grow in the dark (like in a pantry or underground), they go through a process called etiolation. Because they aren't getting light, they don't produce chlorophyll, so they stay pale, ghostly white, or a weird sickly purple instead of a healthy green.

* Rapid cell elongation: The potato is essentially panicking to find light before its stored energy runs out. It pours all its resources into rapidly stretching out these weird, knobby, leafless stalks.


When you combine that pale, fleshy color with the erratic, bumpy texture bursting out of the "skin," it triggers the exact same evolutionary disgust response we have toward mold, infections, or parasites. It's basically your brain's defense mechanism screaming, "That doesn't look right, don't touch it."

## Gemini

Yes, exactly. It’s a clear sign that the potato has been sitting around for a while.


Potatoes are actually living tubers in a dormant (sleeping) state when you buy them. Eventually, that dormancy wears off. The older a potato gets, the more its internal clock says, "Time to grow," especially if it has been exposed to a little bit of warmth, humidity, or ambient light.


As those sprouts grow, they are actively draining the water and starch out of the potato to feed themselves. That’s why really old sprouted potatoes get soft, spongy, and wrinkled—the sprouts are literally sucking the life out of them.
