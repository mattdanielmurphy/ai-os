## Gemini

Mr. Nelson (2100 kg) rear-ended Ms. Grisham (2600 kg) while she was stopped. The combined vehicles moved at 19 km/h immediately after impact. If Mr. Nelson claims he was traveling at 35 km/h, is he being truthful?


To determine if he is telling the truth, you can use the principle of conservation of momentum for an inelastic collision:


$$m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i} = (m_1 + m_2)v_f$$


Where:



* $m_1 = 2100\text{ kg}$ (Mr. Nelson's car)

* $v_{1i} = ?$ (Mr. Nelson's initial velocity)

* $m_2 = 2600\text{ kg}$ (Ms. Grisham's car)

* $v_{2i} = 0\text{ km/h}$ (Ms. Grisham was stopped)

* $m_1 + m_2 = 4700\text{ kg}$

* $v_f = 19\text{ km/h}$


Calculation:
$$2100 \times v_{1i} + 2600 \times 0 = 4700 \times 19$$
$$2100 \times v_{1i} = 89300$$
$$v_{1i} \approx 42.5\text{ km/h}$$


Based on this calculation, Mr. Nelson would have needed to be traveling at approximately 42.5 km/h to result in a post-collision velocity of 19 km/h. Therefore, his testimony of 35 km/h is likely inaccurate.

## Gemini

The "best" Newton's cradle is defined by how closely it adheres to the physics of elastic collisions—specifically, how well it minimizes energy loss through friction, sound, and air resistance.


What Makes a High-Quality Cradle
A true high-performance Newton's cradle is engineered as a precision instrument rather than a decorative desk toy. The key features that distinguish the best models include:



* Mass and Material: Spheres made of high-density, solid steel (often nickel-plated) are essential. They must be perfectly spherical and identical in mass to ensure conservation of momentum is demonstrated accurately.

* Bifilar Suspension: The best cradles use two strings for each ball (a V-shape), which constrains the motion to a single plane. Single-string setups allow the balls to wobble and drift, causing energy to dissipate as they move in unintended directions.

* Precision Alignment: The balls must be perfectly aligned so they are precisely touching when at rest. Even microscopic gaps lead to "clacking" or erratic motion, where the middle balls start to swing rather than remaining stationary.

* Rigid Frame: A frame that is heavy and stable (often aluminum or solid wood) ensures that no energy is lost into the frame itself via vibration or flexing.


Motion and Sustainability
Regarding how long they can sustain motion, there is no set "record" because sustainability is limited by physics. Every collision loses a small amount of energy to heat and sound, and the balls lose energy to air resistance.



* Duration: A well-made desktop model might swing for 30–50 seconds before the visible amplitude of the end balls diminishes significantly. Large-scale, museum-grade cradles can sustain motion for longer due to the higher mass of the spheres, which gives them more inertia relative to the energy lost to friction at the suspension points.

* The "Stationary Middle": You asked for a version where the middle balls stay stationary. This is actually how a perfectly tuned Newton’s cradle is designed to behave. When the spheres are of identical mass and perfectly aligned, the momentum of the moving end ball is transmitted through the chain of stationary balls (which act as a medium) and is imparted entirely to the ball on the opposite end. If your current cradle makes the middle balls dance or swing, it is usually because the spheres are not perfectly aligned, or the suspension is too loose, causing the energy to dissipate rather than transfer through the chain.


If you are looking for the highest quality experience, commercial models labeled as "executive desk toys" (often found on sites like Amazon) typically fail to meet these standards. Investing in custom-built versions from specialized kinetic sculpture creators—where the frames are laser-cut and the strings are calibrated with high-tension aircraft-grade cables—is the only way to achieve that "stationary middle" effect consistently.
