The issue with your LaTeX code is that the underscore symbol (_) is a reserved character in LaTeX used to trigger a subscript. When you write at_impact inside the math environment, KaTeX interprets the underscore as the start of a subscript command, and because it is not properly escaped or handled within the context of text inside math mode, it causes a parsing error or renders incorrectly.


To fix this, you have two primary options depending on your goal:


1. Escape the underscore (Recommended)
If you want the underscore to appear as a literal character in your subscript, use a backslash to escape it: \_.


$$v_{\text{at\_impact}}^2 = v_0^2 + 2(a_{\text{bike}})(d_1)$$

2. Use \text with proper spacing
If your environment is sensitive to the underscore, or if you simply want the text to look cleaner, ensure the entire subscript is wrapped in \text{...}.


Corrected Code:
$$v_{\text{at_impact}}^2 = v_0^2 + 2(a_{\text{bike}})(d_1)$$



Key Takeaways for KaTeX/LaTeX:



* Reserved Characters: Characters like _, ^, \, {, and } perform specific structural functions in LaTeX. Always escape symbols if you intend for them to be displayed as plain text.

* The \text{} Command: This is the best practice for inserting labels or descriptive text into math equations. It ensures the text inside renders in upright, non-italicized font, which is standard for variables that are not single letters (like at_impact or bike).

* Grouping: Always use curly braces {} to group multiple characters together when defining a subscript or superscript; otherwise, LaTeX will only apply the formatting to the very next character.

## Gemini

To answer your question directly: Do not use (X m/s)^2. That would be mathematically incorrect.


Why (X m/s)^2 is incorrect
The expression (X m/s)^2 implies that you are squaring the entire unit, which would result in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$.


Your units represent acceleration, which is defined as the rate of change of velocity ($\Delta v / \Delta t$). Velocity is in meters per second ($\text{m/s}$) and time is in seconds ($\text{s}$), so:
$$\frac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}} = \text{m/s}^2$$


How to format it correctly
In technical writing and physics, the standard way to write this is $\text{m/s}^2$ or $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$. If you are concerned about the exponent "floating" or looking ambiguous, you have two clean options:



* Standard Notation: \text{m/s}^2
This renders as $\text{m/s}^2$. It is universally understood that the square applies only to the seconds.

* Fractional Notation: \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}
This renders as $\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$. This is the most unambiguous way to present the unit.


Recommendation for your document
Given that you are doing formal physics coursework, sticking to the standard $\text{m/s}^2$ is perfectly fine and preferred. If you find that your LaTeX renderer is placing the 2 in a way that looks like it is modifying the entire m/s block, wrap the s in braces:

This explicitly tells the renderer that the superscript 2 belongs specifically to the s. This keeps your notes professional and avoids the unit conversion error that adding extra parentheses would introduce.
