## Gemini

David Bohm approached the "hard problem" of consciousness not by trying to fit mind into a materialist box, but by suggesting that our current understanding of matter is fundamentally incomplete. Instead of seeing consciousness as an accidental byproduct of brain chemistry, Bohm proposed a framework where mind and matter are different aspects of the same underlying reality.


Here are the core pillars of his perspective:


The Implicate and Explicate Order
Bohm’s most significant contribution was the idea that the universe has two primary levels:



* The Explicate Order: This is the world we see—discrete objects, distinct locations, and linear time. This is where classical physics and biology operate.

* The Implicate Order: An underlying "enfolded" reality where everything is interconnected. Just as a hologram contains the whole image in every part, Bohm argued that the entire universe is enfolded into every point of space.


In this view, consciousness isn't "produced" by the brain in the explicate order; rather, it emerges from the implicate order just as matter does.


Soma-Significance
To bridge the gap between mind (psyche) and body (soma), Bohm introduced Soma-significance. He argued that the relationship is a two-way flow:



* Soma to Significance: A physical state (soma) carries a meaning (significance) to the mind.

* Significance to Soma: A mental meaning (significance) immediately manifests as a physical reaction (soma)—like the way a sudden frightening thought causes an instant spike in heart rate.


By treating these as a single, unbroken process, Bohm essentially bypassed the "hard problem" by denying the dualism that creates it. If mind and matter are two sides of the same coin, you no longer have to explain how one "creates" the other.


The Holomovement
Bohm used the term Holomovement to describe the constant process of enfolding and unfolding between these orders. He suggested that consciousness is a more "subtle" level of this movement, while matter is a more "manifest" or "gross" level.



"The mental and the material are two sides of one overall reality... they are not separate but are instead two different aspects of a single, undivided whole."



Comparison to Modern Theories
Bohm's ideas often align with Panpsychism or Dual-aspect Monism, though his version is unique because it is rooted in his interpretation of quantum mechanics (specifically the pilot-wave theory). While mainstream neuroscience still looks for "neural correlates of consciousness," Bohm’s work remains a primary alternative for those who believe the hard problem requires a total revision of our definition of matter itself.

## Gemini

No, David Bohm did not coin the phrase "the hard problem of consciousness."


That term was actually coined by the philosopher and cognitive scientist David Chalmers in 1994 during a talk at a conference in Tucson, Arizona. He later formalized the concept in his 1995 paper, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness."


While Bohm and Chalmers were dealing with the same fundamental mystery, they approached it from different angles:



* David Chalmers (The Namer): Focused on the philosophical distinction between "easy problems" (explaining brain functions like reaction times or integration of information) and the "hard problem" (explaining why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience/qualia).

* David Bohm (The Physicist): Was already developing his theories on the Implicate Order and Holomovement decades before Chalmers gave it that specific name. Bohm passed away in 1992, two years before Chalmers' famous talk.


Essentially, Bohm was working on a solution to the mystery (by rethinking the nature of matter itself) before the specific label we use today was ever officially minted.
