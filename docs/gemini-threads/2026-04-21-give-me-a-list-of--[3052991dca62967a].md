This recipe is scaled for 2 lbs (900g) of ground beef. It combines the savory "beefiness" of MSG with the classic aromatics your mom expects from a "packet-style" seasoning, but with significantly less sodium.


Master Taco Blend (2 lbs Beef)

Cooking Instructions

* Brown the 2 lbs of beef at 6 or 7 on your induction cooktop. Do not stir constantly—let it sit to develop a crust. Drain any excess fat.

* The Cumin Ratio: 1 tsp for 2 lbs is a very "polite" amount. It gives the characteristic taco scent without being overwhelming or bitter.

## Gemini

That leveler is likely doing more harm than good. If the blade has any flex or if you're pressing down at all, it creates a concave (scooped out) surface instead of a flat one. For lightweight powders like onion powder or paprika, that "crater" can easily cut 15–20% off your total weight.


Since you're aiming for precision, stop using the volume spoons and switch to your scale. Volumetric measurements for spices are notoriously unreliable because of how much the powder settles. A "packed" teaspoon of garlic powder can weigh twice as much as a "fluffy" one.


Here are the exact weights for your 2 lb batch so you can stop guessing:


2 lb Beef Seasoning (By Weight)

* Mild Paprika: 14g

* Salt: 12g

* Sugar: 12g

* Cornstarch: 12g (approx. 1.5 tbsp)

* Garlic Powder: 6g

* Onion Powder: 5g

* MSG (Accent): 4.5g

* Ground Cumin: 2.5g

* Black Pepper: 1.5g

* Dried Oregano: 1g



Why the "Leveler" is Failing You

* The Drag Effect: Spices like paprika and onion powder are slightly "sticky" due to oil content or moisture. When you drag a leveler across, it often pulls the "core" of the spice out of the spoon rather than shearing off the top.

* Consistency: A butter knife (the flat back edge) is actually better than a dedicated leveler because it has zero "give" and a wider surface area to ensure a true flat top.


Technical Advice for the Pan
If the meat still feels "weak" even after hitting these weights, it’s likely a concentration issue, not an amount issue.



* The Reduction: If you have more than a half-cup of liquid in there, the flavors are diluted.

* The Heat: Keep the induction at 6 with the lid off until that water is gone. You want the salt and MSG to be "bound" to the meat by the cornstarch, not floating in a broth.

## Gemini

Yes, you can add the cornstarch directly to your dry spice mix, but only if you haven't put the spices in the pan yet.


If you are pre-mixing a dry batch:
Mixing the cornstarch with the spices (paprika, garlic powder, etc.) is actually a pro move. The other spices act as "spacers" that keep the cornstarch particles separated. When you eventually add water to the pan, the starch is already dispersed, so it dissolves smoothly instead of clumping into little gelatinous "balls."


If you are adding it to the pan right now:
Do not just dump the dry cornstarch into the hot, wet pan. It will clump instantly.



* The Slurry Method: Take that 12g (1.5 tbsp) of cornstarch and whisk it into about 2–3 tbsp of cold water in a small glass until it looks like milk.

* The Pour: Pour that milky liquid into the simmering beef and stir immediately. You'll see it go from cloudy to a thick, glossy glaze in about 30 seconds.


Why this fixes the "Mushy" and "Weak" issues:

* Texture: The cornstarch creates a "velvet" coating. It stops the meat from feeling like wet, grainy crumbles and makes it feel more like a cohesive taco filling.

* Flavor: It "glues" the MSG, salt, and spices to the beef. Instead of the flavor sitting in the liquid at the bottom of the pan, every bite of meat is actually seasoned.


The "Old El Paso" Finish
Since you're aiming for that specific packet taste for your mom, that glossy look is half the battle. If the meat looks "matte" or dry, it won't taste like the kit. The cornstarch is what provides that "saucy" shine.


Current Pan Status: Is the liquid starting to thicken up, or is it still looking a bit watery? If it’s still thin, that extra bit of cornstarch slurry will tighten it up almost instantly.
