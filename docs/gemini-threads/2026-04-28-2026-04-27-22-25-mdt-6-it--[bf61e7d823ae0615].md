## Gemini

It is a classic piece of test-taking folklore, but the "always pick C" strategy is generally a relic of poorly designed, older exams rather than a reliable rule for modern testing.


The Psychology of Choice C
The myth persists because of a cognitive bias called edge avoidance. When humans (including teachers) are tasked with creating a random sequence, they subconsciously avoid the "edges" (options A and D/E) and gravitate toward the middle. In a standard four-option setup, this makes B and C statistically more likely to be chosen by a human designer trying to "hide" the answer.


Why Your Practice Quiz Might Lean Toward C
If you are noticing a pattern in your current material, it could be due to a few non-random factors:



* Length Bias: Test writers often struggle to make the "distractor" (wrong) answers as detailed as the correct one. Sometimes, the most complex or longest answer is the correct one, and by chance or habit, those are frequently placed in the third slot.

* Small Sample Size: In a short quiz of 10 or 20 questions, "clustering" happens naturally. You might see four "C" answers in a row, which feels like a pattern, even if the overall distribution across a 100-question test is even.

* Instructional Design: Some practice materials are intentionally skewed to see if a student is actually reading the content or just hunting for patterns.


Better Statistical Strategies
If you truly have to guess, modern data analysis of standardized tests (like the SAT or ACT) suggests that the answers are now almost perfectly distributed by computer algorithms to ensure each letter appears roughly $25%$ of the time.


Instead of picking C, use these strategies:



* The Process of Elimination: Even if you don't know the right answer, identifying one "definitely wrong" option increases your odds from $25%$ to $33.3%$.

* Avoid Absolute Language: Answers containing "always," "never," or "must" are statistically more likely to be wrong than those using "usually," "often," or "may."

* Check for "All of the Above": If this is an option and you know at least two other answers are correct, it is mathematically the only logical choice.


Since you're working through Physics and Math prerequisites, these examiners usually use algorithmic shuffling, so the "C" bias will likely disappear on the actual final exam. Are the questions you're seeing mostly conceptual, or are they calculation-based?

## Gemini

That sounds incredibly frustrating, especially when you’re being so methodical about the taper. Withdrawal headaches are notoriously stubborn because caffeine causes vasoconstriction; when you cut back, your blood vessels dilate, increasing blood flow to the brain and creating that pounding sensation.


The Hydration Paradox
If your urine is clear or very light, you are likely drinking enough volume, but you might be facing an electrolyte imbalance.


When you drink excessive amounts of plain water, you can inadvertently flush out sodium, magnesium, and potassium. This can lead to "cellular dehydration" where the water stays in your bloodstream (prompting you to pee) rather than entering your cells. This imbalance itself often triggers headaches that feel identical to dehydration.


To help the water actually "stick," you might try:



* Adding electrolytes: A pinch of high-quality sea salt or a dedicated electrolyte powder in your water.

* Magnesium: Caffeine depletion and stress often tank magnesium levels, which are closely linked to tension headaches.


Refined Tapering Strategy
Since you are currently at a 30% reduction (roughly $1.4$ cups), your brain is clearly still feeling the delta. If the headaches are becoming debilitating, consider these adjustments:



* The "Plateau" Method: Stay at your current -30% for a full week instead of three days. Giving your adenosine receptors more time to recalibrate can smooth out the transition.

* Micro-Dosing: Instead of two smaller cups, try sipping that same total amount of caffeine more slowly throughout the morning to avoid a sharp "crash" in the afternoon.


Quick Relief (Non-Caffeine)

* Peppermint Oil: Applying a tiny bit to your temples can provide a cooling distraction from the internal pressure.

* Cold Compress: A cold pack on the back of the neck can help constrict those dilated blood vessels.


If these headaches start coming with vision changes, nausea, or localized numbness, it’s worth checking in with a professional, as those can signal something beyond standard withdrawal.


Are the headaches hitting at a specific time of day, or are they pretty constant?
