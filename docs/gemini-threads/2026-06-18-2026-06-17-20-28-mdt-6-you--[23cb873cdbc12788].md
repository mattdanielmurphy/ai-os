## Gemini

You are thinking exactly along the lines of how advanced virtual tours and spatial computing handle this limitation.


Yes, mathematically and mechanically, that is completely possible. Instead of squeezing a wide perspective into a flat, narrow frame, you map the image onto a curved geometry—essentially treating the real estate photo less like a static print and more like a miniature 360° environment.


Here is how that workflow operates under the hood to eliminate the deceptive stretching:


1. Projecting onto a Cylinder or Sphere
When an ML model backsolves the focal length and lens distortion, it can unwrap that flat, stretched image and project it onto the inside of a virtual cylinder or sphere.



* The Result: The extreme stretching at the left and right edges disappears because those pixels are now curved "around" the viewer, exactly how they would exist in real life if you stood in the center of the room.


2. The Dynamic Viewport (Rectilinear Rendering)
Once the image is mapped to a cylinder, you introduce a constrained virtual camera (the viewport) with a "normal" focal length—roughly $35\text{mm}$ to $50\text{mm}$ equivalent, which closely mimics the central perspective of human vision.



* Eliminating Deception: Because the viewport is locked to a natural focal length, the deep wall-stretching disappears. A small bedroom actually looks like a small bedroom.

* User-Controlled Panning: To see the rest of the room, the user simply clicks and drags (or moves their phone) to pan left and right. The software dynamically calculates the correct, undistorted perspective for whatever specific angle the user is looking at in that moment.


Why We Don't See This Everywhere (Yet)
While the math is straightforward, doing this cleanly from a single standard wide-angle photo hits two practical snags:



* Aspect Ratio and Missing Data: A standard photo is usually a $3:2$ or $4:3$ rectangle. If you curve it into a panorama, you quickly run out of image at the top and bottom edges when you pan left or right, resulting in black, empty wedges.

* The "Outpainting" Fix: This is where modern generative AI steps in. Systems can take that curved frame and use generative fill (outpainting) to realistically guess and patch the missing floorboards, ceilings, or corner details that the photographer's lens cut off, making the panoramic panning seamless.


This transition from static, deceptive wide-shots to dynamic, ML-corrected viewports is effectively the middle ground between a cheap 2D gallery and a full 3D Matterport scan.


Would you want to see how the math projects a flat image onto a cylindrical coordinate system, or are you thinking about how this could be implemented in a web framework?
