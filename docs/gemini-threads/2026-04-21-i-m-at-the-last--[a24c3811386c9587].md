## Gemini

You’re right that it won't be overnight, but a week is actually a bit optimistic for feeling a "difference" in mood or energy. Vitamin D is fat-soluble and acts more like a hormone than a quick-acting stimulant, meaning it has to be processed by your liver and kidneys before it can start influencing things like serotonin or bone health.


The Timeline: What to Expect
While everyone’s biology is different (especially with factors like your location in Fort Sask and current medications), the clinical timeline generally looks like this:



* 1–2 Weeks: Your blood levels begin to creep up, but you likely won't feel anything yet.

* 4–8 Weeks: This is the "sweet spot" where many people start noticing a subjective lift in "brain fog" or general fatigue.

* 3+ Months: This is typically how long it takes to fully correct a deficiency and reach a stable "maintenance" level.



Why "Alberta Math" Makes it Harder
Since you’re in Alberta, you're fighting a bit of an uphill battle. Between October and April, the sun is at such an angle that your skin essentially makes zero Vitamin D, regardless of how long you're outside. By this time of year, most Albertans are at their absolute lowest point for the entire calendar year.


Optimization Tips (The "Developer" Approach)
Since you're already managing a complex schedule with the taper and school, here’s how to make the Vitamin D actually count:

* D3 over D2: Make sure your supplement is Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). It’s significantly more effective at raising and maintaining blood levels than D2.

* The Magnesium Connection: Magnesium is required for the enzymes that metabolize Vitamin D. If you're low on magnesium (common if you're dehydrated or high-stress), the Vitamin D might just sit there "unactivated."

Since you are likely on or eligible for the Alberta Adult Health Benefit or a similar low-income drug plan (like those tied to AISH or Income Support), here is how the mechanics work:



* The Prescription Loophole: Alberta Blue Cross and government-sponsored health plans generally do not cover "lifestyle" vitamins you just pick up off the shelf. * The "Medical Necessity" for Depression: You don't necessarily need a blood test to prove a deficiency first. A doctor can prescribe Vitamin D as an adjunct treatment for Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) or Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD).
