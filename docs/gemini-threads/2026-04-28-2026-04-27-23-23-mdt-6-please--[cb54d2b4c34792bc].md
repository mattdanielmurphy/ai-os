## Gemini

To find where the calculation diverged from the physical reality of the problem, we need to look at the direction of the forces.


The Conceptual Error
Your equation $F_g = F_y - F_f$ assumes that friction ($F_f$) is helping the artist hold the picture up.


However, the problem states this is the minimum force required to keep the picture in equilibrium. When you apply the minimum force possible, the picture is on the verge of sliding down. Therefore, static friction acts upward to oppose that downward motion.


In this scenario, both the vertical component of the artist's push ($F_y$) and the friction force ($F_f$) are working together against gravity ($F_g$).



Correcting the Equations
First, let's break down the applied force ($F = 75\text{ N}$) at $45^\circ$:



* Horizontal component ($F_x$): This creates the Normal Force ($F_N$) against the wall.
$$F_x = F \cos(45^\circ) = 75 \times 0.7071 \approx 53.03\text{ N}$$

* Vertical component ($F_y$): The upward part of the push.
$$F_y = F \sin(45^\circ) = 75 \times 0.7071 \approx 53.03\text{ N}$$


Next, calculate the maximum static friction force ($F_f$):



* Since $F_N = F_x$, then $F_f = \mu F_x$.
$$F_f = 0.30 \times 53.03 \approx 15.91\text{ N}$$


Solving for Mass
For the picture to stay still (equilibrium), the total upward force must equal the downward force (weight):
$$F_g = F_y + F_f$$
$$F_g = 53.03\text{ N} + 15.91\text{ N} = 68.94\text{ N}$$


Finally, convert the weight to mass (using $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$):
$$m = \frac{F_g}{g} = \frac{68.94}{9.8} \approx 7.03\text{ kg}$$



Comparison

* Your Result: $3.79\text{ kg}$ (Option C). This happened because you subtracted the friction from the vertical push.

* Correct Result: 7.0 kg (Option D). By adding the friction (because it helps hold the weight up), you find the picture can actually be much heavier and still stay on the wall.

You are absolutely correct that friction is helping hold the picture up. The error wasn't in your thinking, but in your arithmetic sign.


Think of the "Upward Team" vs. the "Downward Team":



* Downward Team: Gravity ($F_g$).

* Upward Team: The artist's vertical push ($F_y$) AND Friction ($F_f$).


To keep the picture from moving, the teams must be tied:
$$\text{Upward Team} = \text{Downward Team}$$
$$F_y + F_f = F_g$$


Where the mistake happened
In your handwritten notes, you wrote:
$$F_g = F_y - F_f$$


By using a minus sign, you mathematically said that friction is fighting against the artist (pulling the picture down). If you subtract friction from your push, you get a smaller total upward force, which results in the $3.8\text{ kg}$ answer.


However, since friction is helping you, you actually have more total upward force than just your arms alone are providing.


The "Minimum Force" Logic
If the artist was pushing with maximum force (pushing so hard the picture was about to slide up the wall), then friction would change sides and point down to oppose that upward motion. In that specific (and different) case, your subtraction would be correct.


But for minimum force, the picture wants to fall, so friction "grabs" the wall and pulls upward to help you.


$$F_y (53.03\text{ N}) + F_f (15.91\text{ N}) = 68.94\text{ N} \text{ (Total support)}$$


When you divide that total support by $9.8$, you get the $7.0\text{ kg}$ mass. Does it make more sense why those two forces should be added together rather than subtracted?

## Gemini

Absolutely. In fact, choosing P as your pivot is a smart move because it simplifies the math by "eliminating" the upward force from support P. Since the distance from the pivot to support P would be zero, the torque produced by that force is also zero.


To solve for the force at support Q, you just need to balance the torques.


1. Identify the Forces

* Weight of the beam ($F_g$): Since it's a "uniform beam," the weight acts exactly at the center.

* Center of the 8.0 m beam = 4.0 m from P.

* $F_g = m \times g = 25\text{ kg} \times 9.8\text{ m/s}^2 = 245\text{ N}$.




* Upward force from Q ($F_Q$): This acts at a distance of 6.0 m from P.



2. Set Up the Torque Equation
For the beam to be in equilibrium, the sum of the torques must be zero:
$$\sum \tau = 0$$


Using P as the pivot:



* Clockwise torque (from gravity): $\tau_g = F_g \times d_g = 245\text{ N} \times 4.0\text{ m}$

* Counter-clockwise torque (from Q): $\tau_Q = F_Q \times d_Q = F_Q \times 6.0\text{ m}$


3. Calculate
$$245 \times 4.0 = F_Q \times 6.0$$
$$980 = 6.0 \times F_Q$$
$$F_Q = \frac{980}{6.0} \approx 163.3\text{ N}$$


Once you have $F_Q$, you can easily find the force at $P$ by ensuring the total upward forces ($F_P + F_Q$) equal the total downward weight (245 N).
