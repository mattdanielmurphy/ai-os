## Gemini

It all comes down to density and buoyancy, but the secret behind this classic kitchen science experiment actually lies in the skins of the fruits.


For any object to float, it must be less dense than the liquid it is placed in. Water has a density of approximately $1\text{ g/cm}^3$. If an object's overall density is higher than that, it sinks; if it's lower, it floats.



The Floating Orange
Even though an orange is typically larger and heavier than a lime, it floats because it is wrapped in a thick, porous rind (the peel).



* The Rind: The peel of an orange is full of tiny, microscopic air pockets.

* The Life Jacket Effect: These trapped pockets of air act like a built-in life jacket. They increase the overall volume of the fruit significantly without adding much weight.

* The Result: This low-density exterior brings the average density of the entire orange down below $1\text{ g/cm}^3$, allowing it to bob to the surface.


If you peel the orange completely and drop it back into the water, it will sink. Without its air-pocket armor, the dense, water-heavy fruit flesh is heavier than the water it displaces.



The Sinking Lime
Limes look similar to oranges, but their physical makeup is quite different under the surface.



* Dense Rind and Flesh: A lime has a much thinner, tightly packed rind with very few air pockets. The flesh of the lime is also incredibly dense and packed tightly with juice.

* The Result: Because there is virtually no trapped air to offset the weight of its mass, the average density of a lime is slightly greater than $1\text{ g/cm}^3$.


Since the lime is denser than the water, it cannot generate enough buoyant force to stay afloat, causing it to drop straight to the bottom.
