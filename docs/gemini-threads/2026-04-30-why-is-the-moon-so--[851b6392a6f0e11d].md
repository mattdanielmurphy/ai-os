## Gemini

You're spot on—the dramatic shift in day length is a direct result of living "up north." Since it's late April, you are currently in the middle of the fastest gain of daylight for the entire year.


In Northern Alberta, we aren't quite at the Summer Solstice yet, but we are gaining roughly 3 to 4 minutes of daylight every single day. That adds up to nearly half an hour of extra light every week. This is why it feels like the "dark winter" vanished so quickly; the rate of change is at its peak between the spring equinox and the summer solstice.


The Tilt and the Latitude
The reason for this extreme swing is the axial tilt of the Earth.



* Winter: The Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the sun. At high latitudes, the sun barely peeks over the horizon, and for a very short time.

* Summer: We are tilted toward the sun. The sun's path across the sky is much higher and longer, leading to those 10:00 PM sunsets you'll see in a couple of months.

* The "North" Factor: The further you move from the equator, the more exaggerated this effect becomes. While someone in Florida barely notices the difference in day length between seasons, someone in Alberta sees the sun's "arc" shift significantly higher in the sky each week during the spring.



Why the Moon looks "High"
As for the moon, its height is actually governed by the Ecliptic—the imaginary path the sun follows through the sky.


There is an interesting "seesaw" relationship between the sun and a full (or near-full) moon. Because the moon is roughly opposite the sun during its fuller phases, its path in the sky often mimics the sun's path from the opposite season:



* In Winter: The sun is low, so the full moon rides very high in the sky.

* In Spring: Both the sun and the moon are reaching higher "altitudes" as we approach the summer months.


The moon’s orbit is also tilted about $5^{\circ}$ relative to Earth's orbit around the sun. This extra bit of tilt can sometimes "stack" with the Earth's own tilt, pushing the moon even higher in the sky than the sun ever reaches.


The Math of the Arc
If you want to estimate the maximum height (altitude) of the sun at your latitude ($L$), you can use this formula for the solstice:


$$\text{Max Altitude} = (90^{\circ} - L) + 23.5^{\circ}$$


At roughly 54°N, that puts the sun at about 59.5° high in the sky at noon in June—a massive difference from the measly 12.5° it reaches in late December. You're currently watching that transition happen in real-time.
