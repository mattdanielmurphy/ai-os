## Gemini

It is frustrating, but it largely comes down to DRM (Digital Rights Management) and Apple’s strict control over the hardware-to-software pipeline.


Essentially, streaming services like Netflix require very high levels of protection to allow 4K playback on computers. To prevent high-quality "ripping," they demand that the entire decryption chain—from the browser process to the graphics processor—be secure and verifiable.


Here is why Safari is currently the only one that pulls this off on macOS:



* FairPlay Streaming: Apple uses its own proprietary DRM system called FairPlay. Safari is the only browser on macOS that has the necessary "entitlements" to interface with Apple’s AVFoundation framework. This framework manages the secure, hardware-accelerated path required for 4K decryption.

* Hardware-Level Security: Because 4K streams often use advanced codecs (like AV1 or HEVC) and require tight security, the content must be decoded by specific blocks in the Apple Silicon "Media Engine." Third-party browsers like Chrome or Firefox run in "sandboxed" environments that, by design, do not have the same deep-level access to these hardware-accelerated DRM paths that Safari has.

* Compliance: From the perspective of studios and streaming services, if they can't guarantee that the decryption process is happening inside a secure, Apple-validated container, they simply block the 4K stream to avoid piracy risks.


While it feels like a browser limitation, it's actually an "architectural" one enforced by the studios. They prioritize preventing unauthorized screen recording or digital extraction over browser compatibility, and Apple limits this high-level security access to their own browser.

## Gemini

You aren't alone in finding this setup bizarre. It isn't just you; it is a point of constant frustration for Mac users.


To be precise: Netflix is not the only one, but it is one of the very few that bothers to go through the massive development effort required to make 4K work inside a macOS browser.


Here is the breakdown of why this feels so limited:


The "4K Browser" Landscape on macOS

* Apple TV App: Apple’s own app naturally supports 4K on macOS. If you have an Apple Silicon Mac, the Apple TV app is the "first-class" way to consume 4K content from Apple’s store or Apple TV+ subscriptions.

* Netflix: They have invested heavily in building a player that specifically hooks into Safari’s AVFoundation implementation for FairPlay DRM. They are one of the only third-party services that deemed it worth the engineering cost to maintain that specific integration.

* The Rest (Disney+, Max, Prime, etc.): Most of these services effectively "give up" on browsers for 4K. They prioritize simplicity. Because building a secure, hardware-accelerated 4K pipeline for one specific browser (Safari) is expensive and prone to breaking every time Apple updates macOS, most providers simply default to 1080p across all browsers on desktop—whether on Mac or Windows (where they often also restrict 4K to the native Windows app or Edge).


Why the others "don't bother"
It comes down to a Cost-to-Benefit analysis for their engineering teams:



* Audience Size: The vast majority of people watching 4K content are doing so on a Smart TV, a dedicated streaming stick (Apple TV 4K, Roku, Fire Stick), or a game console. These devices have locked-down, consistent hardware environments that are much easier to secure for DRM than a general-purpose computer running an open web browser.

* Maintenance: As mentioned, maintaining a 4K player for Safari is a moving target. If Apple changes a security entitlement in a macOS point release, the 4K stream breaks for all those users. It is cheaper for a company like Disney or HBO to just let everyone watch at 1080p on their computer and point them to their TV if they want 4K.

* Piracy Risk: Browser-based players are inherently easier to "hook" or screen-capture than a locked-down app environment. By restricting 4K to native apps or highly specific, Apple-approved environments, they significantly reduce the ease with which a 4K stream can be captured and pirated.


In short: You have correctly identified a landscape where Netflix is the outlier. Most other companies have decided that "4K on desktop browsers" is not a feature worth the headache of constant maintenance and potential security vulnerability.

YouTube doesn't require the same high-level, studio-mandated DRM (like FairPlay or Widevine L1) that Netflix and HBO/Disney do, because they aren't streaming premium, copyright-protected Hollywood features in the same way.

* The "Why" for the others: As touched on, for services like Max, Disney+, or Prime Video, it isn't a technical impossibility—it is a business and security choice. They have opted not to spend the engineering cycles required to maintain the specific DRM handshake with Safari's engine, preferring to keep their 4K catalog locked to platforms where they have absolute, audited control over the hardware: Smart TVs, streaming boxes, and game consoles.


If you ever find yourself needing to watch something like Disney+ or Max in 4K, you have to move away from your Mac entirely and onto a TV-connected device. There is no browser-based workaround for these services that will reliably trigger 4K on macOS, regardless of your hardware specs.


It is a fairly restrictive landscape, but it highlights exactly how much the studios dictate the terms of digital consumption on desktop computers.
