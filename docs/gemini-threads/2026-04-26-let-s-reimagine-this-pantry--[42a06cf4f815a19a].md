You’re spot on about the vertical space. Right now, those tall shelf gaps are essentially "dead air," which forces you to stack items on top of each other. That’s how things get buried and eventually forgotten until their expiration dates have passed.


Switching to a drawer-style system—specifically open-front bins or pull-out drawers—would turn this from a static storage area into a functional "library" of ingredients.


Here is how you could strategically restructure those specific sections:


1. Vertical Dividers & Half-Height Shelving
Since you have a lot of height, adding undershelf baskets or expandable shelf risers would double your surface area instantly.



* The Canned Goods (Mid-Right): Instead of the tiered wire rack you have now, a dedicated "can drawer" or a gravity-fed dispenser would allow you to see exactly how many tins of soup or beans remain without digging.

* The "Condiment Station" (Mid-Left): Those tall bottles of vinegar and oil are currently on a turntable, which is good, but a pull-out drawer here would prevent the "domino effect" when reaching for something in the center.


2. The "No-Front" Drawer System
To achieve that "grab and go" feel you mentioned, consider clear acrylic bins with handles. They act as drawers but give you 100% visibility.



* Bread & Grains: Group the bagels, bread, and wraps into one deep bin.

* Snack/Bar Section: The protein bars and Clif bars are currently taking up a lot of horizontal room. Standing them vertically in a narrow, deep drawer would reclaim about 40% of that shelf.

* Small Packets: Use a divided "stadium" drawer for the Stove Top stuffing and rice mixes so you can flip through them like records in a shop.

That is an incredible shot of a Cork-lid Trapdoor Spider (Cyclocosmia).


At first glance, it almost looks like an ancient coin or a carved seal, but that disc is actually the spider's highly specialized abdomen. This isn't just for show—it’s a fascinating biological defense mechanism.


The Science of the "Shield"
The technical term for this anatomical feature is phragmosis. Here is how it works:



* Living Doorway: These spiders live in vertical burrows. When a predator (like a parasitic wasp or a centipede) tries to enter, the spider heads in first and uses its hardened, truncated abdomen to perfectly "plug" the tunnel.

* The Pattern: The intricate, symmetrical design isn't just aesthetic; the ridges and furrows help the abdomen wedge tightly against the burrow walls, making it nearly impossible for a predator to grip or bypass.

* Material: The "disk" is significantly tougher than the rest of its body, acting like a biological shield or a manhole cover.


Distribution and Behavior

* Habitat: Different species of Cyclocosmia are found in parts of Southeast Asia, Mexico, and the Southeastern United States.

* Lifestyle: They are notoriously difficult to find because they spend almost their entire lives underground. They are sit-and-wait predators, lunging out of their trapdoors only when they feel the vibrations of passing insects.


It's a prime example of how evolution can produce something that looks like it was designed by an architect.

## Gemini

While they certainly look the part, it is statistically more likely you’re looking at a False Black Widow (Steatoda grossa).


In Alberta, true Black Widows are native to the southern parts of the province (around Lethbridge and Medicine Hat). Sightings as far north as Fort Saskatchewan or Edmonton are quite rare and usually involve "hitchhikers" brought in on grapes, plants, or moving boxes.


Quick Identification Check
To tell them apart, check the underside and the coloring:



* True Black Widow: Deep, glossy jet-black with a vivid red or orange hourglass on the belly.

* False Black Widow: Dark chocolate brown or purplish-black (it can look black in dim light). They do not have the red hourglass, though they might have faint pale markings on the top of their abdomen.

* Boreal Combfoot: This is another common Alberta lookalike. They are dark and bulbous but often have a light-colored line around the front of the abdomen.


What to do if you're unsure

* Don't panic: Neither spider is aggressive; they generally only bite if they are being squished or poked.

* Relocate it: If it's indoors and you’re uncomfortable, you can trap it in a glass and move it outside.

* Check the web: Both build messy, tangled "cobwebs" rather than the neat, circular ones you see in cartoons.


If you happen to see a clear red hourglass on the belly, it’s worth being extra cautious. While their venom is potent, they rarely inject a full dose, and serious complications are rare in healthy adults—though you should seek medical attention if a bite occurs.
