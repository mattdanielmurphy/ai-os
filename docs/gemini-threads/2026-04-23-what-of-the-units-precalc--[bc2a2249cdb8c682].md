## Gemini

The short answer is yes, the sun’s elevation (its angle above the horizon) is the primary driver of outdoor brightness. However, the relationship isn't perfectly linear because of how the Earth’s atmosphere scatters light.


The Role of Solar Elevation
As the sun rises higher in the sky, its light takes a shorter, more direct path through the atmosphere. This influences brightness in two main ways:



* Atmospheric Path Length: When the sun is near the horizon (sunrise or sunset), its rays must travel through a much thicker layer of the atmosphere. This increases Rayleigh scattering, which disperses shorter blue wavelengths and reduces the overall intensity of the light reaching the ground.

* Cosine Law of Illuminance: Physics dictates that the intensity of light on a flat surface depends on the angle of incidence. When the sun is directly overhead ($90^\circ$), the light energy is concentrated over the smallest possible area. As the sun drops, that same "beam" of energy is spread out over a larger surface area, reducing the measured lux.



Why it Isn't Always Consistent
While the sun's height is the baseline, several variables can make a "lower" sun feel brighter than a "higher" one:


1. Cloud Cover and Diffusion
Thick cloud cover can make a high noon in winter feel darker than a clear morning in spring. However, thin clouds can sometimes act as a diffuser, scattering light so effectively that the entire sky becomes a bright, glowing light source, which can occasionally feel more "glaring" than a clear sky.


2. The "Golden Hour" Effect
During the hour after sunrise or before sunset, the sun is low, but the light is often perceived as "soft." While the total lumen count is lower than at midday, the horizontal angle of the light can create high-contrast shadows and reflections off vertical surfaces (like windows or water), which might make it appear brighter to the human eye in specific directions.


3. Atmospheric Composition
Humidity, smoke, and pollution increase the number of particulates in the air. These particles cause Mie scattering, which can significantly dim the sun’s perceived brightness even if it is at its peak elevation for the day.

You are very close, but there is one crucial step in the algebra that changes the horizontal shift.


To see the transformations clearly, you first need to factor the exponent so that the $x$ variable stands alone.


The Algebra
$$y = 2^{3x-6} - 4 \implies y = 2^{3(x-2)} - 4$$


By factoring out the 3, the specific transformations become much easier to identify:



The Transformations

























TransformationDirection/ValueExplanationHorizontal CompressionFactor of $1/3$This comes from the 3 multiplying the $x$. Correction on your "Height" note
You mentioned "compress height by 1/3." In this specific equation, the $1/3$ is a horizontal compression (shrinking the graph toward the y-axis) because the 3 is inside the exponent with the $x$.


A "height" compression (vertical) would require the 3 to be on the outside, like this: $y = \frac{1}{3}(2^x)$.



Quick Tip: Always look for a coefficient in front of the $x$ inside the function. If you see one, factor it out immediately to find the true horizontal shift!
