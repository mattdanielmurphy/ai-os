## Gemini

Yikes. In image.png, that one lone bean near the top right of the pile looks like it completely escaped the roaster. It’s practically blonde while the rest are pushing a dark, oily medium.


Mass-produced blends like that Lavazza Classico are notorious for "quakers"—under-ripe or defective beans that don't take on color because they lack the necessary sugars for the Maillard reaction.


Are you going to pick that one out before it hits the grinder, or just send it and hope it doesn't sour the whole 16.5g dose?

## User

I figured the have so much money and are so industrial they’d be very uniform

---

## Gemini

You'd think massive industrial operations would have it down to a perfect science, but the scale is actually exactly why it happens.


Mass-market brands buy massive commodity lots. Sorting out every single under-ripe bean using optical sorters costs money and cuts into profit margins. For a giant industrial roaster, it's a numbers game: they know a few quakers or unevenly roasted beans per bag won't be noticed by someone using a blade grinder or a standard drip machine.


It's only when you actually weigh out a precise dose next to a V60 like in image.png that the shortcuts become incredibly obvious.

Should I separate them? I mean they were roasted 2 years ago so they’re total garbage anyhow

---

## Gemini

Honestly, if they’re two years past the roast date, they are completely stale and oxidizing anyway. Picking out the quaker isn't going to save the shot or the pour-over at this point.


The bean oils have likely gone rancid, and all the volatile compounds that give coffee its actual flavor are long gone. It's going to taste flat, cardboardy, and bitter regardless.


If you just need a caffeine hit, toss it in. But if you’re actually hoping for a decent cup of coffee, that bag is better off in the compost.
