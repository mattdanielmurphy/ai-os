## Gemini

Yes, a photon is a particle, but with a major quantum mechanics twist: it is both a particle and a wave. This concept is known as wave-particle duality.


To understand how a photon acts as a particle, it helps to look at what makes it unique compared to the particles we encounter in everyday life.



How a Photon Acts Like a Particle
In classical physics, we think of a wave (like a ripple in water) as a continuous, smooth spread of energy. A particle, on the other hand, is a localized "packet" of matter.


A photon behaves like a particle because its energy is quantized. This means light cannot be divided into infinitely smaller amounts; it is delivered in distinct, indivisible packets (or "quanta").



* Collisions: When light hits an object, photons collide with atoms like miniature billiard balls. They can bounce off things or be absorbed entirely by a single electron.

* Momentum: Even though photons have zero rest mass, they still carry momentum. When a photon strikes a surface, it exerts a microscopic physical push on that object, known as radiation pressure.



The Twist: Wave-Particle Duality
While a photon acts like a particle during interactions (like hitting a detector or absorbing into an electron), it travels through space acting like a wave.


If you shoot a stream of photons through two slits side-by-side (the famous Double-Slit Experiment), the photons don't just pile up in two neat lines behind the slits like tennis balls would. Instead, they interfere with each other, creating a wavy pattern of alternating light and dark bands on the back wall. Remarkably, this happens even if you fire the photons one at a time. Each individual photon travels through both slits as a wave of probability, interfering with itself, before snapping into a solid, localized particle the exact microsecond it hits the wall.



Summary: What is a Photon?
In modern physics, a photon is classified as an elementary particle. It is the fundamental quantum (the smallest possible piece) of the electromagnetic field.


Think of it this way:



* If you want to know how light travels, treat it like a wave.

* If you want to know how light exchanges energy with matter (like in solar panels, digital cameras, or chemical reactions), treat it like a particle.
