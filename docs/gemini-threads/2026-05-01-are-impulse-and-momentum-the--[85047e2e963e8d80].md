# are impulse and momentum the same? Same units?

> [!NOTE]
> Archived Gemini Thread: [are impulse and momentum the same? Same units?]() | Date: 2026-08-13

## User

are impulse and momentum the same? Same units?

---

## Gemini

They are not the same concept, but they are directly linked by the Impulse-Momentum Theorem.


The Difference

* Momentum ($p$): This is a measure of an object's "motion." It tells you how much force would be required to bring an object to rest. It is calculated as $p = mv$ (mass $\times$ velocity).

* Impulse ($J$): This is the change in momentum caused by an external force over a period of time. It is what happens to an object when a force is applied to it. It is calculated as $J = F\Delta t$ or $J = \Delta p$.


Think of it this way: Momentum is the state an object is in; impulse is what you do to change that state.


The Units
Yes, they share the exact same units.


Even though their formulas look different—one uses mass and velocity ($kg \cdot m/s$), and the other uses force and time ($N \cdot s$)—they are dimensionally equivalent:


$$1 \text{ N} \cdot \text{s} = 1 \left( \frac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \right) \cdot \text{s} = 1 \text{ kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$$


Because impulse is defined as the change in momentum ($\Delta p = J$), it makes sense that they must share the same units.


Since you are working through these problems, keep this distinction in mind: when you are calculating a "change" in motion over time, you are finding Impulse; when you are calculating "amount" of motion, you are finding Momentum.

so can I always write Ns or should I write kg m/s when I'm finding momentum?

## Gemini

Technically, both units represent the same thing, but in physics, it is standard practice to use specific units based on the quantity you are calculating to help keep your work clear.


The Standard Practice

* 
For Momentum ($p$): Use $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$.
Since momentum is defined as $p = mv$ (mass $\times$ velocity), using these base units makes it immediately obvious to someone looking at your work exactly how you calculated the value.




* 
For Impulse ($J$): Use $\text{N} \cdot \text{s}$.
Since impulse is defined as $J = F \Delta t$ (force $\times$ time), using Newton-seconds emphasizes that this is an effect caused by a force acting over time.





Why this matters
Even though the math proves that $1 \text{ N} \cdot \text{s} = 1 \text{ kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$, instructors often look for the unit that corresponds to the definition of the variable you are solving for.


Using the "context-appropriate" unit is a great habit because:



* It shows conceptual understanding: It tells your instructor you know the origin of the value (e.g., "I calculated this from Force and time" vs "I calculated this from mass and velocity").

* It avoids confusion: If you are doing a complex problem involving both energy and momentum, keeping your units distinct ($J$ for Joules, $\text{N} \cdot \text{s}$ for Impulse, $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ for Momentum) makes it much easier to spot errors in your calculations.


Summary: Stick to $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$ for momentum and $\text{N} \cdot \text{s}$ for impulse. You won't be "wrong" if you swap them, but following the convention is much clearer for anyone checking your physics work.

## User

change in momentum means impulse?

---

## Gemini

Yes, exactly. The Impulse-Momentum Theorem explicitly states that the impulse applied to an object is equal to the change in its momentum.


Mathematically, this is expressed as:


$$J = \Delta p$$


Where:



* $J$ is the impulse ($\text{N} \cdot \text{s}$)

* $\Delta p$ is the change in momentum ($m\Delta v$ or $\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}$)


Because they are equivalent, you can calculate impulse by finding the change in momentum, or calculate the change in momentum by finding the impulse (Force $\times$ time). They are two different ways of looking at the exact same physical event.
