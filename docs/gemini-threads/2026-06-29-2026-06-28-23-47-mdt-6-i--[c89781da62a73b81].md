## Gemini

The short answer is yes, theoretically. With controlled environment agriculture (CEA)—which includes advanced LEDs, precise climate control, and tailored automated nutrient systems—you can simulate practically any climate indoors.


You can absolutely grow highly reliable, spectacular-tasting strawberries at home, but doing so comes with a literal price.



The Pursuit of the "Perfect" Berry
In an indoor setup, you have total control over the variables that dictate flavor:



* The Brix Level (Sweetness): By dropping nighttime temperatures and tweaking the potassium and phosphorus ratios in the water right before harvest, you can force the plant to pack more sugars into the fruit.

* Consistency: Because there are no heatwaves, unexpected frosts, or heavy rains to wash out the flavor, every batch can taste exactly like the last.

* Ripeness: Grocery store strawberries are bred for shelf life and transport armor; they are often picked underripe. At home, you can harvest them at absolute peak maturity.



The Cost Reality: How Much More?
Your assumption is spot on: recreating nature inside a room is incredibly energy-intensive.


While a commercial open-field farmer gets free sunlight and rainwater, an indoor grower must pay for every single photon and drop of climate control.


The Breakdown

* Energy Demands: Strawberries are notorious "light hogs" compared to leafy greens. They require high-intensity light for 14 to 16 hours a day to produce fruit. Data from indoor farming sectors shows that strawberries can require around 117 kWh of electricity per month for every square meter of growing space just to cover the lights and the HVAC needed to clear out the heat and humidity they generate.

* The Premium: If you account for the initial setup equipment (tents, fans, hydroponic basins, high-end LEDs) and ongoing electricity costs, growing strawberries at home on a small scale typically costs 3x to 5x more per pound than buying standard berries at a traditional grocery store.


If you are comparing them to high-end, artisanal, or imported gourmet berries (which can run $15 to $20 for a small box), home-grown hydroponics can actually start to look somewhat cost-competitive over time. But compared to a standard $4 plastic clamshell from the supermarket, it is an expensive hobby.

## Gemini

Yes, absolutely. Shifting to the outdoors while controlling the environment is exactly how commercial growers balance the physics of biology with the realities of utility bills.


What you are describing is a high-tech greenhouse or, in extreme climates, a passive solar greenhouse. By using the sun for your primary light source, you immediately cut out the largest electricity hog of indoor farming: the artificial photons.


However, "climate-controlling the outdoors" introduces a massive new battle: thermal insulation. Instead of paying to create light, you are now paying to fight the outside temperature.



1. What is actually viable to grow completely indoors?
If you are sticking to a fully enclosed, artificially lit room, the rule of thumb is simple: if you eat the leaves or the roots before it flowers, it is highly viable.



* Microgreens & Leafy Greens: Lettuce, spinach, kale, and arugula have a tiny footprint, grow incredibly fast (20–30 days), and require much less light intensity than fruit-bearing plants.

* Gourmet Mushrooms: Oyster, Shiitake, and Lion's Mane mushrooms don't need any light. They just need specific humidity and temperature, making them incredibly energy-efficient and highly cost-competitive with grocery stores.

* Hardy Herbs: Basil, cilantro, mint, and rosemary do exceptionally well. Because fresh herbs are sold at a massive premium in grocery stores for tiny plastic clamshells, growing them indoors actually saves you money quite quickly.


Once a plant has to grow a flower, get pollinated, and turn that flower into a heavy, sugary fruit (like tomatoes, peppers, or strawberries), the indoor energy costs skyrocket.



2. The Hybrid Approach: Climate-Controlling the Outdoors
Moving your setup to a greenhouse to capture natural sunlight changes the math dramatically. But your geographic reality dictates exactly how much energy you save.


If you are trying to run a standard glass or plastic greenhouse during a harsh northern winter, a traditional heating system (like natural gas or electric heaters) trying to fight sub-zero temperatures can cost thousands of dollars a month because glass has almost zero insulation value.


To make it viable, you have to use Passive Solar and Geothermal Earth-Sheltered engineering:



* The Chinese Solar Greenhouse Model: Instead of four glass walls, these structures have one transparent south-facing wall to let the sun in. The north, east, and west walls are thick, heavily insulated dirt mounds or concrete barriers that trap heat.

* Thermal Mass (The "Sand Battery" or Water Barrels): You fill the inside with massive barrels of water or a sub-floor rock/sand matrix. During the day, the intense sunlight heats up this mass. At night, when it drops to freezing outside, that mass slowly radiates the trapped heat back into the space, keeping it warm for pennies.

* GAHT (Ground to Air Heat Exchangers): You use a simple fan to push hot air from the top of the greenhouse through pipes buried eight feet underground. The earth stays at a stable temperature year-round (around 5–7°C or 41–45°F). In the winter, the earth warms the air; in the summer, it cools it.


What becomes viable this way?
If you build a highly insulated passive solar greenhouse, you can reliably grow tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, and yes, perfect strawberries, all year round while keeping your supplemental energy footprint surprisingly low. In fact, some growers in deep winter zones use these setups to grow figs, grapes, and even citrus fruits mid-winter using only a tiny fraction of the energy a fully indoor grow room would pull.

## Gemini

In Logic Pro, to apply a single MIDI plugin (like an arpeggiator, chord trigger, or third-party MIDI effect) to multiple tracks simultaneously, you need to use a Summing Stack—but with a specific routing tweak.


By default, MIDI plugins only process MIDI data on the track they are directly loaded onto. Here is how to set it up so one MIDI plugin controls the whole stack:


Step 1: Create a Summing Stack

* Select all the instrument tracks you want to include in the stack.

* Right-click (or Ctrl + click) on one of the selected tracks and choose Create Track Stack... (or press Cmd + Shift + D).

* Select Summing Stack and click Create.



Note: Do not choose Folder Stack, as it doesn't allow for flexible routing and plugin hosting on the main bus.




Step 2: Convert the Main Track to an Instrument Track
By default, the main "Summing Stack" track is just an audio aux channel, which means it cannot host MIDI plugins. We need to turn it into a software instrument track.



* Open the Mixer (X).

* Find the master track for your new stack (it will have a disclosure triangle next to it).

* Right-click the channel strip for that main stack track and select Create Track (or select it and press Ctrl + T). This forces it to appear in your main Tracks tracklist.

* Go back to your main Tracks area, select that main stack track, and look at the Inspector on the left (I).

* Change the channel strip type from an Aux to a Software Instrument.

* In the instrument slot, load External Instrument (found under Utility > External Instrument). Set both the MIDI Destination and Audio Input in that utility plugin to None. This turns the track into a "dummy" instrument that can pass MIDI.



Step 3: Route the MIDI and Audio
Now you need to make sure the sub-tracks listen to the main track's MIDI, while still routing their audio correctly.



* Select all the sub-tracks inside the stack.

* In the Inspector, change their MIDI Input to match the track number or name of your Main Stack track, or set them to sequential MIDI channels if you want to split them. Alternatively, you can simply record-arm all sub-tracks and play through the main track if your preferences allow it.

* Load your desired MIDI plugin into the MIDI FX slot of the Main Stack track.


When you play or write MIDI notes onto the Main Stack track, the MIDI plugin will process the notes first, and then distribute that modified MIDI data down to all the instruments living inside the stack.

## Gemini

You are completely right—my bad. You cannot change an Aux track into a Software Instrument track from the Inspector like that. Logic doesn't allow changing channel strip types on the fly.


To get a single MIDI plugin to feed multiple instruments simultaneously, you have to route the MIDI out of a "master" track and back into your instrument tracks. The cleanest way to do this without messing with Logic's legacy Environment window is using the Mac's built-in IAC Driver.


Here is how to set it up:


Step 1: Turn on the IAC Driver (One-time Mac setup)

* Press Cmd + Space to open Spotlight, type Audio MIDI Setup, and open it.

* In the top menu bar of Audio MIDI Setup, click Window > Show MIDI Studio.

* Look for the IAC Driver icon (it looks like a red or grey square). Double-click it.

* Check the box that says Device is online, then click Apply and close the app.



Step 2: Create the Master MIDI Track

* Back in Logic, create a brand new, normal Software Instrument track. Place it right above your other instruments.

* In the MIDI FX slot of this new track, load the plugin you want to use (e.g., Scripter, Arpeggiator, or Chord Trigger).

* In the Instrument slot (where you would normally load a synth), go down to Utility > External Instrument.

* Open the External Instrument plugin interface and set the MIDI Destination to IAC Driver Bus 1. Leave Audio Input as None.



Step 3: Route your Instruments to listen to the IAC Driver
Now you just need to tell your actual instrument tracks (like your Classic Electric Piano) to listen to that specific MIDI stream.



* Select your first instrument track.

* Look at your Inspector on the left under the Track disclosure arrow (where it currently says MIDI Input: All in your screenshot).

* Change MIDI Input from All to IAC Driver > Bus 1.

* Repeat this for any other instrument tracks you want to control.

* Turn on Input Monitoring (the I button next to record arm) on all of those destination instrument tracks so they live-receive the incoming data.


Now, select your Master MIDI track and play your keyboard. The MIDI notes will pass through your MIDI plugin, travel through the virtual IAC bus, and trigger all your targeted instruments perfectly in sync.
