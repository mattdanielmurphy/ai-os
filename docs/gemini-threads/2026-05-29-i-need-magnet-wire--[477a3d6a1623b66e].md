# I need magnet wire?

## User

I need magnet wire?

---

## Gemini

Yes, you definitely need magnet wire for this project.


Standard hookup wire or jumper wires from a basic electronics kit are usually insulated with a thick layer of flexible plastic or rubber. If you tried to use that, the coil would end up far too heavy and bulky to spin freely on a paperclip stand.


Magnet wire is different because it is coated in an incredibly thin, painted-on layer of enamel insulation. This allows you to wrap the wire tightly into a lightweight coil so that the magnetic fields can interact efficiently, while still preventing the current from short-circuiting across the loops.


You can usually find a small spool of it (22 to 28 AWG thickness works best) at local electronics hobby shops, hardware stores, or online.


Once you track some down, do you want a quick tip on the best way to scrape that thin enamel off the ends to make your switch?

## User

what about just stripped wire?

---

## Gemini

Using completely stripped wire (wire with no insulation at all) is actually a trap for this specific project. It's one of those things that feels like it should work, but physics will get in the way.


If you use entirely bare wire for the loop, two things will happen that will cause you to lose points on the rubric:



* The Short-Circuit (Safety Issue): When you wind completely bare wire into a coil, the individual turns of the loop will touch each other. Electricity always takes the path of least resistance, so instead of flowing around the loop 10 or 15 times to build up a strong magnetic field, the current will just jump straight across the bundle from one side to the other. This creates a massive short circuit, which will drain your battery instantly and cause the wire to get incredibly hot. This directly violates the assignment's safety warning about keeping the battery shorted.

* The "Stuck" Motor: Even if you managed to perfectly space out the bare wires so they didn't touch, a motor needs an automated switch to keep spinning. If the coil is magnetized 100% of the time, it will just turn until it aligns with the permanent magnet underneath, and then it will stop and lock into place. It needs that "on/off" pulse provided by the half-stripped insulation to let momentum carry it around.


The Cheat Code: The Sharpie Trick
If you don't want to buy magnet wire, or if you find scraping the tiny insulation off too annoying, you can actually start with entirely bare copper wire—but you have to create your own insulation.



* Take your bare wire and wrap it around the marker to make the coil, ensuring you leave a tiny gap between each loop so they do not touch each other.

* Take a standard black Sharpie or permanent marker and heavily color in one half of one of the straight axle ends.


The dried ink from a permanent marker acts as a surprisingly good electrical insulator. When the coil spins, the paperclip will touch the bare metal side (turning the motor on), and then hit the inked side (breaking the circuit and turning it off).


Otherwise, sticking to standard magnet wire and scraping just one side is your safest bet to ensure it hits the "It works" criterion!

## User

one half of the straight axle ends? what does that mean?

---

## Gemini

Think of the straight axle ends as short, round matchsticks sticking out of the sides of your coil.


When you look at a round wire, it has a top half and a bottom half (like the earth has a northern and southern hemisphere).


For one of those axle ends, you want to leave the insulation on the bottom half, but scrape it off the top half.


Here is exactly how to do it:



* Lay the coil flat on a table.

* Take your sandpaper or knife and scrape the enamel off the exposed top surface of the wire axle.

* Do not flip the coil over. Leave the side touching the table completely untouched.


By doing this, you've created a half-insulated axle. As the motor spins, the bare copper half will touch the paperclip support and let electricity flow through, kicking the electromagnet on. As it continues to roll over, the insulated half will hit the paperclip, cutting the power. This allows momentum to carry the loop around until it hits the bare side again, creating a continuous spinning loop!

## Gemini

Yes, you want to use relatively thin wire for this, but not microscopically thin.


If the wire is too thick, the coil will be too heavy for the tiny magnetic force to lift and spin. If it is too thin, it becomes incredibly fragile, easily tangles, and won't hold its circular shape when spinning.


The sweet spot for this project is 22 AWG to 26 AWG (American Wire Gauge). In the wire world, the higher the number, the thinner the wire.


How Much Wire Do You Need?
You do not need a massive spool—about 2 to 3 feet (roughly 60 to 90 cm) of wire total is plenty for one motor.


Here is how that breaks down:



* The Coil: Wrapping the wire around a marker or a D-battery 10 to 15 times will take about 12 to 18 inches of wire.

* The Axles: You need to leave about 2 inches of straight wire sticking out of each side to act as the balance arms.

* The Securing Loops: You will use a few inches to wrap around the coil itself to bind it together.


Since bare wire can be tricky to wrap perfectly without the loops accidentally touching and shorting out, having a little extra wire on hand is great in case you need to scrap your first attempt and try winding a second neat coil.

what's the guage of the inside wire in ethernet cables?

---

## Gemini

The wire gauge inside an Ethernet cable depends heavily on the specific "Category" of the cable (like Cat5e vs. Cat6), but it almost always falls between 22 AWG and 28 AWG.


Because American Wire Gauge (AWG) uses an inverse scale, remember that lower numbers mean thicker wires.


Here is how the gauges break down across the most common types of Ethernet cables:


Standard Ethernet Gauges

* 24 AWG (0.51 mm): This is the long-standing "workhorse" size. It is the universal standard for Cat5e and is also frequently used in standard Cat6 cables.

* 23 AWG (0.57 mm): This is slightly thicker and is the standard for high-quality Cat6 and Cat6A cables. The extra thickness helps reduce signal loss and handles the higher frequencies needed for 10 Gbps speeds.

* 22 AWG (0.64 mm): The thickest common size, usually reserved for Cat8 or industrial-grade Cat6A cables designed for heavy-duty Power over Ethernet (PoE) installations.


Specialty Slim Gauges

* 26 AWG or 28 AWG (0.40 mm to 0.32 mm): If you buy those ultra-flexible, "slim" or "thin" patch cables meant for tight spaces or crowded server racks, they use these much thinner wires. They are incredibly easy to route, but because they are so thin, they aren't meant for long distances (usually capped around 15 to 30 meters).



Tie-in for your Motor Project
Since you are tearing down or looking for wire to build your project, an old Cat5e or Cat6 cable is actually a legendary goldmine for DIY electronics.


If you cut one open, you will find four twisted pairs (8 individual wires total) of 23 or 24 AWG solid copper wire. That is the absolute perfect thickness for making the spinning loop of your motor. Just keep in mind that those internal wires have a layer of colored plastic insulation on them, so you will need to completely strip the plastic off the main loop body before trying the Sharpie trick!

power source is 6 AA batteries in series. That'll work? Do I need a resistor?

---

## Gemini

Six AA batteries connected in series will output 9 volts ($6 \times 1.5\text{V}$).


Looking at your assignment rubric, this safely stays under the maximum limit of 12 volts. However, because you are using a completely bare copper wire coil with the Sharpie trick, yes, you will absolutely want to use a resistor.


Without a resistor, 9 volts hooked up to a bare wire coil creates a massive short circuit. The wire will get incredibly hot almost instantly, it might melt the Sharpie ink right off the axle, and it will drain your batteries in minutes. This would violate your assignment's safety warning about shorting the battery.



What Resistor to Use
You want a resistor that limits the current enough to keep things safe and cool, but still allows enough juice through to create a magnetic field strong enough to spin the coil.



* The Sweet Spot: A $10\text{ }\Omega$ to $47\text{ }\Omega$ (ohm) resistor rated for at least 1 or 2 watts is ideal.

* If you only have standard 1/4-watt resistors (the tiny ones usually found in basic electronics kits): Try a $100\text{ }\Omega$ resistor.



⚠️ Note: Standard 1/4-watt kit resistors will get very hot if the motor stalls. If the motor isn't spinning, unhook the battery immediately to keep from burning out the resistor or violating the short-circuit safety rule.



How to Hook It Up on Your Breadboard
Because you are using a breadboard, adding the resistor is incredibly easy. You just use it to bridge the gap between your power rail and your paperclip stand:



* Connect the Positive (+) lead of your 6-AA battery pack to the positive power rail of your breadboard.

* Connect the Negative (-) lead to the negative power rail.

* Plug one end of your resistor into the positive power rail.

* Plug the other end of the resistor into the exact same row where your left paperclip is inserted.

* Use a standard jumper wire to connect the row of your right paperclip straight back to the negative power rail.


This forces the 9-volt current to pass safely through the resistor before it ever hits your homemade motor coil!

3. The Scissors Pivot
If you only have scissors, you can still make it work with a slight adjustment to your technique.



* Open the scissors and place the wire near the hinge (where you have the most control).

## Gemini

Boiling water won't quite get hot enough. The plastic insulation inside an Ethernet cable is usually made of polyethylene or PVC, which requires temperatures well over 150°C to actually melt and slide off. Boiling water caps out at 100°C, so it will just make the plastic slightly soft and gummy without actually freeing the copper.


If you have a full 3-foot stretch to strip and don't want to do it inch-by-inch with a nail clipper, here are two faster bulk methods:


1. The "Zipline" Razor Slice (Fastest)
Instead of trying to pull the insulation off like a sleeve, you want to slice it open down the middle like a hot dog bun.



* Tape one end of your 3-foot wire firmly to a table, or wedge it under something heavy so you can pull against it.

* Hold the other end of the wire tight with one hand so the wire is completely straight and taut.

* Take a utility knife, box cutter, or a sharp pocket knife in your other hand.

* Hold the blade at a shallow angle against the wire and slice down the length of the cable. If you keep the wire taut, the blade will unzip the plastic jacket effortlessly in one or two long passes. Then you just peel the copper out.


2. The Wire-Stripping Straight-Edge
If you have a sturdy pair of scissors or a craft knife but don't want to risk cutting the wire:



* Close your scissors or knife blade onto a cutting board, leaving a microscopic gap just wide enough for the bare copper, but small enough to catch the plastic.

* Step on one end of the plastic insulation on the floor to hold it still.

* Hold the scissor blades firmly in place on the board and pull the wire through the gap toward you. The blades will act like a plow, stripping the plastic off in a long ribbon as you pull.


Once you get that long strand freed up, you are ready to start winding! Just remember to wrap it around something smooth (like a marker) neatly so the bare loops don't overlap haphazardly and ruin your spacing. Have you decided which object you're going to use as your winding guide?
