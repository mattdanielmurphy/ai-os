## Gemini

It is incredibly frustrating when "sandboxing" a new account doesn't actually give you the clean slate you expect. Modern macOS has moved a lot of "login" logic away from the user-facing list and into background services that treat the entire hardware as one big playground.


If you’re seeing clutter from your main apps (like Adobe Creative Cloud, Steam, or various dev tools) bleeding into your fresh account, it’s because their background agents are sitting in the global /Library folders.


Why this happens
Apps increasingly use SMAppService or LaunchAgents that are registered at the system level. When you log in to any account, launchd looks at /Library/LaunchAgents and says, "Well, I'm supposed to start this for whoever just logged in," regardless of whether that user actually wants it.


A few ways to force a "clean" environment:

* The "Allow in Background" Toggles: Since you are on a modern version of macOS, check the list under System Settings > General > Login Items > Allow in Background. While many of these are global, toggling them off in the alternate account should (in theory) only affect that user's session for certain types of modern apps. However, it's inconsistent.

* The Nuclear Option (Manual Scripting): If you really want that account to be clean, you can write a shell script for the alternate account that runs at login and calls launchctl unload or launchctl bootout on specific services you don't want. This effectively "kills" them only for that session, leaving your main account untouched when you switch back.

* Managed User / Parental Controls: Sometimes setting an account up as a "Standard" user with restricted app access can prevent some background agents from even having the permission to spin up, though this can be a headache for a dev environment.


It definitely ruins the "fresh start" feeling when the first thing you see in a new account is a helper tool for an app you haven't even opened yet.


Are you seeing specific persistent icons in the menu bar, or is it mostly background processes showing up in Activity Monitor?

## Gemini

It is a wild disconnect, isn't it? Venom is the poster child for a "critic-proof" movie. While the critical response was pretty brutal—sitting at a 31% on Rotten Tomatoes—the box office numbers told a completely different story.


There are a few specific reasons why this one managed to defy the reviews so aggressively:


1. The "Tom Hardy" Factor
Even the critics who hated the movie generally agreed that Tom Hardy was doing something fascinating. His performance was described as "wicked fun" and a "stumblebum Method goof." He played the relationship between Eddie Brock and the symbiote like a chaotic buddy-comedy, which resonated with audiences much more than the dark, gritty tone the trailers originally suggested.


2. Global Domination (Specifically China)
The movie was an absolute juggernaut internationally.



* China: It pulled in over $262 million in China alone.

* Market Fit: Critics found the tone "incoherent," but many viewers found that exact same weirdness to be "quirky" and "refreshing" compared to the more polished, formulaic Marvel Cinematic Universe (MCU) films.


3. The "SymBrock" Phenomenon
An unexpected driver for the film's success was the internet's obsession with the relationship between Eddie and Venom. Fans began "shipping" the two characters (dubbed SymBrock), which generated massive social media engagement. Sony even leaned into this for the home media release, marketing the film as a romantic comedy.


4. Comparison to the Past
For many fans, this version was simply a massive upgrade over the Spider-Man 3 version of the character. Even if the script was "formulaic," seeing a comic-accurate, 7-foot-tall Venom biting heads off was enough to satisfy the itch that the 2007 version failed to scratch.


It ended up as the seventh-highest-grossing film of 2018, proving once again that sometimes a "messy" movie is exactly what the public wants to spend their Friday night watching.
