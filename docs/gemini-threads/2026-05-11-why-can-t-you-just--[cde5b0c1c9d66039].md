## Gemini

It comes down to a mix of public safety, environmental protection, and strict waste management laws. While a single needle might seem insignificant, the "sharps" designation exists for several specific reasons:


1. Risk of Needlestick Injuries
Even if you are the only person using the medication, once a needle enters your household trash, it passes through many hands. Sanitation workers, janitors, and processing plant staff are at high risk of accidental "needlesticks" when bags tear or compactors crush waste. These injuries can transmit bloodborne pathogens (like Hepatitis or HIV) or cause serious infections.


2. Community Safety
Loose needles in a standard garbage bin can easily fall out or poke through the plastic. This creates a hazard for children, pets, or wildlife that might encounter the trash. A dedicated sharps bin is puncture-resistant and designed to lock, ensuring the needle is never exposed again once it’s dropped inside.


3. Legal and Environmental Regulations
In many regions, including Alberta, it is actually illegal to dispose of sharps in the regular garbage or recycling. Biohazardous waste requires a different processing stream than standard landfill waste. Sharps bins are typically incinerated at high temperatures to completely destroy both the metal and any biological material.


4. Proper Disposal in Your Area
Since you are in Alberta, you have a straightforward way to handle this:



* Pharmacy Programs: Most pharmacies in the province will provide you with a yellow sharps container for free.

* The Exchange: Once the bin is full, you bring it back to the pharmacy. They will take the full one for proper biohazardous disposal and give you a fresh, empty one.

* Provisional Bins: If you don't have a yellow bin yet, you can use a thick, puncture-resistant plastic container (like a laundry detergent bottle) with a screw-top lid. Label it clearly as "SHARPS" and duct-tape the lid shut before taking it to a pharmacy—but check with your local pharmacist first, as they usually prefer the standardized yellow bins.


Note: Never put sharps in your blue recycling bin. They cannot be recycled and pose a major threat to the workers at sorting facilities.

If a bag rips open (which happens constantly), a loose capped needle can become a projectile or get wedged in a conveyor belt. Statistically, a significant portion of needlestick injuries happen to the users themselves while they are trying to put the cap back on. This is why medical protocols generally advise against re-capping needles entirely; the safest move is a "one-way trip" directly into a puncture-proof bin immediately after the injection.

## Gemini

The $k$ (sometimes written as $n$) isn't a specific number you missed in the problem; it is a mathematical placeholder used to describe an infinite number of points.


In the Question 10 solution you are looking at, the function is a wave that goes on forever. Since it crosses the x-axis (the $\theta$-intercepts) over and over again, mathematicians use $k$ to represent any integer ($\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots$).


How to read $\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi k$:
This formula is a "generator" for all the intercepts:



* If $k = 0$: $\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi(0) = \mathbf{\frac{\pi}{2}}$ (The first positive intercept)

* If $k = 1$: $\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi(1) = \mathbf{\frac{3\pi}{2}}$ (The next intercept to the right)

* If $k = -1$: $\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi(-1) = \mathbf{-\frac{\pi}{2}}$ (The first intercept to the left)


Why is it $+\pi k$ and not $+2\pi k$?
For the cosine function, the intercepts happen every half-period.



* A full cycle is $2\pi$.

* But the wave crosses the "zero line" twice per cycle (once on the way down, once on the way up).

* The distance between $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ is exactly $\pi$.


The "General Solution"
When you see $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ at the end of a line (like in the Brief Explanation for 10e), it’s just a formal way of saying: "This happens at $\pi/2$, and then again every $\pi$ distance forever in both directions."


If your specific test question asks for intercepts on a limited domain (like $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$), you just plug in integers for $k$ until the results fall outside that range.
