# non-newtonian fluid means you could run on it?

non-newtonian fluid means you could run on it?

---

## Gemini

Yes, that is exactly right! However, it specifically applies to a certain type of non-Newtonian fluid.


A non-Newtonian fluid is simply any fluid that doesn't follow Newton's law of viscosity—meaning its thickness (viscosity) changes depending on how much force or stress you apply to it.


To understand how you can run on one, it helps to look at the two main ways these fluids behave:


1. Shear-Thickening Fluids (The ones you can run on)
With these fluids, the viscosity increases when stress is applied. The harder and faster you hit them, the more like a solid they behave.



* The Classic Example: Oobleck (a simple mixture of cornstarch and water).

* How running works: When your foot strikes the surface of Oobleck with high force, the cornstarch particles lock together, turning the liquid into a temporary solid under your feet. As long as you keep moving quickly and putting high force into each step, you can literally run across a pool of it.

* The Catch: If you stop moving, the force drops, the material behaves like a liquid again, and you will instantly sink.


2. Shear-Thinning Fluids (The ones you definitely cannot run on)
With these fluids, the viscosity decreases when stress is applied. The harder you hit them, the more liquid they become.



* Examples: Ketchup, quicksand, and paint.

* Why you can't run on them: If you tried to run across a giant vat of ketchup, the force of your foot striking the surface would actually make it less viscous (thinner), causing you to plunge right in.



So, if you ever find yourself facing a pool of cornstarch and water, keep your knees high and your pace fast, and you'll stay right on top!

how do they fluids exist? what's happening there?

---

## Gemini

To understand how these fluids exist, we have to look at what is happening at the microscopic level. The secret lies in how the microscopic solid particles suspended within the liquid interact with one another when force is applied.


Here is the molecular breakdown of what is happening inside the two different types of non-Newtonian fluids.



Inside a Shear-Thickening Fluid (e.g., Oobleck)
In a normal state, Oobleck is just a high concentration of tiny solid starch particles suspended in water.



* At Rest or Gentle Pressure: The starch particles are completely surrounded by a thin layer of water. This water acts as a lubricant. When you move your hand through it slowly, the particles have plenty of time to slide past one another smoothly. The fluid flows like a thick liquid.

* Under Sudden Impact (Running/Hitting): When you strike the surface rapidly, you instantly force the water out from between the starch particles. Without that lubricating water layer, the particles smash directly into each other and jam together. They form a temporary, rigid network called hydroclusters.



What's happening: The mechanical friction between the jammed particles completely locks them in place, momentarily turning the liquid into a solid. The moment that force stops, water rushes back between the particles, and it liquefies again.




Inside a Shear-Thinning Fluid (e.g., Ketchup or Paint)
Shear-thinning fluids work in the exact opposite way, usually because they are made of long, tangled molecular chains (polymers) or weakly bonded clusters.



* At Rest: The long molecules inside the fluid are tangled together in a messy, randomized jumble, or they are held together by weak chemical bonds. This structural mess creates a lot of internal resistance, making the fluid incredibly thick and sluggish (like ketchup refusing to come out of a glass bottle).

* Under Sudden Impact or Stress: When you apply force—like shaking the bottle, stirring violently, or hitting it—you force those tangled, messy polymer chains to straighten out and align parallel to the direction of the movement.



What's happening: Once the molecules are neatly aligned and organized, they can slide past each other with almost zero resistance. The internal friction plummets, and the fluid suddenly becomes much thinner and flows easily.




The Big Picture: Why do they exist?
In physics, standard fluids (like water or alcohol) are called Newtonian fluids. Their viscosity only changes if you change their temperature or pressure. Their molecules are small, simple, and don't change shape or lock together when hit.


Non-Newtonian fluids exist because they are complex mixtures—either liquids packed with suspended solids (colloids) or liquids made of massive, flexible polymer chains. Because these large components can physically change their shape, arrangement, or spacing in response to mechanical stress, the fluid's overall physical properties change on the fly.

## Gemini

To understand why ketchup, paint, and cornstarch-water (Oobleck) behave this way, we have to look at their physical recipes. None of these are "pure" liquids like water or alcohol. Instead, they are all mixtures containing microscopic structures that physically jam together or untangle when you push on them.


Here is exactly what is happening under the hood of each substance to make them non-Newtonian:



1. Cornstarch and Water (Shear-Thickening)
Cornstarch and water forms a colloid—a dense suspension of solid particles floating in a liquid.



* The Structure: Cornstarch particles are microscopic, rigid, and irregularly shaped grains. In a bowl of Oobleck, these grains are packed incredibly close together, separated only by a very thin layer of water that acts as a lubricant.

* Why it's Non-Newtonian: * When you move slowly, the water has time to flow around the starch grains, keeping them lubricated so they can slide past one another. It feels like a liquid.

* When you hit or run on it quickly, you violently smash the starch particles together, instantly squeezing the water out from between them. Without that lubricating water, the jagged starch grains physically lock and jam into each other, forming a rigid, solid network called a hydrocluster.






2. Ketchup (Shear-Thinning)
Ketchup is a highly concentrated network of crushed plant solids and long-chain molecules.



* The Structure: Ketchup is packed with tomato cell fragments, sugars, and a stabilizing ingredient called xanthan gum. Xanthan gum is made of massive, tangled, chain-like polymer molecules.

* Why it's Non-Newtonian: * At rest, those long xanthan gum chains and tomato fibers form a messy, randomized, tangled "ball of yarn." This chaotic jumble traps the water inside, creating massive internal friction that prevents the ketchup from flowing. It acts almost like a solid, which is why it gets stuck in the bottle.

* When you shake, smack, or squeeze the bottle, the mechanical force forces those tangled, messy chains to instantly straighten out and align parallel to the direction of the flow. Once they are neatly lined up like microscopic spaghetti strands, they slide past each other with virtually no resistance, causing the ketchup to suddenly liquefy and pour out.






3. Latex/House Paint (Shear-Thinning)
Paint is engineered to be non-Newtonian on purpose using specialized chemical additives called thixotropes.



* The Structure: Paint consists of color pigments, a liquid binder (like latex or acrylic), and long polymer thickening agents. At rest, these ingredients form weak chemical bonds with each other, building a delicate, loose microscopic scaffolding throughout the liquid.

* Why it's Non-Newtonian: * Paint needs to be thick while sitting in the can so the heavy color pigments don't sink to the bottom and separate.

* When you dip a brush or roller into the paint and push it against a wall, the mechanical shear stress instantly breaks those weak microscopic bonds and forces the polymer strands to align. The paint becomes thin and fluid, allowing it to spread smoothly without dragging.

* The moment the brush leaves the wall, the force stops. The polymer scaffolding quickly re-forms, making the paint thick again within seconds. This prevents the paint from dripping and running down your walls while it dries.

## Gemini

You are exactly right! Cornstarch does not dissolve in water. If you leave a glass of Oobleck sitting on the counter for a few hours, the cornstarch will completely separate from the water and settle into a hard, chalky layer at the very bottom.


To understand why this happens, it helps to look at what cornstarch actually is.



What Even IS Cornstarch?
At its core, cornstarch is a pure carbohydrate. It is a powdery substance extracted from the endosperm of a corn kernel—the meaty, starchy interior that serves as the seed's main energy reserve.


Chemically, it isn't made of simple, single sugar molecules. Instead, it is made of two massive, complex chain molecules packed tightly together:



* Amylose: Long, straight, linear chains of glucose.

* Amylopectin: Massive, highly branched chains of glucose.


Plants weave these enormous chains together into hard, microscopic, semi-crystalline packages called starch granules.



Why Doesn't It Dissolve?
When something dissolves in water (like salt or sugar), the water molecules surround the individual molecules of the substance, break them apart from one another, and pull them into the solution.


Cornstarch refuses to do this for two main reasons:



* The Molecules are Too Big: The amylose and amylopectin chains are gigantic. Water molecules simply aren't strong enough to tear these massive, tightly bound structures apart at room temperature.

* It Forms a Suspension, Not a Solution: Because the water can't break the granules down, the tiny solid particles just float around in the liquid. This is called a colloid or a suspension. It's more like throwing microscopic rocks into a swimming pool than melting sugar into tea.



The Secret to Cooking: Forcing it to Dissolve
While cornstarch won't dissolve in cold water, you can force it to interact with water using heat. This is a process chefs call starch gelatinization:



* When you heat a mixture of cornstarch and water to around 62°C to 70°C (144°F to 158°F), the water molecules gain enough thermal energy to finally burst inside the rigid starch granules.

* The granules absorb the water like tiny sponges and swell to many times their original size.

* Eventually, the granules pop, spilling the long amylose and amylopectin chains out into the liquid. These long chains tangle up with each other, trapping the rushing water molecules and turning a thin liquid into a thick sauce, gravy, or pudding.


So, in room-temperature water, cornstarch stays a stubborn, solid granule (giving you the perfect setup for a non-Newtonian fluid). But add heat, and you unlock its power as a culinary thickener!

## Gemini

Custard is a culinary preparation made by blending dairy (milk, cream, or both) with eggs, which is then heated to thicken it.


Instead of relying on flour or cornstarch to make the liquid thick, traditional custard relies entirely on the coagulation of egg proteins. When heated gently, the proteins in the egg yolks untangle and bond together, trapping the liquid dairy and turning it into a smooth, rich sauce or gel.


Custards can vary wildly in texture—ranging from a thin, pourable sauce to a thick pastry cream or a firm, baked solid.



The Two Main Types of Custard
Custards are generally split into two categories based on how they are cooked:


1. Stirred (or Runny) Custard
Cooked on a stovetop while being constantly stirred to prevent the eggs from curdling.



* Crème Anglaise: A classic, pourable vanilla dessert sauce poured over cakes or fruit.

* Curd: Fruit-based varieties like lemon curd, which add fruit juice to the egg and sugar mix.

* American "Pudding": Many commercial or homemade puddings are technically stirred custards, though they often add cornstarch to make them more stable.


2. Baked (or Set) Custard
Cooked undisturbed in an oven, usually in a bain-marie (water bath) to ensure the heat stays gentle and even. The eggs cook into a delicate, solid gel structure.



* Crème Brûlée: A rich baked custard topped with a layer of hardened, caramelized sugar.

* Flan / Crème Caramel: A baked custard cooked over a layer of caramel, then inverted onto a plate so the caramel sauce runs down the sides.

* Quiche: Custard isn't just for dessert! A quiche is a savory baked custard packed with cheese, meats, and vegetables inside a pastry crust.



Why Custard Can Be Tricky to Make
Custard is a classic test of temperature control. Egg yolks cook and solidify at around 65°C to 70°C (149°F to 158°F).


If you heat a custard too quickly or let it get too hot, the proteins bond together too tightly. They will squeeze out the trapped water and clump together, turning your smooth sauce into watery, sweet scrambled eggs. To prevent this, starch (like flour or cornstarch) is sometimes added to recipes like pastry cream; the starch molecules physically get in the way of the egg proteins, preventing them from over-clumping and making the custard much more stable.
