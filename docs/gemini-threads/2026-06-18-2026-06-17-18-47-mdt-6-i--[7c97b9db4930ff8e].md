[2026-06-17 18:56 MDT-6] why is the periodic table the way it is for lanthanide and actinide?

---

## Gemini

The lanthanide and actinide series are separated into two rows at the bottom of the periodic table due to a combination of atomic structure and practical page design.


Here is exactly why they are arranged that way.


1. Electron Subshells ($f$-block)
The periodic table is organized by how electrons fill different energy levels or "subshells" ($s$, $p$, $d$, and $f$).



* Lanthanides (atomic numbers 57–71 or 58–71) and Actinides (atomic numbers 89–103 or 90–103) represent the filling of the $f$-subshell.

* Because the $f$-subshell can hold a maximum of 14 electrons, these series are 14 elements wide.

* In terms of chemical behavior, they all fit into Group 3 of the periodic table, right between the $s$-block and the $d$-block (transition metals).


2. The "Long" vs. "Wide" Table
If you were to insert the lanthanides and actinides directly into the main body of the periodic table where they structurally belong, the table would become incredibly wide.


Instead of the standard 18 columns, the table would stretch out to 32 columns.


Standard Layout (18 Columns Wide):
[ s-block ]                    [ p-block ]
[ s-block ]     [ d-block ]    [ p-block ]
                [f-block pulled down]

Fully Expanded Layout (32 Columns Wide):
[ s-block ] [  f-block (Lanthanides/Actinides)  ] [ d-block ] [ p-block ]

While the 32-column layout is technically more accurate in terms of sequential atomic numbers, it is highly impractical for standard use. Moving the $f$-block to the bottom allows the table to fit cleanly on standard pieces of paper, posters, and screens without making the other groups microscopic.


3. Chemical Similarity
Elements in the same column usually share similar properties. However, because the incoming electrons in lanthanides and actinides go into an inner $f$-subshell rather than the outermost shell, these elements look and behave very similarly to one another horizontally.


Pulling them out into their own rows emphasizes this horizontal relationship:



* Lanthanides are all relatively soft, silvery-white metals with very similar chemical reactivity (often called "rare earth elements").

* Actinides are all radioactive, and most of them are synthetic (man-made) elements that only exist for fractions of a second in lab settings.


By pulling them down, the periodic table maintains a clean grid that keeps elements with vertical similarities aligned while managing the massive footprint of the $f$-block.

---

## Gemini

The short answer to both of your questions is yes, but with an important caveat regarding what "real life" actually means geometrically.


Computer vision and machine learning (ML) handle these tasks using distinct pipelines for estimation (backsolving) and correction.



Part A: Can ML reliably backsolve for the lens and distortion?
Yes, quite reliably. This task is known in computer vision as Single-Image Camera Calibration or Camera Resectioning.


How it Works
Traditional methods require you to photograph a known geometric pattern (like a checkerboard) to map how lines bend. Deep learning models skip this by relying on the Manhattan World Assumption—the concept that human-built structures (like houses) are filled with parallel and orthogonal lines (walls, ceilings, door frames, floor grids).



* Feature Extraction: Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or Vision Transformers (ViTs) scan the interior image to detect structural lines, vanishing points, and the horizon line.

* Parameter Regression: The model maps these visual cues against a geometric camera model (such as the Brown-Conrady or pinhole model). Instead of guessing the exact focal length in millimeters (which depends on the sensor size), the ML model predicts the Field of View (FoV), tilt, roll, and specific radial distortion coefficients ($k_1, k_2$).

* Synthetic Training: These models are highly accurate because they are trained on massive datasets of perfectly flat, synthetic 3D indoor scenes that have been intentionally distorted with randomized, simulated wide-angle lenses. The network learns exactly how a $12\text{mm}$ lens stretches space compared to a $24\text{mm}$ lens.



Part B: Can ML "CORRECT" the photo to reflect real life?
Yes, mechanically—but it introduces a new problem. Once the ML model determines the mathematical degree of distortion, reversing it is a solved computational geometry problem. The software re-maps the pixels back into a linear perspective. This is called Perspective Correction or Keystoning Correction. Commercial AI tools (like Imagen AI, Lightroom's automated geometry tools, or custom U-Net architectures) already automate this for real estate workflows to ensure that leaning walls become perfectly vertical.


However, correcting a wide-angle shot introduces a strict trade-off:


1. Barrel Distortion (The "Fish-Eye" Effect)
This is the physical bending of straight lines near the edges of the frame.



* The AI Fix: Extremely reliable. The AI can un-bend these lines, making every wall and door frame perfectly straight.


2. Perspective Distortion (The "Stretching" Effect)
This is what actually makes real estate photos deceptive. When a wide-angle lens ($14\text{mm}$ to $16\text{mm}$) is used to capture a small bedroom, objects close to the camera scale up massively, while the back wall pushes far into the distance. This is not a "lens defect"; it is mathematically accurate linear perspective for that wide field of view.



* The Limitation: If you force an AI to change this perspective to look like a "normal" lens ($50\text{mm}$), the field of view narrows dramatically. To make the room layout look tight and natural like human vision, the AI would have to crop out $60%$ of the image, leaving you with a close-up photo of a closet door rather than a view of the whole room.


The Realistic Output
What AI perspective correction actually delivers is an image where all vertical and horizontal lines are perfectly parallel and plumb, eliminating the dizzying "funhouse" lean. It fixes the accidental tilt of the photographer's camera, but it cannot fundamentally shrink a cavernous-looking $10 \times 10$ bedroom back to its true physical boundaries without discarding the peripheral spatial data you need to see the room.
