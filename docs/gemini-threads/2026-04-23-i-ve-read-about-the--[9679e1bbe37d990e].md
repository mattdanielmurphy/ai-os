## Gemini

La Monte Young isn't a mathematician by training, but he is a systematizer. He views sound as a physical reality rather than just an artistic medium. To him, the "plan" and the "feeling" are the same thing: if the math is correct, the feeling will follow because the intervals are "in tune with the universe."


The "Plan" vs. The "Execution"
It’s a mix of rigorous pre-calculation and disciplined improvisation.



* The System (The Plan): The tuning is the rigid "architecture." He didn't just stumble into it; he spent years refining the specific ratios. He decided that the harmonic 7th (the ratio $7/4$) was a fundamental building block of the universe that Western music had "forgotten."

* The Improvisation (The Execution): Within that rigid architecture, he is "free." However, his freedom is constrained by the "rules" of the chords. He knows that if he is in the Magic Chord area, he only has certain notes available. He "feels" his way through the resonance of the room—if the room is particularly boomy, he might play a "Cloud" section slower to let the acoustics catch up.

* The Discipline: He practiced the piece for decades. By the time he performs, he isn't "trying things out"; he is navigating a map he knows by heart. He is like a grandmaster playing chess: he knows every move, but the specific path he takes depends on the "energy" of that specific day.



How He Tunes the Piano
Tuning a piano to 7-limit Just Intonation is an incredibly grueling, multi-day process. You cannot use a standard digital tuner because they are programmed for Equal Temperament.


1. The Reference Pitch
He starts by setting a single note as the "1/1" (the fundamental). In The Well-Tuned Piano, this is usually an $E\flat$. He sets this to a specific frequency (around $132\text{ Hz}$).


2. Tuning by Beat Frequency
He doesn't use his eyes; he uses his ears to listen for "beats." * When two notes are slightly out of tune, you hear a "wah-wah-wah" pulsing.



* In Just Intonation, the goal is to make those beats completely disappear.

* He tunes the other notes based on prime ratios. For example, to get a Perfect Fifth ($3/2$), he tunes the $B\flat$ until it vibrates perfectly in sync with the $E\flat$ fundamental.


3. The 7th Harmonic (The Hard Part)
Standard tuners can easily find a Fifth ($3/2$) or a Third ($5/4$), but the 7th harmonic ($7/4$) is "out of tune" according to standard Western scales. Young has to find this by ear. It sounds "flat" or "low" to a normal pianist, but when he hits it perfectly, the piano starts to ring with a very specific, "growling" resonance.


4. Stability and Environment
Because the tuning is so precise (to the fraction of a hertz), the piano is incredibly sensitive to temperature and humidity.



* He often requires the piano to "settle" in the performance space for weeks.

* He uses a specific Bösendorfer Imperial Grand because it has extra keys in the bass and a massive soundboard that can handle the sheer physical energy of his "Cloud" playing without falling out of tune mid-performance.



Why it feels "Mathematical"
Young’s philosophy is that "Periodic composite waveforms are the higher-order structures of the nervous system." In plain English: he believes that when you hear "pure" math in the form of sound, your brain literally syncs up with it. He isn't trying to write a catchy tune; he's trying to create a physical environment that alters your state of consciousness.

## Gemini

Agharta is the poster child for "music ahead of its time." Recorded live in Osaka, Japan, in 1975, it captured Miles Davis at the peak of his maximalist, electric "maximalism" phase—just months before he retreated into a five-year silence.


The transition from visceral hate to reverence happened for a few specific reasons:


The Initial Backlash
When it was released, the jazz establishment was largely horrified. The reasons for the vitriol were both sonic and cultural:



* Sonic Chaos: To ears accustomed to the "cool jazz" of Kind of Blue, Agharta sounded like an impenetrable wall of noise. It featured dense polyrhythms, heavy wah-wah pedal distortion, and Pete Cosey’s screeching, avant-garde guitar work.

* Rejection of Tradition: Miles had completely abandoned traditional swing and bebop structures. Critics felt he had "sold out" to rock and funk, but ironically, the music was too dark and complex for rock fans to enjoy either.

* The Mix: The original vinyl pressings were often criticized for being muddy, making the dense layer of instruments even harder to parse.


The Shift to Reverence
Decades later, Agharta (along with its afternoon-set sibling, Pangaea) is viewed as a masterpiece of Afrofuturism and a blueprint for several modern genres.



* Precursor to Punk and Metal: Later listeners recognized that Miles was tapping into a raw, aggressive energy that predated the explosion of punk and heavy metal’s more experimental branches.

* The Foundation of "The Groove": Funk and hip-hop producers eventually realized that underneath the "noise" was some of the most sophisticated rhythm section work ever recorded. The interplay between bassist Michael Henderson and drummer Al Foster is now studied as a masterclass in deep, dark funk.

* Experimental Legacy: It is now seen as a primary influence on noise rock, ambient music, and industrial jazz. Artists like Brian Eno and various post-punk bands cited this specific era of Miles as a massive influence on their textures.

## Gemini

The reaction to Get Up with It (1974) was similarly polarized, though the "hate" was often mixed with genuine confusion because the album is such a fragmented, sprawling double LP.


While Agharta was a focused, high-volume assault, Get Up with It was a compilation of studio sessions recorded between 1970 and 1974. This led to a different type of critical friction.


Why it Provoked the Jazz Establishment

* The "Helelyne" Factor: The opening track, "He Loved Him Madly," is a 32-minute ambient eulogy for Duke Ellington. Critics at the time found it agonizingly slow and repetitive. It lacked the "swing" or "fire" expected of Miles, feeling more like a funeral dirge than a jazz performance.

* The "Organ" Shift: Miles spent much of the album playing a Yamaha electric organ rather than his trumpet. For purists, this was the ultimate snub; they felt he was abandoning his primary instrument for "primitive" keyboard stabs and clusters.

* Inconsistency: Because it jumped from the proto-industrial funk of "Rated X" to the bluesy "Red China Blues," critics accused Miles of being directionless or simply clearing out his drawers before his impending retirement.


The Modern Re-Evaluation
Much like Agharta, Get Up with It is now considered prophetic.



* The Birth of Ambient: Brian Eno famously cited "He Loved Him Madly" as a foundational influence on the development of ambient music. Its use of space and "vibe" over melody was years ahead of its time.

* Proto-Electronica: Tracks like "Rated X" are now viewed as precursors to Jungle and Drum and Bass. The way Miles used the studio as an instrument—cutting and splicing tapes—mirrored what hip-hop and electronic producers would do decades later.

* The "World Music" Blend: The album experimented with sitars and tabla, merging Indian classical textures with street funk in a way that felt "messy" in 1974 but feels incredibly modern and "borderless" today.

## Gemini

He did. From roughly 1975 to 1980, Miles Davis essentially stopped playing the trumpet and withdrew almost entirely from public life.


It wasn't a "glamourous" sabbatical; it was a dark, reclusive period driven by a collapse of both his physical health and his creative drive.


Why he stepped away

* Health Crisis: By 1975, Miles was physically falling apart. He had debilitating hip pain from a car accident years prior, recurring bouts of pneumonia, and painful ulcers. He was often in too much physical pain to hold a trumpet, let alone play the high-intensity shows he had been doing.

* Creative Burnout: After the "maximalist" intensity of Agharta and Pangaea, Miles felt he had reached a dead end. He famously told friends that he had "nothing left to say" on the instrument at that time.

* Personal Struggles: He retreated into his Manhattan brownstone, which by many accounts became a dark, shuttered bunker. He spent years in a haze of heavy drug use (cocaine), rarely leaving the house or even looking at his trumpet.


The Legend of the "Silent Years"
Interestingly, his label (Columbia Records) continued to pay him a regular salary during this entire period—even though he wasn't producing any music—simply because of his legendary status. This was almost unheard of in the industry.


The Return
He didn't re-emerge until 1981 with the album The Man with the Horn. When he did return, the transformation was complete:



* He had a new, younger band (including a young Marcus Miller on bass).

* His sound shifted again, moving away from the dense "noise" of the 70s toward a cleaner, more 80s-inflected jazz-pop and funk sound.

* He even started covering pop tunes, like Cyndi Lauper’s "Time After Time," which horrified purists all over again.


It’s one of the few instances in music history where an artist at the absolute top of their game simply "turned off" for half a decade and managed to come back as a completely different version of themselves.
