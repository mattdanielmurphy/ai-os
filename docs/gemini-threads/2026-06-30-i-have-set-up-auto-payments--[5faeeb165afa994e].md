When you set up auto-pay (Pre-Authorized Debits) directly through the Canadian Tire Bank website using an external bank account like KOHO, there is a specific process they follow that explains why it takes a couple of months to kick in and exactly when the money leaves your account.


Here is how Canadian Tire's auto-pay system works and how you can verify if it is active:


How to Check If It's Working
Looking at your current statement details, your Statement Date was June 22, 2026, and your Due Date is July 13, 2026.


To confirm if the auto-payment has successfully registered for this cycle, look at the physical or PDF copy of your latest monthly statement (the one issued on June 22).



* If it is active: There will be a printed message on the statement itself—usually near the top or bottom of the summary page—that says something like: "The Minimum Due (or Full Balance) will be automatically withdrawn from your bank account on [Date]."

* If it is NOT active yet: The statement will look completely normal and will not mention an automatic withdrawal, meaning you still need to make this month's $5.25 payment manually to avoid any issues.


Why Does It Take 2 to 3 Months?
When you link an external account via a routing and account number on their portal, Canadian Tire has to send a verification request to the automated clearing network to register the Pre-Authorized Debit (PAD) agreement. Because of how their billing cycles cut, if an agreement isn't fully processed before a statement generates, it won't apply until the following statement cycle. This is why banks standardly warn that it can take 1 to 2 statement periods to become active.


When Does the Money Actually Get Taken?
Your fear is entirely valid—the system does wait until right before the deadline.


Canadian Tire typically processes the auto-pay withdrawal on the actual due date (in this case, July 13) or occasionally up to 2 to 3 business days just before it. They do not pull the money the moment the statement drops. Because of this timeline, you need to ensure that your KOHO account has the necessary funds sitting there ready to go by July 10–13 so the debit doesn't bounce.


Recommendation: Check your June 22 PDF eStatement. If you don't see the text explicitly stating that an automatic withdrawal will occur, go ahead and click Make a Payment on your dashboard to handle the $5.25 manually this month so you don't risk a late flag while the backend paperwork finishes processing.

## Gemini

Yes, manual underwriting still absolutely exists, but its role has completely shifted. Instead of being the standard way to process applications, manual underwriting is now the exception network—the backup system used only when automated algorithms can't make a clear-cut decision.


Large retail banks, credit card issuers, and fintechs rely on automation for 95%+ of their volume because it’s cheap and fast. However, humans still step in under specific circumstances, depending heavily on the type of credit you are applying for.


Here is where and why manual underwriting is still used:


1. Mortgages (The Most Common Place)
Even with massive automation, the mortgage industry still uses manual underwriting every day. If you apply for a mortgage in Canada or the US and the automated system flags your profile as "Refer" instead of "Approve" or "Decline," a human underwriter takes over.



* Complex Income: If you are self-employed, own multiple businesses, or rely on variable commissions and bonuses, algorithms struggle to calculate your stable income. A human has to manually audit tax returns and financial statements.

* Credit Repair Nuances: If you have a past bankruptcy or consumer proposal that has been discharged, automated systems might instantly reject you based on the old black mark. A manual underwriter can look at the "letter of explanation," verify your spotless post-bankruptcy history, and manually override the system to grant an approval.


2. High-Net-Worth & Private Banking
If you are applying for high-end credit lines or custom wealth-management loans, automated scoring models break down because the asset structures are too complex. Private bankers use manual underwriting to assess unique collateral, like corporate shares, luxury real estate portfolios, or investment accounts, rather than just looking at a standard credit score and pay stub.


3. Subprime and Credit-Builder Lenders
Some specialized lenders who cater specifically to people with severely damaged credit utilize a hybrid model. While an algorithm does the initial filtering, a human underwriter might manually review bank statements to verify steady cash flow, looking for stability that your credit bureau score doesn't reflect.



Why It's Rare for Credit Cards
For standard credit cards like your Canadian Tire card, manual underwriting is virtually dead. The profit margins on a $1,000 to $2,500 limit are too small to justify paying a human analyst $40/hour to review the file. If an algorithm can't approve you automatically based on your bureau data, the system will simply issue an automated decline letter.


In short: Automation handles the rules, but humans are still kept on the payroll to handle the nuances, especially when hundreds of thousands of dollars are on the line.
