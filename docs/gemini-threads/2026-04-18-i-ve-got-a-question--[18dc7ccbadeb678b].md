## Gemini

That is a sharp observation. You’re touching on the exact reasons why artificial gravity via rotation is "fictitious" gravity—it's actually just inertia being redirected.


Here is a breakdown of how those physics play out for the astronaut’s movement and their internal organs.



1. The "Running Against the Grain" Problem
You are absolutely correct. Since the "gravity" is entirely dependent on your tangential velocity ($v$), your motion relative to the floor matters immensely.


The formula for centripetal acceleration is:
$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$$


If the station is spinning clockwise at 5 m/s and you run counter-clockwise at 5 m/s, your net velocity relative to the universe (an inertial frame) is zero.



* The Result: You would effectively "cancel out" the artificial gravity. You would lift off the floor and float in place while the station continued to spin around you.

* The Catch: This is only easy in small stations. In a massive station with a huge radius, the rotation speed ($v$) would be much higher, meaning you'd have to run at Olympic-sprinter speeds just to feel slightly lighter.



2. Do the "Internals" Feel It?
This is where it gets interesting. You’re right that the floor only touches your feet, but because your body is a connected structure, that force is transmitted through you.


The "Squeeze" Transmission
Think of your body like a stack of springs. The floor pushes your feet. Your feet then push your ankles, your ankles push your leg bones, and your bones push up on your organs.



* On Earth, gravity pulls every single atom of your liver downward, and your skeletal system holds it up.

* In Space, the floor pushes your skeleton into your liver to force it to move in a circle.


To your brain, these two sensations feel identical. The "squeeze" between your chair and your spine is what you interpret as weight.


The Gravity Gradient
There is a catch, though. Because $a_c$ depends on the radius ($r$), the "gravity" at your feet is actually stronger than the "gravity" at your head.



* Your feet are further from the center (larger $r$).

* Your head is closer to the center (smaller $r$).


If the station is small, your blood would actually "feel" heavier in your feet than in your head, which can cause significant cardiovascular issues and vertigo that you wouldn't experience on Earth.



3. The Coriolis Effect
The biggest giveaway that you aren't on Earth is when you move. If you try to pour a cup of water or throw a ball inside a rotating station, the object will appear to curve wildly through the air.


Since there is no actual gravitational field pulling the object down, it just tries to travel in a straight line (inertia) while the room curves away from it. This is the Coriolis Effect, and in a small, fast-spinning station, it would make most people incredibly motion-sick very quickly.

Inquiry Question
How can we create "artificial gravity" in deep space? How can we ensure that our astronauts experience $9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$? Can movies offer us some insight into this?


Artificial gravity can be created in space by rotating a spacecraft so that the inner floor pushes inward on the astronauts, providing the centripetal acceleration needed for uniform circular motion. This normal force acts as a support force that replicates the feeling of weight. To ensure astronauts experience exactly $9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$ (or $1g$), we must precisely balance the radius of the rotating station and its rotational period according to the formula $a_c = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}$. Movies like Mission to Mars offer insight into this concept, but as shown in the calculations below, they often distort the required parameters for cinematic effect.



Part 1: Core
Movie: Mission to Mars


1. The Earth's gravitational field (due to its mass) creates an attractive force that causes us to be pulled in towards its centre. Since the Earth is a solid, we end up stopping at the surface. We feel our weight because of this squeeze between us and the Earth. What do we call this "squeeze force" that we feel? Explain why one feels "weightless" while in a freefall (skydiving for example) even though a force of gravity is still present.


The "squeeze force" we feel when standing on a surface is called the Normal Force ($F_N$). Our apparent weight is really just the sensation of this support force pushing against us, not gravity itself. When a person is in a freefall, the force of gravity ($F_g$) is still accelerating them downward, but because there is no solid surface pushing back up against them, the Normal Force is zero ($F_N = 0 \text{ N}$). Since we only "feel" gravity through the normal force, the absence of $F_N$ makes the skydiver feel weightless.



* Internal Force Transmission: While the floor only touches the feet, the body is a connected structure that transmits this "squeeze" upward. The floor pushes the skeleton, which in turn pushes against the internal organs to force them into the circular path. This mechanical "squeeze" mimics the sensation of Earth's gravity, where the skeletal system supports organs against the pull of a mass.


2. What would happen to the astronaut if the space station stopped rotating?


If the space station stopped rotating, the astronaut would no longer be subjected to the centripetal acceleration required to move in a circle. Without the floor constantly curving into their path and pushing them inward, they would simply continue moving in a straight-line path due to their own inertia (Newton's First Law). They would appear to float weightlessly relative to the station until they collided with some part of the interior.



* Note on Counter-Rotation: Because artificial gravity depends on tangential velocity ($v$), an astronaut can "cancel" the effect by moving. If an astronaut runs in the opposite direction of the station's spin at a speed equal to the rotation speed, their net velocity relative to the universe becomes zero. Consequently, they would lift off the floor and float in place while the station spins around them.


3. When the object is rotating, the astronaut will experience a Net Force ($F_{centripetal}$) directed towards the centre. However, the astronaut does not fall towards the centre. Using Newton's Laws, explain why the astronaut remains on the inner surface of the space station.


As the station rotates, the astronaut's inertia causes their body to want to travel in a straight, tangential line. However, the inner floor of the space station constantly curves inward into the astronaut's path. By Newton's Second Law, the floor exerts an inward Normal Force on the astronaut, providing the required net force ($F_c$) to constantly change their direction of motion into a circle. The astronaut does not fall to the centre because this force is merely acting to overcome their forward inertia, keeping them pressed against the floor.






* 
Equation of motion for the astronaut: $$F_{net} = F_N$$




* 
Substitute $F_c$ for $F_{net}$:
Since the astronaut is in uniform circular motion, $F_{net} = F_c = m a_c$. Therefore:
$$F_N = m a_c$$

Which formula is more useful for the videos provided? Justify your choice and write your new equation of motion with the appropriate version of $a_c$.

Substituting this into our equation gives the new equation of motion:

* Improvement 1: Decrease the Period ($T$). To generate $1g$ at $r = 4.58 \text{ m}$, $T \approx 4.29 \text{ s}$.

* Improvement 2: Increase the Radius ($r$). To generate $1g$ at $T = 26.0 \text{ s}$, $r \approx 167.8 \text{ m}$.


2. In the movie, the astronauts climb up the ladder to the centre of the space station where they "dance" in a weightless environment. Explain whether or not this is what would actually happen.


At $r = 0 \text{ m}$, $a_c = 0 \text{ m/s}^2$, so weightlessness exists. However, in a station this small, the gravity gradient is so steep that moving even a meter from the center would create a noticeable pull, making the "spacious" dancing scene impossible.


3. Would Jerry's head experience the same simulated "gravitational pull" as his feet?


No. Since $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$, and the head is closer to the center ($r_{head} = 2.69 \text{ m}$), his head feels $0.16 \text{ m/s}^2$ while his feet feel $0.27 \text{ m/s}^2$. This $41%$ difference would cause extreme vertigo.


4. The Coriolis Effect: Why moving is a giveaway.


In a rotating station, any object moving relative to the station (like a thrown ball or poured water) will appear to curve wildly. Since there is no real gravitational field pulling it down, the object travels in a straight line while the room curves away from it. In a small, fast-spinning station, this Coriolis Effect would make simple tasks disorienting and cause severe motion sickness.



Final Conclusion Statement
Gravity can be replicated via uniform circular motion, but the station in Mission to Mars is a scientific "fictitious force" failure. It is too small and rotates too slowly to produce $1g$, and the steep gravity gradients would make life aboard practically impossible.
