eg if I say q=e for proton, it doesn't say whether it's positive e or negative e.
I guess the simplest way would be just q=+e and for an elec. q=-e?

## Gemini

Yes, you have nailed it. Writing $q = +e$ for a proton and $q = -e$ for an electron is exactly how it is done in physics.


By definition, $e$ represents the elementary charge, which is a purely positive constant value:


$$e \approx 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$$


Because $e$ itself is always a positive number, it doesn't inherent a sign. Therefore, to specify the charge of a specific particle, you must manually attach the sign to the front of the variable:



* Proton: $q = +e$

* Electron: $q = -e$

* Alpha particle (Helium nucleus): $q = +2e$


Using $+e$ and $-e$ is the cleanest, most universally recognized shorthand in physics notes and calculations to show both the magnitude and the sign of a charge simultaneously.

## Gemini

The direction of the proton's path is determined by the Right-Hand Rule (RHR) for magnetic force, not the direction of the accelerating voltage.


The voltage was only used to get the proton moving to the right (velocity $v$). Once it enters the magnetic field ($B$), the voltage is no longer acting on it.


1. Determining the Direction
To find the direction of the force ($F_B$) on a moving charge, use your right hand:



* Thumb: Points in the direction of the velocity ($v$). In this diagram, that is to the right.

* Fingers: Point in the direction of the magnetic field ($B$). The dots "$\odot$" indicate the field is coming out of the page (toward you).

* Palm: The direction your palm pushes is the direction of the force on a positive charge (like a proton).


With your thumb to the right and fingers pointing at your face, your palm should be facing downward (toward the bottom of the page). Therefore, the proton will curve in a downward arc (clockwise) toward the negative $y$-axis.



2. Why the Voltage Doesn't Matter for the Turn
The voltage $(\Delta V)$ provided the kinetic energy to the proton before it entered the field.



* The work done by the electric field is $W = q \Delta V$.

* Once inside the uniform magnetic field, the force is always perpendicular to the velocity. This means zero work is done by the magnetic field, and the proton's speed remains constant while only its direction changes.


3. Calculating the Radius (Part B)
To get that $7.3\text{ cm}$ answer, you combine the energy from the voltage with the centripetal force:



* Find Velocity ($v$):
$$qV = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{2qV}{m}}$$

* Find Radius ($r$):
The magnetic force provides the centripetal force ($qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}$), which simplifies to:
$$r = \frac{mv}{qB}$$


Using the mass of a proton ($1.67 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$) and its charge ($1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$), you'll arrive at that $0.073\text{ m}$ result.

## Gemini

Yes, absolutely. Both iOS and Android have built-in settings specifically designed to handle this exact nightmare. You can configure your phone to completely silence alerts for incoming messages if the sender isn’t in your contacts, sorting them into a separate list so they don't pop up on your screen.


Depending on what device you are using, here is how to kill off those notifications:


On iPhone (iOS)
Apple splits this into two steps: one filters the texts into a separate tab, and the other explicitly turns off the notification sounds and banners for them.



* Filter the messages: Open Settings > Apps > Messages (or just Messages on older iOS versions). Scroll down and toggle on Filter Unknown Senders (sometimes labeled Screen Unknown Senders).

* Turn off the notifications: Go to Settings > Notifications > Messages. Scroll to the bottom, tap Customize Notifications, and toggle Unknown Senders to Off.


Result: Texts from anyone not in your contacts will go straight into an "Unknown Senders" tab inside the Messages app. Your phone will not vibrate, ring, or light up when they come in, though you can still check that tab manually whenever you want to make sure you didn't miss something legitimate (like a delivery update).



On Android (Google Messages)
Android uses built-in spam protection that automatically catches a massive percentage of these, but you can also tighten the rules.



* Open the Messages app.

* Tap your profile icon in the top right corner and go to Messages settings.

* Tap Spam protection and ensure Enable spam protection is toggled On. This uses an automated filter to silently dump obvious phishing texts into a "Spam & blocked" folder.

* If you want a stricter blanket rule to silence all unknown numbers, go to your phone's main Settings > Notifications > Do Not Disturb > People > Messages and set it to Contacts only. (You can leave Do Not Disturb toggled off normally, or customize a specific Focus/DND profile for your work hours to entirely isolate your notification shade from non-contacts).


Setting this up takes less than a minute and immediately cuts down the digital noise.

You can silence and filter them on macOS with a few quick adjustments:


1. Filter and Silence Unknown Senders in Messages
Just like on iOS, you can force the Mac Messages app to segregate unknown numbers and kill the alerts for them.



* Open the Messages app on your Mac.

* In the menu bar, click Messages > Settings... (or press Cmd + ,).

* Go to the General tab.

* Check the box next to Filter unknown senders.

* Next, open your system Settings (via the Apple menu or Spotlight).

* Navigate to Notifications > Messages.

* Look for the notification grouping options or customize alerts, and ensure that notifications for Unknown Senders are disabled or silenced.


Result: The texts from numbers not in your address book will silently drop into a separate "Unknown Senders" list view in the app sidebar without triggering a banner or a sound.



2. Leverage Focus Modes for a Hard Blanket Block
If you want an airtight way to guarantee zero pings from random numbers while you are working or coding, you can enforce this at the system level using Focus.



* Open System Settings > Focus.

* Select your default working Focus profile (or create one).

* Under Allow Notifications, click on People.

* Set Allow Messages From to Contacts Only.


When that Focus mode is active, macOS will automatically drop any incoming text alert that doesn't originate from a known contact card, keeping your desktop completely free of scam interruptions.
