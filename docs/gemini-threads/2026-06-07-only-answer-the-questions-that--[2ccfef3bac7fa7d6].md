By separating the charges, you store electrical potential energy. If you give those electrons a path (a circuit), they will "fall" back toward the positive charges.



Potential vs. Potential Difference (Voltage)
In physics, we distinguish between the absolute potential and the difference between two points:


1. Electric Potential ($V$)
This is the amount of potential energy stored per unit of charge at a specific location.
$$V = \frac{E_p}{q}$$
Where $E_p$ is electric potential energy and $q$ is charge. It tells you how much a single Coulomb of charge would "want" to move if placed at that exact spot.


2. Electric Potential Difference ($\Delta V$ or Voltage)
This is what actually matters in circuits and induction. It is the difference in electric potential between two distinct points (like the two ends of the wire in your lesson).



The Battery Visual: Think of a standard AA battery. The chemical reactions inside force electrons to pile up on the negative terminal, leaving the positive terminal depleted. Because of this separation, a potential difference of $1.5\text{ V}$ exists between the terminals.

* Because charges are now separated, you have created an Electric Potential Difference across the ends of the wire.

## Gemini

When a region gets stuck in a relentless 15-day rainy stretch, it usually boils down to a massive atmospheric traffic jam. Under normal conditions, weather systems are pushed along smoothly from west to east by the jet stream—the high-altitude, fast-moving river of air that acts as Earth's steering mechanism.


But every now and then, that steering mechanism breaks down, resulting in two main culprits behind a multi-week deluge:


1. Atmospheric Blocking (The Traffic Jam)
Sometimes, the jet stream slows down and begins to loop wildly, creating massive, stagnant curves. When these curves stall out, they create an "atmospheric block."


One common version is called an Omega Block because the air currents shape themselves like the Greek letter $\Omega$. A massive high-pressure system gets wedged in the middle, flanked by two low-pressure systems on either side.



If you are trapped underneath one of those low-pressure troughs, you are stuck in the "active storm track." Because the whole system is completely immobilized by the blocking high next door, the same band of rain clouds just spins over your head day after day, unable to move along.


2. Atmospheric Rivers (The Firehose)
If the 15-day rain stretch is incredibly heavy rather than just a constant drizzle, it is often fed by an atmospheric river.


These are long, narrow columns of exceptionally dense water vapor stretching across the ocean—essentially rivers in the sky. A single major atmospheric river can carry a flow of water vapor roughly equivalent to 15 times the average flow at the mouth of the Mississippi River.


When a stalled weather pattern locks one end of this sky-river directly over a coastline or a valley, it acts like a stationary firehose. It will continuously dump massive amounts of moisture onto the same exact spot until the entire global wind pattern shifts enough to break the connection.


3. Persistent Summer Convection
In inland or tropical areas during the summer, a 15-day streak can happen without a massive storm system. If the local ground is highly saturated, the morning sun heats up the wet earth, causing rapid evaporation. This moisture rises, cools, and forms afternoon thunderstorms. The rain falls, soaks the ground again, and resets the exact same cycle for the next day.


Ultimately, weather patterns love to find a groove. Once a blocking pattern or a moisture conveyor belt locks into place, it takes a major shift in global air currents to finally push it away and bring back the sun.

## Gemini

Stanley Kubrick spent decades developing A.I. Artificial Intelligence (originally based on Brian Aldiss's 1969 short story Supertoys Last All Summer Long). He ultimately handed the project to Steven Spielberg in the mid-1990s because of a mix of technological limitations, personal creative blocks, and a fundamental belief about who was best suited to tell the story.



1. Technological Limitations (The CGI Gap)
Kubrick envisioned a highly advanced visual world for the film. In the 1970s and 1980s, the computer-generated imagery (CGI) required to make the synthetic world and the robot child David look completely convincing simply didn't exist.


He initially tried to create a real, fully functioning animatronic robot child to play David, but the results were stiff and uncanny. When Jurassic Park came out in 1993, Kubrick saw what Spielberg achieved with digital effects and realized the technology was finally catching up to his vision—but he still felt he wasn't the right director to spearhead a CGI-heavy production.


2. The Nature of the Story
The film is essentially a sci-fi retelling of Pinocchio, following a robot boy who desperately wants to become real so he can win back the love of his human mother.


Kubrick, famous for his detached, clinical, and cynical cinematic style, felt he struggled to capture the raw, emotional innocence required for the character of David. He believed his own approach might make the film too cold.


3. The "Director Exchange"
Kubrick and Spielberg had a close, decades-long friendship despite their completely different filmmaking sensibilities. In 1994, Kubrick officially asked Spielberg to direct A.I. while Kubrick would stay on as a producer.



Kubrick’s Reasoning: As Spielberg later recalled, Kubrick told him that the story was actually much closer to Spielberg's sensibilities than his own. Kubrick believed Spielberg could bring the necessary warmth, magic, and humanity to David's tragic quest, while Kubrick could guide the darker, philosophical framework from behind the scenes.






The Ultimate Outcome
Spielberg initially declined, encouraging Kubrick to direct it himself. However, after Kubrick passed away in 1999, his widow, Christiane Kubrick, and producer Jan Harlan approached Spielberg and asked him to complete the film as a tribute to Stanley.


Spielberg agreed, writing the screenplay himself based on Kubrick's extensive treatments and conceptual art. The resulting 2001 film became a fascinating hybrid of both directors' styles: capturing Spielberg's trademark emotional vulnerability alongside Kubrick’s dark, uncompromising vision of a dystopian future.

To see exactly what launchd has loaded versus what actually physically exists in your ~/Library/LaunchAgents directory, we can use a script.


Because launchd manages processes across both your specific user session (gui/ domain) and system-wide paths, it often has background tasks active that don't match up 1:1 with the files in your personal directory.


Here is a tool to audit this, along with instructions on how to handle the results safely.


The Audit Blueprint
Run this script in Terminal. It queries the active GUI service manager for your user ID, lists all loaded job labels, reads your local directory, and handles the comparison:


# 1. Grab all currently active launchd service labels for your GUI session
ACTIVE_AGENTS=$(launchctl print gui/$(id -u) | awk '/services = {/,/^}/' | awk '{print $NF}' | grep -v '}')

# 2. Grab all plists sitting physically in your user LaunchAgents folder
LOCAL_FILES=$(ls ~/Library/LaunchAgents 2>/dev/null | sed 's/\.plist$//')

# 3. Print out the discrepancies
echo "=== LOADED SERVICES NOT IN YOUR USER FOLDER ==="
for agent in $ACTIVE_AGENTS; do
    # Skip standard native Apple processes to avoid noise
    if [[ ! "$agent" =~ ^com\.apple\. ]]; then
        if ! echo "$LOCAL_FILES" | grep -qx "$agent"; then
            echo "Label: $agent"
        fi
    fi
done

* Global Agents (/Library/LaunchAgents): Third-party software designed for all users on the machine installs files here. Tools like Jump Desktop Connect, Google Software Update, or logitech drivers often choose this global path instead of your personal home directory.

* Transient App Processes: Some apps register services dynamically with launchd at runtime without writing a persistent .plist file to your drive.


To see if an orphaned label belongs to a global directory instead of your user space, check the machine-wide directory first:


ls /Library/LaunchAgents

launchctl bootout gui/$(id -u)/<INSERT-LABEL-HERE>
