## Gemini

Yes, they do—and a massive number of them. While most people know Mars, Incorporated for candy bars like Snickers and M&Ms, they are actually the largest provider of veterinary care in the world.


Through their Mars Veterinary Health division, they operate around 3,000 animal hospitals and clinics globally, covering about half of all corporate-owned veterinary clinics in the United States.


If you've taken a pet to a hospital in North America or Europe, there is a very high chance it was a Mars-owned facility. Some of their biggest brands include:



* VCA Animal Hospitals: Over 1,000 general, specialty, and emergency hospitals across the US and Canada (acquired in 2017 for $9.1 billion).

* Banfield Pet Hospital: More than 1,000 clinics, very frequently located inside PetSmart stores.

* BluePearl Specialty and Emergency Pet Hospital: A network focused primarily on 24/7 emergency care and specialized veterinary medicine.

* AniCura & Linnaeus: Large networks spanning hundreds of clinics across Europe and the UK.


They also own Antech Diagnostics, which runs the laboratory and imaging infrastructure that thousands of independent and corporate vet clinics use to process bloodwork and scans. All of this sits under their broader "Mars Petcare" umbrella, which also handles their massive pet food brands like Royal Canin, Pedigree, and Whiskas.

How did that happen? Seems weird

---

## Gemini

It does sound weird at first. You wouldn't expect the people who make Twix to be running the ER clinic down the street. But when you look at how the company evolved, it actually makes a lot of cold, calculating business sense.


The transition happened over decades, driven by a few distinct factors:


1. They were already a pet food giant
Mars isn't just a candy company; they've been in the pet business since the 1930s. They acquired major brands like Pedigree, Whiskas, and eventually Royal Canin and Iams. In fact, Mars makes more money from pet care than they do from chocolate. Moving from pet food to pet health was a natural stepping stone.


2. Vet clinics are incredibly stable businesses
While the market for candy bars can stagnate and people skip luxury goods during an economic downturn, they rarely skimp on healthcare for their pets. People view pets as family members and are willing to pay high prices for emergency care, diagnostics, and surgeries. This makes veterinary medicine incredibly stable and profitable.


3. A massive "Roll-Up" strategy
Mars didn't build thousands of clinics from scratch. They used their massive cash reserves to buy out existing corporate chains that had already consolidated smaller independent clinics:
































YearAcquisitionImpact2007Banfield Pet HospitalMars took full ownership of the chain, instantly embedding clinics inside hundreds of PetSmart locations.2015BluePearlBrought a massive network of 24/7 specialty and emergency hospitals under the Mars umbrella.2017VCA Animal HospitalsThe massive $9.1 billion buyout added nearly 800 hospitals and gave Mars control of Antech Diagnostics (the labs independent vets use).2018AniCura & LinnaeusExpanded their empire deep into Europe and the UK by buying the continent's largest clinic networks.
4. Changing industry demographics
For decades, the standard veterinary model was an independent vet owning a local practice, working 60-hour weeks, and eventually selling it to a younger vet when they retired.


But as vet school tuition skyrocketed, graduating veterinarians began entering the workforce with massive student debt. Fewer young vets could afford the millions of dollars required to buy a practice. Mars and other corporate buyers stepped into that gap, buying out retiring owners who had no independent buyers lined up.


By building this vertical ecosystem, Mars effectively owns the food the pet eats, the clinic they go to when they get sick, and the lab that processes their bloodwork.
