It is incredibly tight, and you are completely right—market rent and campus residence costs have climbed significantly, making that $1,446 figure feel completely out of touch with real-world expenses.


The reason for the gap is that the government uses a standardized, province-wide baseline rather than adjusting for specific campus housing rates or inflation spikes. They expect that baseline to cover rent, utilities, food, clothing, personal care, and local transport all at once.


Since residence alone is going to swallow up around $1,200 of that monthly allowance, here is how you can manage the difference:


1. The Total Funding Pool Cover
Keep in mind that the $1,446/month is just the formula they use to calculate your overall financial need. If your total calculated need (Tuition + Fees + Living Allowance) qualifies you for the absolute maximum funding limit, you will receive a lump sum of money.


If your total loan and grant payout ends up being higher than your actual tuition bill, the leftover money deposited into your bank account might give you a bit more breathing room than just a strict $1,446 monthly budget.


2. Check for the Disability Grant Top-Up
Because your application includes a disability status, you will be applying for the federal/provincial disability grants via the Schedule 4 form. If approved, the Canada Student Grant for Students with Disabilities provides up to $2,800 per year (allocating an extra $350 per month for an 8-month study period).


This grant money is explicitly designed to help cover the extra costs of being a student with a disability, but because it comes as a non-repayable grant on top of your standard loan calculation, it effectively acts as a buffer for your living costs.


3. Consider an Appeal Later if Needed
If you get your official award letter and the total amount is simply not enough to cover your University of Alberta residence contract, you can submit a Request for Review (Appeal) after your application is processed. While they rarely change the standard living allowance flat rate, they do sometimes make concessions if you can prove your mandatory campus housing costs are completely unavoidable.

## Gemini

Chocolate oat milk is a mixed bag when it comes to acid reflux, and for most people, it is more likely to trigger or worsen symptoms rather than help them.


Here is the breakdown of why it affects reflux, separating the oats from the chocolate:


The Problem: Chocolate
Chocolate is a notorious trigger for gastroesophageal reflux (GERD) for two main reasons:



* Lower Esophageal Sphincter (LES) Relaxation: Chocolate contains methylxanthines (specifically cocoa chemicals like theobromine and caffeine). These relax the valve-like muscle at the bottom of your esophagus, making it much easier for stomach acid to creep back up.

* High Fat Content: Cocoa butter and fats slow down digestion. A stomach that stays full longer puts more upward pressure on that esophageal valve.


The Positive: Oat Milk
Plain oat milk is generally excellent for reflux.



* Low Acid: It is highly alkaline compared to citrus fruits or coffee, meaning it won't irritate an already inflamed throat.

* Viscosity: It has a thick, smooth texture that can help coat the lining of the esophagus, providing a temporary soothing barrier against stomach acid.

* Fiber: Whole oats contain soluble fiber, which supports smooth digestion and prevents the stomach pooling that causes reflux.



The Verdict
While the oat milk base is soothing, the addition of chocolate and added sugars usually cancels out the benefits. If you are currently experiencing a flare-up, drinking it might cause a noticeable burn.


If you want a dairy-free milk that actively helps soothe reflux, switching to plain, unsweetened oat milk or almond milk (which is also highly alkaline) is a much safer bet.

First, define the function as $f(x) = 4x^3 + 3x - 2$. Because $f(x)$ is a polynomial, it is continuous over the entire set of real numbers, including the closed interval $[-2, 2]$.


Next, we evaluate the function at the endpoints:



* $f(-2) = 4(-2)^3 + 3(-2) - 2 = -32 - 6 - 2 = -40$

* $f(2) = 4(2)^3 + 3(2) - 2 = 32 + 6 - 2 = 36$


Since $f(-2)$ is negative $(-40)$ and $f(2)$ is positive $(36)$, the value $y = 0$ lies between $f(-2)$ and $f(2)$. According to the IVT, there must exist at least one value $c$ in the interval $(-2, 2)$ such that $f(c) = 0$, confirming the existence of a solution.
