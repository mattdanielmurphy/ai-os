## Gemini

In the table from Lesson 6: Transformations of Trigonometric Functions Part II, the notation $|a|$ and $|b|$ (which you might also see as $|a|$ or $|b|$) represents the Absolute Value.


The reason it is written that way in the formula for Amplitude and Period is to ensure the result is always a positive distance, regardless of whether the original number in the equation is negative.


1. For Amplitude ($|a|$)
The amplitude is the distance from the midline to the peak. Distances can't be negative.



* If your equation is $y = -2\sin(x)$, the graph is reflected (flipped), but the "height" of the wave is still 2.

* Using the absolute value ensures that even if $a = -2$, the amplitude is recorded as $2$.


2. For Period ($\frac{2\pi}{|b|}$)
The period is the horizontal length of one full cycle.



* If you have $y = \cos(-3x)$, the negative sign just means the graph is reflected horizontally.

* The "length" of the wave doesn't become negative; it is still $\frac{2\pi}{3}$. The absolute value symbols "wash away" the negative sign to give you a physical length.


In Short:
The double bars are a reminder that Amplitude and Period are physical measurements of the wave's size and "speed." Even if the function is being flipped or reflected (indicated by a negative sign), the size of the wave itself remains a positive value.


Think of it like a car: whether you are driving in drive or reverse, the speedometer (the magnitude) always shows a positive number.

## Gemini

It is a bit of a "math dialect" thing. While a single bar $|a|$ is the standard way to write absolute value, the double bars $|a|$ are technically the symbol for a Norm.


In most high school or introductory college textbooks, they are used interchangeably, but here is why your Lauzon Pre-calc 12 materials might be using them:


1. Emphasis on Magnitude
In trigonometry and physics, we often deal with vectors (quantities with both size and direction). The double bars $|v|$ specifically represent the magnitude (the length) of that vector.



* By using $|a|$ for Amplitude, the curriculum is emphasizing that you are measuring the "size" of the wave's displacement, regardless of whether it's positive or negative.


2. Distinguishing from Algebra
Sometimes authors use double bars to distinguish a functional parameter (like the $a$ or $b$ in a trig equation) from a simple negative number in a basic equation. It's a visual cue that says, "We are looking at the geometric property of this coefficient."


3. Pure Aesthetics
In some mathematical typesetting (like LaTeX), $|x|$ is simply used to make the vertical bars stand out more clearly when the formula is crowded with other symbols, fractions, or parentheses.



Bottom Line: For your purposes in this unit, $|a|$ means exactly the same thing as $|a|$. If you see $|b|$, just treat it as "make the number $b$ positive" so you can calculate the period correctly.



If you were to write $|a|$ on your test instead of $|a|$, your teacher would almost certainly accept it as correct; the double bars are just a more "formal" way of expressing the same idea.
