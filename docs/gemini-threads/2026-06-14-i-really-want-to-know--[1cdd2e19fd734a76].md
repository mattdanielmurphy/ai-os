## Gemini

It is a super logical guess given how endless and maze-like those halls look, but the reality behind Kane Parsons' (Kane Pixels) The Backrooms is actually the exact opposite.


Instead of relying on random procedural algorithms, Kane hand-built and meticulously designed every single room, hallway, and structural layout using Blender.


Here is exactly how he pulled off the look that sparked a massive internet phenomenon and caught the attention of A24 for a feature film adaptation.


Hand-Crafted Chaos, Not Algorithms
While video games based on the lore often use procedural generation to create infinite corridors, Kane wanted strict control over the pacing, framing, and narrative tension.



* The Blender Workflow: Every wall segment, pillar, and fluorescent light fixture was placed manually. He constructed specific "sets" inside the 3D software to match the exact choreography of the camera movements.

* The Illusion of Infinitude: To make the spaces feel completely endless without melting his computer's graphics card, he used clever level design. Hallways wrap around, open into massive drops, or terminate in tight corners that hide the boundaries of the actual 3D model.


The Visual Secret Sauce
The reason the original short film looked so unsettlingly real—convincing millions that it might be lost physical footage—comes down to his post-processing workflow rather than the 3D geometry itself.



1. The VHS Degreaser
After rendering the clean 3D animation out of Blender, Kane brought the footage into Adobe After Effects. He applied heavy layers of artificial degradation to mimic an old magnetic tape:



* Chromatic aberration (color bleeding at the edges)

* Heavy digital noise and analog grain

* Slight ghosting and frame jitter

* A strict 4:3 aspect ratio aspect stretch


2. Camera Tracking & Camera Shake
To ground the CGI, the camera couldn't feel like a smooth, floating virtual viewport. Kane tracked real-world movement to give it a human touch. He often recorded himself walking around his own house holding a physical camera or phone, then imported those exact shaky-cam motion paths into Blender to drive the virtual camera. When the character runs, trips, or pans frantically, you are seeing a direct translation of real human movement.


3. The Audio Environment
The visuals only do half the work. The oppressive atmosphere relies heavily on the audio design:



* The Hum: A low-frequency, slightly modulated buzz meant to replicate old, cheap fluorescent ballasts. It fills the silence and creates a baseline level of low-key anxiety.

* Foley Art: Kane meticulously recorded footsteps on different surfaces, cloth rustling, and heavy breathing, filtering them to sound like they were compressed through a cheap built-in camcorder microphone.


Transitioning to the Movie
Because everything was already built with standard cinematic workflows (hand-keyed layouts, scripted pacing, and deliberate lighting), the jump to a Hollywood scale with A24 fits perfectly into how he was already operating.


Instead of changing the philosophy from procedural to manual, the feature film production allows him to scale up the manual design—combining physical practical sets with expansive, high-fidelity CGI extensions to build out the scale of Level 0.

## Gemini

Yes, it is entirely digital once he drops into the maze! The only live-action part of that original 9-minute video is the very beginning.


The short starts with a group of teenagers in 1991 filming an amateur live-action skate/movie project in a real backyard and parking lot. Kane actually filmed his friends for that intro. But the exact second the cameraman stumbles backward, "no-clips" through the ground, and falls from the sky into that yellow room, the entire video transitions completely into a 100% CGI environment built inside Blender.


Because it was a single animator working on a home computer, he had to use a few clever tricks to maintain the illusion that a real person was walking around a physical space:



* The Invisible Transition: When the camera falls through the floor, Kane hides the seam between the real world and the 3D render with a sudden lens blur, a camera whip-pan, and heavy digital glitching. By the time the camera stabilizes, you are looking at a virtual room.

* The "Actor" is just a Camera: Since the video is entirely first-person POV, Kane never had to animate a main character walking, opening doors, or interacting with the environment. The camera is the actor.

* The Camcorder Filter Disguises the Flaws: Raw 3D animation can sometimes look "too perfect" or plasticky. By running the clean Blender render through intense VHS degradation filters in After Effects, the compression artifacts and digital grain naturally blurred out the digital edges, tricking the human brain into thinking it was looking at real-world camcorder tape.


As he expanded the series into a full narrative universe with subsequent videos, he started mixing in more live-action elements—like filming real people in green screens or hazmat suits and seamlessly compositing them into his massive 3D-rendered hallways. But for that very first viral video, everything past the 40-second mark is pure digital animation.

## Gemini

Ollama stores downloaded LLMs inside /usr/share/ollama/.ollama/models. Looking at your output, /usr is taking up 24 GB, which matches perfectly with a few large models being stored there.


The cleanest and safest way to remove these models is through Ollama itself, rather than deleting files manually.



1. List the Installed Models
First, see exactly what models are installed on your system:


ollama list

This will output a table showing the names and sizes of the models.


2. Remove the Unwanted Models
To delete a specific model, use the rm command followed by the model name from the list. For example, if you have llama3 or mistral that you don't need:


ollama rm llama3

(Replace llama3 with the actual name of the model you want to delete).


3. If Ollama Commands Fail (Due to 100% Disk)
Because the disk is completely full, the ollama service might be crashing or refusing to respond to the list or rm commands. If that happens, you can manually delete the model manifests and blobs to free up space.


Run this command to clear out Ollama's entire model cache directory:


sudo rm -rf /usr/share/ollama/.ollama/models/*

Note: This will delete all Ollama models. You will have to re-download (ollama run <model>) any models you actually want to keep later, but it will instantly free up massive amounts of space in your /usr partition.



4. Verify the Space is Free
After running either the ollama rm command or the manual rm -rf, check your disk space again:


df -h /

You should see /dev/sda1 drop well below 100%, allowing your system services to run normally again.
