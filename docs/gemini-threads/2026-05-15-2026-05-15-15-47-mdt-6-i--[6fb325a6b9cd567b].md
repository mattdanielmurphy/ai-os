## Gemini

The routing logic itself is sound, and a 256-sample buffer is more than enough to handle a default piano patch without breaking a sweat. The issue here isn't Logic Pro or your CPU headroom—it is almost certainly clock drift within your macOS Aggregate Device.


When you combine two separate physical audio interfaces into a single Aggregate Device, each interface relies on its own internal hardware clock (crystal oscillator) to determine exactly when samples are processed. Because no two hardware clocks run at the exact same physical speed, they will gradually drift apart over time.


After a few minutes of playback, the timing mismatch between the two devices causes the digital audio buffers to fall out of sync, resulting in a continuous stream of digital clicks, pops, or distortion—even when no instruments are being played.


How to Fix It
You can resolve this by forcing macOS to actively synchronize the two hardware clocks.



* Open the Audio MIDI Setup app on your Mac (located in Applications > Utilities).

* Select your Aggregate Device from the left sidebar.

* Identify the Clock Source drop-down menu at the top. Ensure one of your two interfaces is explicitly selected as the primary clock master.

* Look at the list of sub-devices below. You will see columns for your interfaces.

* Enable the "Drift Correction" checkbox for the secondary interface (the one that is not set as the Clock Source).


Enabling Drift Correction tells Core Audio to automatically resample the secondary interface's audio stream on the fly so that it stays perfectly in lockstep with the primary interface's clock, eliminating the accumulation of buffer errors that cause the clicking.


If Drift Correction is already turned on for the secondary device, try swapping which interface acts as the primary Clock Source to see if one has a more stable master clock than the other.


Did enabling drift correction clear up the clicking after a few minutes of idling?

## Gemini

That is a massive clue. Having Logic look at a solo hardware device for input and a separate Aggregate Device for output is a notorious recipe for clocking conflict.


Even though the "USB Audio CODEC" was part of the output aggregate, Logic was forced to open two completely separate core audio driver streams—one for input, one for output. Without a shared master clock between those two streams, they will drift, the buffers will mismatch, and the clicking will start after a few minutes. Bringing the input into the Aggregate Device so Logic only has to talk to one virtual device for everything is absolutely the right move.


Regarding your other settings:


The "I/O Safety Buffer" Location
Logic hides this setting depending on your version and view settings. If you don't see it right below the I/O Buffer Size, you need to ensure Advanced Tools are enabled (if you are on an older macOS/Logic version) or check under Logic Pro > Settings > Display > General to ensure your layout isn't filtering out advanced audio options. However, with your new aggregate setup, you likely won't need it.


Would changing Summing to 32-bit float help?
No, changing the summing precision won't affect or fix the clicking.


Summing precision (32-bit vs. 64-bit double precision) dictates the math Logic uses to combine multiple audio streams into a stereo mixdown.



* 64-bit summing provides an astronomically low noise floor when summing hundreds of tracks, but it requires slightly more CPU overhead to compute the extra bits.

* The clicking you are experiencing is a physical hardware buffer sync issue, not a mathematical rounding error or a CPU peak from audio summing. Leaving it at 64-bit is perfectly fine, and dropping it to 32-bit won't stop clock drift.


Give it a few minutes of idling or playing with your new unified Aggregate Device setup. If the clocks are going to drift, they usually give up the ghost within 5 to 10 minutes. How is the audio holding up now?
