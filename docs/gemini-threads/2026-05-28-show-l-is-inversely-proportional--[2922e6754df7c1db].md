## Gemini

When two variables are inversely proportional, it means that as one value increases, the other decreases at a rate that keeps their product constant.


In mathematics, this relationship between $L$ and $B$ is expressed as:


$$L \propto \frac{1}{B}$$


To turn this proportionality into an equation, we introduce a constant of proportionality, usually denoted as $k$:


$$L = \frac{k}{B}$$


Key Characteristics

* Constant Product: The equation can be rearranged to $L \times B = k$. No matter how the individual values change, their product remains the same.

* Graph Shape: If you were to plot this relationship, it would form a curve known as a hyperbola. As $B$ approaches zero, $L$ increases toward infinity, and vice versa.

The distinction between the "-ward" and "-wards" endings usually comes down to geography and grammar. While they are often interchangeable, there are subtle rules that govern their use.


The Regional Split
In general, American English prefers the version without the "s," while British English tends to use the "s" version for adverbs.

Grammar Rules
Regardless of where you are, there is one hard rule: Adjectives never take the "s." When the word modifies a noun, it must be the singular form.



* Correct: "The upward trend in stock prices."

* Incorrect: "The upwards trend in stock prices."


However, when used as an adverb (describing how something moves), both are technically correct, though regional preferences apply:



* "He looked upward." (Common in US)

* "He looked upwards." (Common in UK)



Meaning Nuances
In some contexts, the choice can change the "vibe" of the sentence:



* Directional vs. Metaphorical: In the US, "backward" often describes a physical direction ("He stepped backward"), while "backwards" is sometimes used to describe a state of being or an unconventional manner ("He knows the book backwards and forwards").

* Formal vs. Informal: The "-ward" version (without the "s") is generally considered more formal and is the standard choice for academic or professional writing in the United States.



Common Examples

* Toward vs. Towards: This follows the same pattern. "Toward" is the standard American form; "Towards" is the standard British form.

* Afterward vs. Afterwards: Again, "Afterward" is American; "Afterwards" is British.



Pro Tip: If you want to be safe in any professional setting (especially in North America), stick with the version without the "s". It works as both an adjective and an adverb, making it the more versatile choice.

## Gemini

Unlike the ward vs. wards distinction, which is a matter of regional style, the difference between anyway and anyways comes down to formal grammar versus colloquial speech.


The Short Answer
In any formal, professional, or academic writing, always use anyway.



* Anyway is the standard adverb.

* Anyways is considered an informal, colloquial variation.



The Breakdown

* 
Anyway (Standard): This is the correct form used to mean "regardless," "in any case," or to transition back to a main topic.



"The weather was terrible, but we went for a run anyway."
"Anyway, as I was saying before we got interrupted..."





* 
Anyways (Informal): While you will hear this constantly in casual spoken English across North America, it is generally considered grammatically incorrect in writing. It essentially functions as a slang variant used exclusively as a transition word in speech.






Why the "s" Doesn't Work Here
With words like upward/upwards, the "s" comes from an old adverbial suffix in Old English.


Anyway, however, is a combination of "any" and "way." Because "way" is a singular noun in this compound structure, adding an "s" doesn't follow the same historical linguistic rules—it's just a colloquial habit that caught on over time.



Bottom Line: If you're texting a friend or writing dialogue for a character, anyways is completely fine. If you're writing code documentation, an email to a client, or an essay, stick to anyway.
