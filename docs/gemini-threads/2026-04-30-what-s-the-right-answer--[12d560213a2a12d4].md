* 
The argument ($a$) must be greater than zero:
In your case, the argument is $(x - 3)$.
Therefore, $x - 3 > 0$, which simplifies to $x > 3$.




* 
The base ($b$) must be greater than zero and not equal to 1:
In your case, the base is $x$.
Therefore, $x > 0$ and $x \neq 1$.





To find the overall domain, you must satisfy all of these conditions simultaneously. Since any number greater than 3 is already greater than 0 and not equal to 1, the condition $x > 3$ is the most restrictive and determines the domain.

Actually, you have described the First and Third laws. Here is the standard ordering for Newton's Laws of Motion:



* First Law (Law of Inertia): An object will remain at rest or move at a constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force. (This matches the first part of your description).

* Second Law (Law of Acceleration): The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass ($F = ma$).

* Third Law (Law of Action-Reaction): For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. (This matches the second part of your description, such as the relationship between gravity and a normal force).

## Gemini

A common way to remember Newton's Three Laws is to think of them in a sequence that describes how an object starts, behaves, and interacts:


1. The First Law: Inertia (The "Lazy" Law)
"An object keeps doing what it's doing."
Think of it as a stubborn object. If it’s sitting on the couch, it wants to stay there. If it’s sliding across ice, it wants to keep sliding until something forces it to stop or change direction. It resists any change to its state of motion.


2. The Second Law: Force and Motion (The "Math" Law)
"Force equals mass times acceleration ($F = ma$)."
This is the only law with a direct formula. Think of this as the work law:



* If you want to move a heavy object (high mass) quickly (high acceleration), you need a lot of force.

* It defines exactly how much change happens when you push something.


3. The Third Law: Action and Reaction (The "Mirror" Law)
"Forces always come in pairs."
Think of this as symmetry. You cannot push something without it pushing back on you just as hard. If you push against a wall, the wall pushes back on your hand with the exact same amount of force. It’s like a reflection: every action has an identical, opposite reaction.

## Gemini

It is completely understandable why they feel equal—they are tightly woven together to describe a single, unified system of motion. However, their ordering is deliberate because they build on each other logically:



* First Law (The Definition): It sets the stage by defining what happens when no net force exists (an object keeps doing what it's doing). It establishes the "natural state" of motion.

* Second Law (The Measurement): Once you know that force is what changes that state, this law gives you the exact "how-to" to measure that change ($F = ma$). It provides the mechanics for what happens when a force is applied.

* Third Law (The Interaction): This explains where those forces actually come from in the real world. It clarifies that you cannot exert a force on an object without that object exerting one back.


The Logical Progression
Think of it like building a structure:



* 1st Law: You need a foundation (Inertia).

* 2nd Law: You need the building blocks and dimensions to calculate how to change things ($F=ma$).

* 3rd Law: You need to account for the structural integrity and the feedback (Action/Reaction) that makes the system work.


If you are looking at your current Physics 12 Lesson on Momentum, you'll see this logic in action. The text demonstrates how the Third Law is essentially the root for the Law of Conservation of Momentum. By looking at the forces between two objects, you derive that the total change in momentum must be zero.
