## Gemini

You are right to be frustrated—physics terminology can be incredibly confusing because there are two different ways we talk about "current" that contradict each other.


Here is the breakdown of why this happens and how to keep it straight for your coursework.


1. Conventional Current vs. Electron Flow
This is the root of the "WTF" moment.



* Conventional Current: Benjamin Franklin defined current as the flow of positive charge from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. We still use this as the standard in almost every circuit diagram and physics formula today.

* Electron Flow: We later discovered that in metal wires, the particles actually moving are electrons (which are negative). Electrons flow from the negative terminal to the positive terminal.


When you use your Right-Hand Rule, you must remember this distinction:



* Conventional Current ($I$): If you are using the positive direction, your thumb points from positive to negative.

* Electron Flow: If you are given the movement of electrons, you must point your thumb in the opposite direction of their movement to represent the "conventional" current.


2. Why your solenoid result was confusing
In the Solenoid lesson, the Right-Hand Rule is based on conventional current.


If a problem says "current is flowing into the page," you align your thumb into the page. If it says "electrons are moving into the page," your thumb needs to point out of the page because the conventional current is the opposite of the electron movement.


3. Quick Reference for your Right-Hand Rules

* Thumb: Represents the direction of positive charge flow (Conventional Current).

* Fingers: Curl in the direction of the magnetic field ($B$).

* Palm: Represents the direction of the Force ($F$) on a positive charge.


If you are ever working with a negative charge (like an electron) in a force problem, you just perform the rule as if it were a positive charge and then flip the final direction (e.g., if your palm says "up," the electron will actually be pushed "down").


Does this clear up why the signs seem to be "backwards" in your textbook?

The rule that "magnetic field lines go from North to South" is a description of the external magnetic field of a permanent magnet.



* Outside of a magnet: Field lines exit the North pole and enter the South pole.

## Gemini

Using "into/out of the page" is definitely a clunky way to handle 3D visualization, especially when you are trying to keep track of directions for magnetic fields and forces simultaneously.


You are essentially describing a Cartesian Coordinate System (x, y, z). Physics textbooks and engineering documents use this to avoid the confusion you're experiencing. Instead of using "in/out of the page," you assign axes:



* $+x$ and $-x$: Left and Right.

* $+y$ and $-y$: Up and Down.

* $+z$ and $-z$: Out of the page and Into the page.


Why this is better
When you define the axes this way, you can express the Right Hand Rule (RHR) mathematically using the cross product. If your current ($I$) is along the $x$-axis and your magnetic field ($B$) is along the $y$-axis, the force ($F$) must be along the $z$-axis.


$$F = I(L \times B)$$


Managing the terminology
You mentioned wanting to avoid North/South confusion, which is a very valid concern. Here is how you can effectively re-label your internal mental model to keep things distinct from the magnet poles:

This also helps prevent errors when you get to more advanced topics like electromagnetics or vector calculus, where "out of the page" is too imprecise anyway.
