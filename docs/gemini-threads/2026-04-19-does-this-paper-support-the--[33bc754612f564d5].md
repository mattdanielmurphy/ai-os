## Gemini

Yes, the paper you are viewing, Artificial gravity as a countermeasure for mitigating physiological deconditioning during long-duration space missions, provides strong support for the core physics and physiological claims in your statement.


Support for Physics Claims
The paper confirms that gravity can be replicated through rotation and explains the mechanical process behind it:



* Uniform Circular Motion: The article states that artificial gravity is achieved by the "steady rotation of all or part of the vehicle."

* Creation of Weight: It explicitly describes how centrifugation provides an "apparent gravity vector" where the resulting centrifugal force gives a crewmember the sensation of standing upright, much like on Earth.


Support for Physiological Claims
The paper corroborates your idea that the force is felt throughout the body, not just at the points of contact:



* Internal Support and Fluid Shifts: The research notes that artificial gravity provides a "broad-spectrum replacement" for Earth's gravity. It specifically mentions that it affects hydrostatic pressure, which influences the "circulation of blood to the head and from the lower extremities."

* System-wide Impact: The authors argue that this force stimulates the entire human system simultaneously—activating "antigravity muscles," stressing bones and the cardiovascular system, and stimulating the vestibular system (otoliths).


Technical Nuance: The Gravity Gradient
One detail the paper adds to your research is the gravity gradient. Unlike on Earth, where gravity is nearly uniform across your height, a short-radius centrifuge creates a noticeable difference in the magnitude of force between an astronaut's head and feet. This means that while the force is indeed transmitted through the whole body, the "weight" felt by the organs near the head might be significantly less than that felt by the legs.
