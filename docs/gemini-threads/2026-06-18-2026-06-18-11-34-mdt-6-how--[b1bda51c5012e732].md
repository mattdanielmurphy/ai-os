To pivot this exact 17g setup into an iced pour-over (often called Japanese Iced Coffee), we have to rewrite the brewing physics.


If you just brewed your hot recipe and poured it over ice, the melting ice would massively dilute the coffee, leaving you with a watery, weak cup. To fix this, you use a split-mass strategy: you swap out a large portion of the brewing water for solid ice cubes right in the carafe, and brew a highly concentrated hot coffee directly over them. The hot coffee melts the ice instantly, locking in the volatile aromatics while diluting the concentrate back to the perfect drinking strength.


Here is how to adjust the math and workflow for your 17g dose using a $1:13$ total ratio.



The Iced Coffee Formulation (17g Coffee / 225g Total Mass)
Instead of 225g of hot water, you split the weight roughly $40%$ ice and $60%$ hot water:



* Ice Mass (in the mug/carafe): 90g

* Hot Water Mass (in the kettle): 135g

* Kettle Temperature: Keep it at 90°C to protect those brittle decaf grounds.


Because you have significantly less hot water to work with, the Hoffmann-style multi-pour won't work—the water level would drop too fast and lose thermal mass. We collapse the recipe into a single main pour after the bloom to maintain heat and constant hydrostatic pressure.

Critical Mechanical Adjustments for Iced Brewing

* Grind Even Finer: Because you are passing only 135g of hot water through the coffee instead of 225g, the contact time is naturally cut in half. To counter this and force a proper extraction, you must tighten your J-Max grind size significantly finer than your hot pour-over setting. You want to create enough mechanical resistance to stretch that short 135g drawdown to around 2:00 to 2:15 total time.

* Swirl the Mug at the End: Once the drawdown finishes, remove the V60 and give the mug a vigorous swirl for 5 seconds. You want to ensure the last remaining slivers of ice melt completely, instantly chilling the beverage down to refrigerator temperatures and stabilizing the flavor.


It cuts down the brewing steps even further and gives you a crisp, incredibly clean iced cup without any of that oxidized cardboard bitterness popping through!

## Gemini

Yes, you can use an old Kindle as an e-ink display for a Raspberry Pi, but not as a direct, plug-and-play hardware monitor. Because Kindle screens lack standard video inputs (like HDMI or MIPI DSI) and run closed-source, proprietary display controllers, you cannot simply bridge the hardware gap with a ribbon cable or an adapter.


Instead, the community relies on software-driven workarounds—collectively known as "Kindleberry Pi" setups.


Here are the two primary ways to make it work, along with the technical reality of using one.



Method 1: The Terminal/SSH Mirror (Best for E-Ink Models)
If you have an older e-ink Kindle (like a Kindle 3 Keyboard, Touch, or Paperwhite), the most reliable method requires jailbreaking the Kindle to use it as a wireless terminal client.



* Jailbreak the Kindle: You must install custom firmware to open developer access on the device.

* Install a Terminal Emulator: Install an app like Kterm natively on the Kindle.

* Establish a Connection: Connect both the Pi and the Kindle to the same local network (or set the Pi up as a Wi-Fi hotspot).

* SSH and Multiplex: Use SSH from the Kindle to log into the Raspberry Pi. By running a terminal multiplexer like screen or tmux on the Pi, you can mirror your active terminal session onto the Kindle screen.



Note: If you want a full graphical UI rather than just a command line, you would have to look into VNC setups or tools like PaperTTY (a Python tool that renders the Pi’s framebuffer to a text/TTY environment), though compatibility varies heavily by Kindle model.



Method 2: The VNC/RDP Route (For Kindle Fire Tablets)
If your old Kindle is actually a Kindle Fire (which uses a standard LCD/LED screen, not e-ink), the process is completely different and much easier:



* Enable side-loading on the Fire tablet.

* Install a standard Android VNC or Remote Desktop (RDP) client.

* Run a VNC server on the Raspberry Pi and log in over Wi-Fi.



The Reality Check: Performance Limitations
Before diving into a jailbreak, keep these physical constraints in mind:



* Severe Refresh Latency: E-ink screens are designed for static text. Typing or moving a cursor will result in a noticeable delay (often 200 ms to 500 ms) as the screen struggles to refresh.

* Ghosting: Fast updates on an e-ink panel leave artifacts behind, requiring frequent full-screen flashes to clear the display.

* No Video/Animation: Don't expect to run a standard desktop GUI smoothly, watch videos, or run UI-heavy applications.


A Cleaner Alternative
If you love the concept of an e-ink cyberdeck or low-power terminal but want to avoid software headaches, look into dedicated e-paper HATs designed specifically for the Pi’s GPIO headers (such as those made by Waveshare or Pimoroni). They provide clean Python/C libraries and direct hardware interfaces without needing to hijack a locked-down consumer operating system.

## Gemini

Yes, you can absolutely do wireless updates. Depending on which method you used to connect the Kindle to the Raspberry Pi, you have a few ways to push updates to the system entirely over the air (OTA).


Here is how you handle updates for both the Raspberry Pi and the custom Kindle software without plugging either device into a computer.



1. Updating the Raspberry Pi (OTA)
Since the Raspberry Pi is a fully functional Linux computer connected to your local Wi-Fi, managing its software wirelessly is completely standard.



* The Core OS & Packages: If you are logged into the Pi via your Kindle terminal session (or an SSH client on another machine), you can run standard system updates:
sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y



* Unattended Upgrades: You can configure the Pi to automatically download and install security patches in the background. Installing the unattended-upgrades package handles this without requiring you to manually trigger it.

* Headless Deployment: If you make changes to your custom interface scripts or configuration files, you can push them from your main computer to the Pi wirelessly using scp, rsync, or by pulling updates directly from a remote Git repository.


2. Updating the Kindle Software wirelessly
Updating the software on the e-ink Kindle itself wirelessly is trickier because it is a locked-down client, but it depends on your specific setup:


Wireless Package Transfer (KUAL)
If you jailbroke the Kindle, you likely installed KUAL (Kindle Unified Application Launcher).



* To add new extensions or update binaries wirelessly, you don't need to plug in a USB cable.

* You can SSH directly into the Kindle (since the jailbreak enables an SSH server on the device) and use scp or an SFTP client to drop update files directly into the Kindle's /extensions folder over Wi-Fi.


The Downside of Official Kindle OTA Updates

⚠️ Important Warning: If your jailbroken Kindle connects to standard Amazon Wi-Fi servers, it may attempt to automatically download an official Amazon firmware update. If it succeeds, the official OTA update will likely strip your jailbreak and lock you out of your custom Raspberry Pi display setup.



To prevent this while keeping Wi-Fi active for the Pi connection, builders usually employ one of these tricks:



* Backing up the Jailbreak: Install the "Jailbreak Rescue" (RPi/MKK) packages which allow the jailbreak to survive an accidental OTA update.

* Blocking Amazon Domains: Configure your local network or router to block Amazon's firmware update servers while still allowing local IP communication between the Kindle and the Pi.

* Using a Closed Local Network: Connect both devices to a local router or a Wi-Fi hotspot hosted directly by the Raspberry Pi that has no external internet access at all.
