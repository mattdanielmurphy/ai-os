what's a solenoid?

---

## Gemini

In its simplest form, a solenoid is just a length of wire wound into a tightly packed coil, usually in the shape of a cylinder. While it looks like a simple spring, it acts as a bridge between electricity and magnetism.


How It Works
When you pass an electric current through the wire, it creates a magnetic field. Because the wire is coiled, the small magnetic fields from each individual loop add together, concentrating the force into a strong, uniform magnetic field running through the center of the coil.


Essentially, a solenoid allows you to create a temporary magnet (an electromagnet) that you can turn on or off with a switch.






Common Applications
Solenoids are everywhere because they are great at converting electrical energy into mechanical movement.



* Electromagnets: When current flows, the solenoid can pick up metallic objects.

* Actuators/Valves: In many machines, a solenoid is used to move a metal rod (a plunger) back and forth. This movement can open a water valve in a dishwasher, click a door lock into place, or engage the starter motor in your car.

* Inductors: In electronics, solenoids are used to store energy or filter signals in circuits.


Key Characteristics

* Uniformity: Inside the hollow center of the coil, the magnetic field is remarkably straight and consistent.

* Controllability: By changing the amount of current or the number of loops in the wire, you can precisely control how strong the magnet becomes.

* Polarity: As we discussed with the Right-Hand Rule, the direction of the "North" and "South" poles depends entirely on which way the electricity is flowing and the chirality of the wrap.


Since you’ve been looking at how two solenoids interact, you can think of them as "controllable bars" that can push or pull depending on how you wire them up.
