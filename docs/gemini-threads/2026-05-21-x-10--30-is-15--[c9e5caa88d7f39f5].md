## Gemini

Yes, that is correct.


An "order of magnitude" refers to a factor of 10. To find the difference in orders of magnitude between two numbers, you look at the difference between their exponents in scientific notation:


$$-15 - (-30) = 15$$


Since $10^{-15}$ is $10^{15}$ (one quadrillion) times larger than $10^{-30}$, $10^{-30}$ is 15 orders of magnitude smaller.

Written in 1923, this piece is raw, harsh, and deeply serious. Bloch utilizes quarter-tones (notes between the standard keys on a piano) in the strings to create an unsettling, eerie atmosphere. ## Gemini

In most academic contexts, "potential difference" ($V$) is treated as a scalar quantity where the sign indicates the nature of the work done or the relative change in energy, but when the word "between" is used, it typically refers to the magnitude ($\Delta V$).


The Directional Logic
If you were required to provide a sign, it would depend entirely on the "path" taken:



* Moving $4.0\text{ m} \rightarrow 1.5\text{ m}$ (Toward the negative charge): You are moving with the electric field lines. The potential becomes more negative, so $\Delta V$ is negative.

* Moving $1.5\text{ m} \rightarrow 4.0\text{ m}$ (Away from the negative charge): You are moving against the electric field lines (doing work). The potential becomes less negative, so $\Delta V$ is positive.


Why it’s often "Sign-Agnostic"
In the Summary Table (Reference R. 57067) of your notes, potential is defined as $V = \frac{kQ}{r}$. When a question asks for the difference "between" two points without specifying an initial and final state, it is asking for the "gap" on the number line.



The Verdict: Yes, it is technically ambiguous regarding the sign. However, in Physics 12, if a question uses the word "between," the expected answer is almost always the absolute value (positive). If they wanted a negative result, they would likely phrase it as "What is the change in potential for an electron moving from A to B?"



If you check the "Answer" button in that Practice #2 frame, you will likely see a positive value representing that magnitude.

Lesson 2 : 5.1 Electrostatics
Static Introduction
For more than two thousand years, people have observed situations where objects that are rubbed together may be attracted to, or repelled from each other. For example, the ancient Greeks noticed that when amber (which they called elektron) was polished, it attracted small objects. They thought this ability was due to some special power that the stone had. People wore amber jewellery because of this so-called “power.” If it attracted small objects, maybe amber would attract health, energy, or even love.


We now know this special power of amber is nothing more than static electricity. It is the same feature that causes a balloon to stick to a wall after you rub it on your hair. In this lesson, you will learn more about static electricity.


Charges in an Atom
An atom is the smallest part of any material that can't be broken apart by chemical reactions. An atom consists of three types of sub-atomic particles: proton, electron, and neutron. Because they are smaller than a single atom, they are called sub-atomic particles.


[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Electricity and the Atom]


Neutral atoms have equal numbers of protons and electrons resulting in a net zero charge on the atom. If an atom has unequal numbers of electrons and protons we refer to it as an ion. A large object with significantly greater numbers of electrons than protons is said to be negatively charged, and one with greater protons than electrons is said to be positively charged.

3 Ways to Create a Charge

* Friction: rub two objects that allow electrons to be transferred from one to the other. Some materials easily give up electrons, while some materials easily 'grab' electrons. Rub two opposing materials and you can expect electrons to move from one to the other.

* Conduction: touch a charged object to a neutral object, allowing the transfer of electrons. This creates a charge (spread out) over both objects.

* Induction: without touching, bring a charged object close to a neutral object. This forces the charges in the neutral object to become separated, creating opposing charges in different parts of the second object. Charge by Friction
Charges may be placed on certain objects through friction. Essentially what is done is that charged particles are rubbed off one object and onto another. As protons are located in the nucleus of an atom they are unaffected by the rubbing, rather it is electrons in orbital clouds around the nucleus which are rubbed off due to friction. These electrons become transferred onto the other object involved in the rubbing. The object losing electrons will now have more protons than electron, and results in a positive charge. The object gaining the electrons will have a surplus, resulting in a negative charge of equal but opposite sign as the positively charged object.

Lesson 3 : 5.2 Coulomb's Law
Coulomb's Law
We've observed that opposite charges attract and similar ones repel. The next question is "how much force is actually involved in these attractions and repulsions?" Basically, now that we have the directions of electrostatic forces sorted out, let's talk about magnitudes.


[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Coulomb's Law]


Coulomb's Law is expressed algebraically as:
$$F_e = \frac{k Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}$$
Where:



* $F_e$ = electrostatic force in Newtons

* $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ = the charges on the two objects involved

* $r$ = the distance between them

* $k$ = constant of value $9.0 \times 10^9 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2 / \text{C}^2$


Take a moment and note the similarity between this equation and the general equation of gravitational force.


Unit of Charge (e-)
Note that neutral (uncharged) objects are attracted by either positive or negative. This is because electrons in the neutral object are forced away leaving a positive side when touching a negative object. If a positive object touches a neutral one electrons are attracted from the far side of the neutral making the side touching the positive object negative.


Near the turn of the 20th Century Robert Millikan performed an experiment which determined the magnitude of the charge on an electron. This became known as the smallest unit of charge possible, and is equal but opposite in sign to the charge on the proton. An elementary charge has a value of $1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ coulombs (C)}$. These units are named after Charles Coulomb who originally attempted to find the same value in the 1700's but was limited by technology of the day. Charged objects often have multiple elementary charges, in the order of a billion billion, so commonly charges are measured in micro-Coulombs ($\mu\text{C}$). A micro-Coulomb is $10^{-6} \text{ C}$.

Lesson 4 : 5.3 Electric Fields
Electric Fields Lines
Much like talking about (g) as an gravitational field near the surface of the Earth to predict how this field impacts other masses, we can talk about Electric Fields. Electric fields describe how a charge will be impacted in a particular location.


[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Electric Field Lines 1]


A gravitational field always has its direction directed toward the mass creating the field. However electric fields have direction based upon the charge (+ or - ) creating them. As the electro-static force is defined as attraction if negative, the electric field is defined as toward a negative charge, and away from a positive charge.


Mixed Field Lines
Sometimes we have multiple charged points.


[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Electric Field Lines 2]


It is important to note that FIELD LINES NEVER CROSS EACH OTHER.


Formula #1
Let's remember that the gravitational force on an object (it's weight) is related to the object's mass and it's gravitational field at it's current location:
$F_g = mg$ (where $g$ = gravitational field at current location)


So, we could also determine the field strength using:
$$g = \frac{F_g}{m}$$


Similarly, the electrostatic force on an object is related to the object's charge and it's electric field it's current location:
$F_e = QE$ (where $E$ = electric field at current location)


So, we could also determine the field strength using:
$$E = \frac{F_e}{Q}$$
where;



* $E$ = the electric field, in N/C.

* $F$ = the electrostatic force, (vector) in Newtons, N

* $Q$ = the charge at the point in space where we are measuring the electric field in Coulombs, C


Directions
Fields are vector quantities (ie. they have direction). Gravitational fields are ALWAYS towards the center of the mass causing them. Electrical fields (like the field lines we drew earlier) are ALWAYS away from the positive charges causing them and/or towards the negative charges causing them).


Formula #2
For point charges (defined as spherical, uniform charges) we can use the equation:
$$F_e = \frac{k Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}$$
Plugging this into our new formula for electric fields:
$$E = \frac{F_e}{Q}$$
and cancelling out one of the charges since we're only dealing with one charge here, and we get:
$$E = \frac{F_e}{Q} = \frac{k Q Q}{r^2 Q} = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$$


So, the equation that we can take away and use for Electric Fields due to point charges would be:
$$E = \frac{k Q}{r^2}$$
Where:



* $E$ = electric field strength (N/C)

* $Q$ = charge creating the electric field (Coulombs, C)

* $r$ = distance between the charge $Q$ and the point where the strength of the field, $E$, is being considered (metres, m)


It relates to the force (Coulombs's law) easily as $F_e = E Q_2$


Faraday's Cage
A fascinating application of understanding charge distribution and electric field lines is the Faraday's Cage.


[VIDEO: WCLN - Physics - Faraday's Cage (or shield)]


Faraday's Cage works due to electric charges within the cage's conducting material to be distributed in a way that cancels out the field's line effects within the cage. This phenomenon is used to:



* protect sensitive electronic equipment

* protect people and equipment against charges

* minimize microwaves outside of devices

* protect data cables from distorting signals

* etc...



Lesson 5 : 5.4 Electric Potential Energy
Potential Energy (Ep)
We know that the conservation of energy is a very useful tool. Let's learn to apply it to electrostatic situations.


[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Electrical Potential Energy]


The formula for kinetic energy stays the same, but we need to adjust for different forces involved in work and potential energy.


Because a force ($F_e$) exists between electrically charged objects, that force has the ability to do work by moving itself or something else over a distance parallel to the direction of the force. As a result, charged objects must have energy (defined as the ability to do work), which may be calculated relative to being infinitely far away. That is to say that if two charged objects were infinitely far apart, the charges would not be able to influence one another and would have no potential to do work. In this manner, the potential electrical energy between two POINT CHARGES (spherical charged objects) which are some distance $r$ apart becomes a function of the charge, their separation, and a constant. We arrive at the equation for electrical potential energy:
$$E_p = \frac{k Q_1 Q_2}{r}$$
Where:



* $Q_1$ = Charge on one of the objects.

* $Q_2$ = Charge on the other object.

* $r$ = Distance between charged objects.


Voltage
An important concept in electrostatics and current studies is that of voltage.
Voltage = the potential energy per unit charge


Students are most familiar with the concept of voltage in the 1.5 volt batteries used in toys and portable stereos; the 1.5 volts is the amount of energy delivered by the battery — the amount of charge flowing out. Similarly if a point charge of value Q has some Ep at a distance, the voltage of that charge is:
$$V = \frac{E_p}{Q}$$
Where



* $V$ is voltage measured in volts (V),

* $E_p$ is potential energy measured in joules (J)

* $Q$ is charge measured in coulombs (C)


Solving for $E_p$ gives $E_p = VQ$, this can be combined into the law of conservation of energy, as $VQ$ replaces any term with $E_p$. You can use this creatively to solve a variety of problems.


Scalar or Vector?
Electric Fields are vector quantities (ie. they have direction). $E_p$ and $V$ are scalar quantities (ie. they don't have direction). So, why are $E_p$ and $V$ negative sometimes then?? The negative associated with $E_p$ and $V$ don't indicate direction. They indicate a decrease or loss in energy or voltage. Thus, they aren't vectors!



Lesson 6 : 5.5 Voltage
Voltage
Let's look at voltage now. We'll find that it's very similar to electrical potential energy, but it's important to distinguish them.


[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Voltage 1/3 - Intro]


To distinguish them, consider that:
Voltage = potential energy per unit of charge
Another way to consider it is that it is:
Voltage = the amount of energy available to do work on individual charges


Voltage is independent of charge, but the actual amount of work done does depend on the charge of the object passing through the potential difference. From this comes the equation:
$$\Delta V = \frac{\Delta E_p}{Q}$$
Note that $\Delta V = V_2 - V_1$. If $V_1$ is zero, then $\Delta V$ just becomes $V$. And if we incorporate our formula for $E_p$, we get:
$$V = \frac{k Q_1 Q_2}{r Q} = \frac{k Q}{r}$$

[VIDEO: .WCLN - Physics - Voltage 2/3 - In a Uniform Field]

CRT (Cathode Ray Tube)
One important invention in the 20th century was the cathode ray tube (CRT). This is a device which sends a ray of electrons from a negative source (cathode) through a high voltage to a screen; the electrons then cause the screen to glow at the area of impact.


[VIDEO: WCLN - Physics - CRT Intro]

Design Analysis
As you might have figured out, the CRT is an application of the electrostatics from previous lessons in this unit.


[VIDEO: WCLN - Physics - CRT Design Analysis]

$F_{\text{net}} = ma$
$EQ = ma$
$\frac{qV_d}{d} = ma$
