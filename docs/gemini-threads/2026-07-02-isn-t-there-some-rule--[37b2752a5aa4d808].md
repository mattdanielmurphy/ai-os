what IS tides

---

## Gemini

Based on the Mutable Instruments Tides (2018) manual, Tides is a versatile Eurorack synthesizer module described as a "tidal modulator." At its core, its entire functionality revolves around a single, simple concept: ebb and flow (a voltage goes up, and a voltage goes back down). By manipulating how this voltage rises and falls, Tides can transform into several completely different utility and modulation sources for a modular synthesizer system.


Here is what Tides can become depending on how you configure it:


1. Modulation Source (LFO or Envelope)

* Cyclic Mode: If you make the ebb and flow repeat continuously, it functions as a highly customizable LFO (Low Frequency Oscillator) or an audio-rate VCO (Voltage Controlled Oscillator).

* One-Shot Mode: If it only executes the cycle once in response to an incoming trigger, it acts as a unipolar AD (Attack/Decay) or AR (Attack/Release) envelope generator.


2. Multi-Output Signal Processor
Tides features four distinct outputs, and the relationship between them is determined by its Output Mode. It can serve as:



* A waveshape splitter: Outputting four different variations of the waveshape taken from different stages of its signal processing chain.

* A router/crossfader: Dynamically shifting the signal across the four outputs.

* A phase/time shifter: Shifting the peak of the waveform across the outputs in time (creating phase-shifted LFOs or varied envelope shapes).

* A frequency divider/multiplier: Running the four outputs at different rhythmic ratios (for poly-rhythmic LFOs) or musical intervals (to create just-intonation chords at audio rates).


3. Core Controls
Instead of traditional, separate "Attack" and "Decay" controls, Tides uses a streamlined approach to shape its waveforms:



* Frequency: Sets the total time it takes for the entire cycle to complete.

* Slope: Adjusts the balance of that time between the ascending (attack) phase and descending (decay/release) phase.

* Shape: Alters the curvature of the waveforms.

* Smoothness: Features a dual action—turning it counter-clockwise acts as a low-pass filter to smooth out edges, while turning it clockwise acts as a wavefolder to inject complex harmonic kinks and bumps into the signal.


It also supports External Clocking, meaning you can patch a clock signal into it to perfectly synchronize its LFOs, divide/multiply tempos, or track incoming pitches cleanly when running at audio rates.

## Gemini

It's completely fair—wrapping your head around what four independent-looking jacks are doing simultaneously can get confusing fast.


Instead of thinking of Tides as a single generator, it helps to think of it as a factory that produces a master waveform, and the four Output Modes just change how that master wave is sliced, diced, or distributed across the four jacks.


Here is the plain-English breakdown of what happens when you turn that SHIFT/LEVEL knob in each mode:



Mode 1: Different Shapes (LED Off)

* What it does: The module takes the master waveform and runs it through different internal processing stages at the same exact time.

* The Outputs: Each jack gives you a completely different flavor of the wave. You get the raw slope, a smoother version, a sharper version, etc.

* What the Shift knob does: In this specific mode, the knob just acts as a standard attenuverter (volume/amplitude control) for the primary output.


Mode 2: Crossfading / Routing (LED Lit)

* What it does: It takes your main waveform and plays a game of telephone across the four jacks.

* What the Shift knob does: Turning the knob smoothly morphs and moves the signal from one jack to the next.

* If the knob is all the way left, the signal only comes out of Output 1.

* Turn it slightly, and it crossfades so the signal is splitting between Outputs 1 and 2, and eventually moves entirely down the line to Output 4. It’s perfect for sending a single modulation source to different modules over the course of a patch.





Mode 3: Time / Phase Shifting (LED Lit)

* What it does: All four jacks output the exact same waveform shape, but they don't peak at the same time.

* What the Shift knob does: It controls the delay or "phase shift" between the jacks.

* In Cyclic (LFO) mode, it staggers the waves in time. While Output 1 is peaking, Output 2 is halfway up, Output 3 is at the bottom, etc. (Great for creating cascading, organic modulation).

* In One-Shot (Envelope) mode, it alters the Attack/Decay slope differently for each jack so they reach their apex at staggered intervals.





Mode 4: Frequency Ratios / Clock Division (LED Lit)

* What it does: It changes the speed of outputs 2, 3, and 4 relative to Output 1.

* What the Shift knob does: It sets the multiplication or division math.

* At 12 o'clock: All four outputs run at the exact same speed.

* Turn Clockwise: Outputs 2, 3, and 4 speed up at different ratios. At audio rates, this instantly tracks as musical intervals (chords).

* Turn Counter-Clockwise: Outputs 2, 3, and 4 slow down. At LFO speeds, this creates perfectly synchronized poly-rhythmic modulation.





As the manual note mentions, if you are using this fourth mode as a slow LFO and turn the SLOPE way down, those shifting outputs will create sharp, sudden voltage drops—sharp enough that you can use them as rhythmic triggers to fire off other envelopes or drum modules in your rack.

## Gemini

To get a set of constantly evolving LFOs with minimal effort, you want to put Tides into Mode 3 (Phase Shifting) or Mode 4 (Frequency Ratios). Both modes instantly give you four synchronized LFO outputs from just a single core rate setting, meaning they naturally stay musically or rhythmically related while doing their own thing.


Here is the easiest way to patch it for maximum movement:


1. The Mode 4 Approach: Poly-rhythmic Drift (LED Red)
This is usually the most satisfying method for complex, self-changing modulation because it splits your outputs into different speeds.



* The Setup: Set the central frequency to low/modulations, switch the output mode to Red, and set the SHIFT/LEVEL knob somewhere around 10 o'clock or 2 o'clock. Output 1 will run at your main speed, while Outputs 2, 3, and 4 will run at slower or faster rhythmic ratios.

* The "Always Changing" Trick: Take one of those outputs (say, Output 4) and patch it right back into the SLOPE or SHAPE CV input on Tides itself. Because the outputs are running at different speeds, the module will constantly alter its own waveform curves over time, preventing the modulation from ever sounding like a predictable, repeating loop.


2. The Mode 3 Approach: The Quad-Phase Wave (LED Amber)
If you want all your LFOs to move at the exact same speed but never peak at the same time, use Phase Shifting.



* The Setup: Set the output mode to Amber and turn the SHIFT/LEVEL knob to stagger the waves.

* The Movement: If you modulate the SHIFT CV input using a tiny bit of external random voltage (like a smooth random wave from a Marbles or a subtle LFO from another module), the phase relationship between the four outputs will constantly float and drift. One LFO will slow down its peak while another speeds up, creating a massive, breathing web of modulation across your rack.


Bonus: Quick "Set and Forget" Settings

* Shape & Smoothness: Set both the SHAPE and SMOOTHNESS knobs near 12 o'clock to start with smooth, clean curves.

* Slope: Keep SLOPE away from the extreme edges (like straight 7 o'clock or 5 o'clock) if you want smooth, rolling LFOs. Extreme settings create sharp, immediate drops that behave more like sudden gates or triggers than smooth modulators.

how can I take a signal and modulate it so it has a certain min/max range

---

## Gemini

To clamp, shrink, or shift a voltage signal so it fits perfectly within a specific minimum and maximum range, you need a utility process called attenuation, inversion, and offsetting (often referred to in Eurorack as an Attenuverter + Offset utility).


Because Eurorack signals can be chaotic—like a bipolar LFO swinging from $-5\text{V}$ to $+5\text{V}$, while your target filter input only wants $0\text{V}$ to $+8\text{V}$—you need to manipulate both the width (gain) of the wave and its center point (position).


Here is the exact step-by-step logic of how to reshape your signal, along with the modules that make it easiest:



The 3-Step Math Formula
To scale any signal to a specific min/max range, you use three classic utility functions in order:



* Attenuate (Scale the Width): Shrink or stretch the total peak-to-peak amplitude of the original wave until its total span matches your target span.

* Example: If your LFO goes from $-5\text{V}$ to $+5\text{V}$ (a $10\text{V}$ total span) but you want it to ultimately range from $0\text{V}$ to $+4\text{V}$ (a $4\text{V}$ total span), you must attenuate the signal down to $40%$ of its original size.




* Invert (Flip if needed): If you want the signal to move downwards when the original goes upwards, run it through an attenuverter to flip the polarity.

* Offset (Shift the Position): Add or subtract a constant DC voltage to slide the entire reshaped waveform up or down the voltage scale until it hits your exact min and max targets.



Best Modules for the Job
Instead of doing math in your head, these modules give you dedicated, visual control over your signal ranges:


1. The Visual Master: ALM Busy Circuits - Tangle Quartet / Pip Slope / O/A/x2
Modules like the ALM O/A/x2 or a dedicated dual attenuverter give you a knob for scale (gain) and a knob for offset (position). You patch your LFO in, watch your target module, and tweak until it hits the sweet spot.


2. The Golden Standard: Mutable Instruments - Blinds (or clones)
If you want to modulate the min/max range dynamically using another CV source, Blinds is a quad scale/offset utility. It allows you to use a second control voltage to widen, shrink, or flip your primary signal on the fly.


3. The Utility Swiss-Army Knife: Make Noise - Maths
Channels 2 and 3 of Maths are designed specifically for this.



* Patch your signal into the input of Channel 2.

* The Channel 2 knob acts as an attenuverter (scale).

* If you plug nothing into Channel 3, its knob generates a pure DC offset voltage.

* Patch the output of Channel 2 and Channel 3 into a multi or a mixer channel together, and the combined output will be your perfectly scaled and shifted wave.


4. The Exact Precision Tool: Intellijel - Triplatt or Quadratt
These compact 1U or 3U modules let you switch each channel between unipolar (attenuation) and bipolar (attenuversion) modes, with a normalization trick that turns empty inputs into offset generators. It's the cleanest way to manually clamp a signal to a rigid window.

* Sequence the Arp "Count" parameter instead: If you are modulating the Arp settings via CV, you can route your CV to the Count parameter.
