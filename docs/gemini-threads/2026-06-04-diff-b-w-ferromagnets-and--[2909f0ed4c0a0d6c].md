diff b/w ferromagnets and electromagnets:
ferromagnets are permanently charged, are charged as a result of the properties of the material itself; the magnetic domains are aligned microscopically (on the quantum level: by their spin and orbitally), an example is a bar magnet.
Pros: no electricity required; cons: strength can't be modulated, can't be turned off, limited strength.

An example is a junkyard magnet that lifts cars.
Cons: requires electricity, probably more laborious to build and complex than a bar magnet; Pros: can be modulated in strength, can be switched on/off at will, can be made to be extremely strong.

Your breakdown is solid, accurate, and hits the core functional differences perfectly. Your breakdown of the pros and cons is spot on for both types.


Regarding your observation about the microscopic vs. macroscopic distinction: you have excellent intuition here. You are completely right that an individual electron flowing through a copper wire is subatomic, not something you can see with the naked eye.


However, the term macroscopic actually does apply to electromagnets, just not because of the size of the electron itself. Here is why that distinction is used, and how you can tweak your explanation to make it perfectly precise.



Why "Macroscopic" is Used for Electromagnets
In physics, the distinction isn't about the size of the particles, but rather the scale of the phenomenon causing the magnetism:



* Ferromagnets (Quantum/Microscopic Scale): The magnetism originates entirely from internal, fundamental particle properties—specifically the intrinsic spin and orbital angular momentum of electrons bound inside the atoms.

* Electromagnets (Macroscopic Scale): The magnetism is generated by a bulk current—trillions of electrons moving together through a large-scale physical structure (the wire/solenoid) that exists in our everyday macroscopic world.


Refining Your Text
To make it clear and avoid the confusion about electron size, you can shift the focus to intrinsic properties vs. external currents.


Here is a quick way to polish those specific sentences:



Ferromagnets generate a magnetic field due to the intrinsic quantum properties of the material itself, where the spins of bound electrons permanently align within microscopic magnetic domains.


Electromagnets generate a magnetic field via an external electrical current flowing through a bulk conductor (like a wire or solenoid).



One Small Technical Detail to Add
If you want to make the comparison even tighter, it's worth noting that most powerful electromagnets actually combine both principles.


A standard junkyard electromagnet isn't just an empty solenoid coil; it has a ferromagnetic core (usually iron) inside the wire loops. When the current turns on, its macroscopic field forces the microscopic domains of the iron core to align instantly, multiplying the total magnetic strength thousands of times over. When the current stops, the domains randomize again, and the magnet turns off.
