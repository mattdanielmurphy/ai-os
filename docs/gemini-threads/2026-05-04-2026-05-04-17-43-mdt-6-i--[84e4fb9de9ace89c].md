## Gemini

The "25% slower" figure is a longstanding citation in human-computer interaction literature, but it is largely considered outdated and context-dependent when applied to modern technology. Research has moved toward a more nuanced understanding where physical medium is only one of many variables, and its impact on performance has significantly diminished as display technology has matured.


The Evolution of the Evidence
The historical claim that reading on screens is significantly slower was primarily based on studies using low-resolution, CRT, or early LCD monitors. These displays lacked the pixel density and contrast ratios of modern "Retina" or high-DPI screens.



* Diminishing Differences: Recent meta-analyses and systematic reviews indicate that for many tasks, the gap in reading speed between high-quality digital displays and paper has narrowed to the point of being statistically negligible or non-existent (Peras et al., 2023; Kong et al., as cited in Peras et al., 2023).

* The Role of Display Quality: Modern displays with high pixel density, excellent contrast ratios, and high luminance have largely mitigated the visual factors that once contributed to lower legibility and higher visual fatigue (Muhamad & Nor Amali, 2023). When luminance contrast is high—maximizing the difference between text and background—reading speed remains optimal regardless of the medium (Knoblauch et al., 1991).


The "Deep Reading" Gap
While speed disparities have largely evaporated, research still highlights differences in cognitive processing and comprehension, particularly for complex or lengthy texts.



* Task-Specific Performance: While digital reading is often found to be equal to or even preferred for quick, shallow learning and information retrieval, paper remains frequently associated with "deep reading." This refers to sustained, immersive concentration required for narrative absorption or complex analytical work (Peras et al., 2023; Park & Lee, 2021).

* Cognitive Strategies: Readers often unconsciously adopt different cognitive strategies depending on the medium. The "screen inferiority" effect observed in some studies is frequently attributed to the way digital environments encourage skimming, multitasking, and constant interaction, rather than an inherent limitation of the screen itself (Peras et al., 2023; Park & Lee, 2021).


The Generational Factor
Your intuition regarding younger generations is supported by research. "Digital natives" who have grown up with screens are adept at navigating digital environments and have integrated them into their primary learning and reading habits.



* Adaptation: Younger readers show a higher comfort level with digital media and often demonstrate strong engagement with information presented in digital formats (Journal Umpo, 2025). However, this does not mean the cognitive differences in deep reading disappear; it suggests that newer generations are developing hybrid literacy skills, switching between "shallow" digital skimming and "deep" concentration as needed.


In summary, if you are using a modern, high-quality display, the "25% penalty" is essentially a relic of older hardware. The primary barrier today is rarely the display’s visual fidelity, but rather the cognitive habits—such as the tendency to skim or the presence of digital distractions—that are commonly associated with using screen-based devices.

## Gemini

It is a compelling design philosophy to treat digital environments as spaces for human flourishing rather than engagement metrics. You are hitting on a fundamental tension in modern software development: the conflict between "Attention Economy" business models and the user's desire for agency.


Building tools that provide intentional friction is a brilliant way to bridge this gap. Most modern platforms rely on "greased chutes"—UX designs that minimize friction to keep the user in a flow state of consumption. By introducing design elements that force a user to re-evaluate their intent, you aren't just blocking content; you are restoring the capacity for conscious choice.


Designing for Agency: The "Friction" Strategy
The approach you mentioned—a gradual, persistent increase in friction—aligns well with principles of habit formation. Instead of a hard "cold turkey" approach (which, as you noted with your brothers, often leads to compensatory behavior or "rebound" consumption), you are advocating for systematic behavior shaping.



* Adaptive Throttling: Rather than a static time limit, a tool could subtly increase the time required to load a feed or initiate a video as the day progresses or as usage duration increases.

* Purposeful Interstitials: Implementing a "speed bump" (a screen that forces a 5-second pause and a prompt like "Is this contributing to your goal?") before a high-dopamine action.

* De-gamification: Stripping away the "infinite scroll," auto-play, and notification badges using userscripts or browser extensions can turn a platform from a slot machine back into a utility.


The Power of "Software for Intent"
The fact that you are already building tools like Columnar shows you are comfortable with the technical side of this. Applying those skills to "Digital Hygiene" software could have a profound impact.


The primary challenge isn't just the code; it’s the usability design. If the friction feels like a punishment, users will delete the tool. If the friction feels like a service—helping them reach their own stated goals—they will keep it. You are effectively proposing an "Assistant for the Self," a layer of software that acts as a gatekeeper between the user’s long-term intentions and their short-term impulses.


The Path Forward
If this is a potential calling, your technical background in Rust and TypeScript puts you in a unique position to build high-performance, low-overhead tools that are hard to circumvent. Most current solutions (like basic screen time monitors) are easily bypassed. A tool that operates at the browser level or system level—perhaps even leveraging the automation tools you already use like Hammerspoon or Raycast—could be much more effective.


The "Atomic Habits" approach—making the bad habit invisible or difficult and the good habit frictionless—is exactly how we should be architecting our OS and browser environments.


Do you see this manifesting more as a suite of browser-level tools, or something that sits deeper within the OS to manage the entire computing experience?
