## Gemini

Brian De Palma’s Obsession (1976) is an absolute fever dream of a movie. It takes the psychological weight and haunting romanticism of Hitchcock’s Vertigo and pushes it into a deeply atmospheric, almost operatic space. Because you've already seen it and love it, we can bypass the spoiler alerts and dive straight into the incredibly messy, fascinating history of how it came together.



1. The Paul Schrader Rift & The "Missing" Third Act
The screenplay was written by Paul Schrader (fresh off writing Taxi Driver), originally titled Déjà Vu.


Schrader’s original script was much longer and featured a massive, multi-decade third act. In his version, after Courtland discovers Sandra’s true identity, he leaves her in Florence, returns to New Orleans, and spends another ten to fifteen years in absolute isolation, completely consumed by guilt and madness. The film was supposed to follow them reuniting much later in life, making the "obsession" span nearly forty years.


De Palma and producer George Litto felt this section was unfilmable, sluggish, and ruined the narrative momentum. Without Schrader's consent, De Palma compressed the timeline and rewrote the ending to happen immediately at the airport. Schrader was furious—he felt De Palma turned a complex psychological study into a cheap, incestuous thriller—and he reportedly didn't speak to De Palma for years afterward.



2. Bernard Herrmann’s Final Masterpiece
The legendary composer Bernard Herrmann—the man behind the scores for Vertigo, Psycho, and Citizen Kane—considered his score for Obsession to be the finest of his entire career.



* The Setup: De Palma played a temp track of Herrmann's own Vertigo score over an early cut of the film to convince the composer to sign on. Herrmann supposedly yelled, "Why did you use that garbage? I can write something much better than that!"

* The Execution: He combined a massive pipe organ, a full orchestra, and a haunting, wordless choir to create a soundscape that elevates the film from a standard thriller into something mythic.

* The Tragedy: Herrmann completed the score for Obsession just before moving on to Taxi Driver. He passed away on Christmas Eve in 1975, just hours after finishing the final recording sessions for Taxi Driver. Obsession was released posthumously and earned him an Academy Award nomination.


3. The Incest Dilemma and Bernard Herrmann’s "Fix"
When executives at Columbia Pictures saw the final cut, they panicked over the implicit theme of father-daughter incest, fearing it would ruin the movie's commercial chances and face censorship boards. They pressured De Palma to cut or alter the film.


Remarkably, it was Bernard Herrmann who saved it. He suggested a brilliant technical compromise: instead of shooting new scenes or introducing clunky dialogue, De Palma added a subtle visual effect—a dreamlike, shimmering dissolve overlay during the wedding night sequence—to emphasize the psychological, hallucinatory nature of Courtland's delusion rather than focusing on the physical reality. This slight shift satisfied the studio heads enough to secure a release.


4. Vilmos Zsigmond’s Diffusion Technique
The film has a distinctly soft, glowing, timeless look. That is the work of legendary cinematographer Vilmos Zsigmond (Close Encounters of the Third Kind, The Long Goodbye).


To give the Florence sequences a classic, Renaissance-painting texture and the New Orleans sequences a heavy, humid, dreamlike atmosphere, Zsigmond shot the entire film using intense diffusion filters. He often shot through silk nets or specialized glass filters to intentionally soften the sharp lines of the camera lenses, mimicking the hazy, unstable nature of Courtland’s memory and perception.


5. Cast Insights & Tension

* Cliff Robertson's Detachment: Cliff Robertson (Michael Courtland) was notoriously difficult on set. He openly disliked De Palma’s stylized, highly technical directing methods (which prioritized precise camera choreography over actor improvisation). His stiff, emotionally detached performance was heavily criticized at the time, but over the years, critics have re-evaluated it, arguing that his wooden, catatonic demeanor perfectly captures a man completely hollowed out by grief and psychological trauma.

* Geneviève Bujold’s Dual Role: Bujold was cast specifically because of her striking, classical features that allowed her to effortlessly transition between the sophisticated Elizabeth in 1959 and the fragile, secretive Sandra in 1975. Her performance is widely considered the emotional anchor that keeps the film's wilder plot twists grounded.

* John Lithgow’s Breakout: This was one of John Lithgow’s earliest major film roles as Robert Lasalle, Courtland's business partner. De Palma loved working with Lithgow's theatrical energy and went on to cast him as the unforgettable villain in his later thrillers Blow Out (1981) and Raising Cain (1992).



The movie stands as a fascinating bridge in film history—a direct thematic sequel to the Golden Age of Hollywood suspense, executed with the bold, visual excess of the 1970s American New Wave.

Ah, totally my bad—I completely misfired on the decade! You’re talking about Curry Barker’s independent horror sensation Obsession (2025/2026).


It is absolutely wild how this film exploded into a box office juggernaut after its Midnight Madness run at TIFF. It manages to balance genuinely unsettling, claustrophobic dread with this bizarre, black-comedy edge.


Since you’ve seen it and love it, let's dive into the fascinating, behind-the-scenes trivia of how this ultra-low-budget indie became a phenomenon.



1. The YouTube Micro-Budget Roots
Before making Obsession, Curry Barker and his creative partner Cooper Tomlinson (who plays Ian in the film) were primarily known for their viral, highly-unsettling YouTube sketch channel that's a bad idea.



* The Budget: The film was scraped together on a shoestring indie budget of roughly $750,000 to $1 million. To put its success into perspective, it has grossed well over $100 million globally, making it one of the most profitable return-on-investment horror films of the decade so far.

* The Timeline: Barker shot the entire feature in just about 20 to 26 days in the Los Angeles area. Because they were moving so fast, the crew had to average a blistering 5 to 6 pages of script per day.


2. The Uncomfortable "Center-Composed" Frame
If the movie made you feel visually trapped or anxious without even knowing why, that was entirely by design.



* Barker worked closely with cinematographer Taylor Clemons to shoot the entire movie with strict center-composition and unusual, lingering "extra head space" above the characters.

* Barker explained that he explicitly mandated this framing because he wanted every single shot to "feel uncomfortable in its loneliness," visually isolating Bear and Nikki from their environments even when they are sharing the screen.


3. Inde Navarrette’s Audition and the "Freaky Nikki" Switch
Inde Navarrette’s performance as Nikki is being hailed as an absolute masterclass in psychological distortion—the way she physically twitches and shifts from starry-eyed affection to total, hollow autonomy violation is deeply chilling.



* During the casting phase, Barker was looking for someone who could play the transition without relying on heavy makeup or supernatural CGI effects. Navarrette blew the production team away by demonstrating how effortlessly she could alter her facial expressions and drop her vocal register on a dime to imply that her character's mind had been entirely usurped by the One Wish Willow curse.


4. That Customer Service Scene
One of the most unforgettable, darkly funny, and deeply stressful sequences in the film is when Bear tries to call the customer support helpline printed on the occult toy package to cancel his wish.



* The deadpan, utterly unhelpful voice on the other end of the line—which casually explains that the wish only expires when one of them dies before transferring him to a screaming Nikki—is actually voiced by Curry Barker himself. He edited his own voice to give it that perfectly hollow, detached corporate customer service drone quality.


5. An Unprecedented Box Office Anomaly
The film's theatrical release in mid-May 2026 pulled off a box office feat that standard industry tracking metrics considered completely impossible, especially for a horror film.



* After a solid but modest opening weekend, Obsession actually saw a massive 39% increase in its second weekend over the Memorial Day frame.

* Outside of the Christmas holiday season, a film already playing in over 2,500 theaters seeing a massive spike in weekend-two revenue is virtually unheard of. It was fueled entirely by frantic word-of-mouth and viral social media discussions over the film's bleak, tragic metaphor for modern dating entitlement and consent.




The Tragic Flaw: Barker has stated in interviews that the core thesis of the film is a critique of absolute selfishness. "Any time you wish for something, it's probably going to be selfish," he noted, framing Bear not as a classic horror movie monster, but as a weak, paralyzed coward whose inability to be honest leads to total catastrophe.
