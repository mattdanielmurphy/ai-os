## Gemini

It all comes down to the Standard Anatomical Position. In anatomy, descriptions are always based on a person standing upright, feet forward, with palms facing out.


The reason terminology shifts for the extremities is to avoid ambiguity when those parts of the body rotate or move through different planes.


Anterior vs. Posterior
These terms are used for the "trunk" and major joints because they are fixed relative to the front and back of the body:



* Anterior: The front (e.g., your chest or the front of your hip).

* Posterior: The back (e.g., your shoulder blades or the back of your knee).


Dorsal vs. Volar (Wrists and Hands)
For the wrist and hand, "anterior" and "posterior" can get confusing because the forearm can rotate (supination and pronation).



* Volar (or Palmar): Refers to the palm side. "Volar" comes from the Latin vola, meaning the palm of the hand or sole of the foot.

* Dorsal: Refers to the back of the hand (the "dorsum").


Lateral vs. Medial (Ankles)
Because the foot is oriented perpendicular to the rest of the leg, "front and back" isn't always the most descriptive way to view the complex ligaments of the ankle.



* Medial: Toward the midline of the body (the "inside" of your ankle).

* Lateral: Away from the midline (the "outside" of your ankle).


Dorsal vs. Plantar (Feet)
While your poster shows "Lateral" and "Medial" for the ankle, you will often see Dorsal and Plantar for the foot itself:



* Dorsal: The top of the foot.

* Plantar: The sole of the foot (from the Latin planta).


Using these specific terms ensures that a surgeon or physical therapist in one part of the world knows exactly which ligament you're talking about, regardless of how the patient is currently sitting or lying down.

## Gemini

Zepbound and Mounjaro contain the same active ingredient, tirzepatide, manufactured by Eli Lilly. The primary difference is their FDA-approved indication: Mounjaro is approved for Type 2 Diabetes, while Zepbound is approved for chronic weight management.


Pricing and Savings Programs
Without insurance coverage, the retail price for both medications typically ranges from $1,050 to $1,200 per month in the United States. In other regions, such as Canada, the out-of-pocket cost for tirzepatide is often significantly lower, often ranging between $400 and $500 CAD per month.


The "discounted membership" usually refers to the Eli Lilly Savings Card:



* Zepbound Savings Card: For individuals with commercial insurance that does not cover the drug, the card can reduce the cost to about $550 per month. If insurance does cover it, the cost can drop to as low as $25.

* Mounjaro Savings Card: Requires a Type 2 Diabetes diagnosis. For those with commercial insurance that does not cover it, the cost is typically around $575.

* Requirements: These cards generally require commercial (private) insurance. They cannot be used with government-funded programs like Medicare or Medicaid.


Max Dosage and "Splitting" Strategy
The practice of purchasing the highest available dose (15mg) to use for multiple smaller doses is a common strategy to reduce the "per mg" cost, as all dose strengths are usually priced the same.



* 
Device Types:



* Single-Use Auto-Injectors (US): Most Zepbound/Mounjaro in the US comes in "locked" single-use pens. To split these doses, some individuals manually "deconstruct" the pen or inject the contents into a sterile glass vial to be drawn up with a standard insulin syringe.

* Vials: In some markets (including Canada and recently introduced for specific doses in the US), tirzepatide is available in single-use vials. Drawing from a vial into a syringe makes it easier to measure specific, smaller doses from a larger concentration.




* 
Cost Efficiency:



* A 15mg pen/vial contains 15mg of tirzepatide. If a user is on a 5mg maintenance dose, a single 15mg unit can theoretically provide three doses.

* This effectively cuts the monthly cost by two-thirds if the pharmacy and prescription allow for the higher dose.





Important Considerations

* Prescription Requirements: A physician must write the prescription for the 15mg dose. Many doctors are hesitant to prescribe the maximum dose for someone starting at a lower dose due to safety and titration protocols.

* Sterility Risks: Transferring medication from an auto-injector to a vial introduces a risk of contamination. Using a single-use vial for multiple doses (multi-dosing) also carries a risk of bacterial growth if not handled with strict sterile technique, as these formulations often lack preservatives.

* Off-Label Use: Using Mounjaro for weight loss (without a diabetes diagnosis) is considered "off-label." While legal for a doctor to prescribe, it may disqualify the user from using the manufacturer’s savings card, which often requires a specific diagnosis code.

* Compounded Alternatives: Some choose to use "compounded tirzepatide" from specialized pharmacies, which is often cheaper than the brand-name versions and comes in multi-dose vials, though these are not FDA-approved brand-name products.

In Canada, the "discounted membership" refers to the Patient Support Programs (PSP) run by Eli Lilly. Unlike the US "Savings Card" which requires insurance, the Canadian programs provide support for uninsured or underinsured patients.



* mymounjaro.ca / myzepbound.ca: You can self-enroll or have your doctor enroll you.

The "Max Dosage" Splitting Strategy
Since both medications in Canada are primarily distributed in the KwikPen format (which contains four doses per pen), the strategy of buying the maximum strength (15 mg) to save money is very common.



* Cost Efficiency: A 2.5 mg pen typically costs around $340, while a 15 mg pen costs approximately $460. By purchasing the 15 mg pen and taking smaller doses, the cost per mg drops significantly.

* Click Counting: The KwikPen is a dial-style injector. While the manufacturer does not provide instructions for partial doses, users often use "click counting" to measure smaller amounts:

* A full 15 mg dose is typically 60 clicks (confirm your pen’s specific dial resistance first).

* To get a 5 mg dose from a 15 mg pen, you would dial 20 clicks.

* This allows a single $460 pen to last for 12 weeks of 5 mg doses, bringing your monthly cost down to roughly $153.




* Needles: Unlike the single-use pens in the US, KwikPens require you to buy pen needles separately (e.g., 32G 4mm needles). A box of 100 is roughly $20–$30 at any Alberta pharmacy.



Where to Buy in Alberta
Pricing varies by pharmacy due to differing dispensing fees and markups.



* Costco Pharmacy: Usually the lowest out-of-pocket price in Alberta (often near $340–$350 for the starter 2.5 mg dose and $440–$460 for the 15 mg dose). You do not need a Costco membership to use their pharmacy.

* Superstore / Loblaws: Generally competitive with Costco.

* Shoppers Drug Mart: Often has the highest markups, sometimes $50–$100 more per pen than Costco.



[!IMPORTANT]
Sterility and Safety: KwikPens are designed to be used with a fresh needle for each injection. They are officially rated for use for 30 days after the first puncture. Using a pen for 8–12 weeks via the "splitting" method exceeds this timeframe, which carries a risk of the medication losing potency or developing bacterial growth. Keep the pen refrigerated between uses and always use a sterile needle.
