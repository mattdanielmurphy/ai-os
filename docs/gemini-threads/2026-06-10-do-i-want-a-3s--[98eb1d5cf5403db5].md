## Gemini

Go with the 10-second pre-infusion.


With fresh beans, a long pre-infusion is usually used to gently saturate the puck and maximize extraction of those bright, complex flavors. With stale beans, the goal is purely damage control—and a longer soak is your best friend here.


Here is why 10 seconds will help you a lot more than 3 seconds:


1. It helps prevent the "bottomless spray"
Stale coffee grounds are dry, brittle, and don't bond together well. If you hit them with full 9-bar pressure after only 3 seconds, the water will instantly find weak spots, drill holes through the puck, and spray everywhere. A 10-second pre-infusion lets the water slowly saturate the entire bed at low pressure, causing the grounds to swell up and fill in any micro-gaps before the heavy pressure hits.


2. It artificially creates resistance
Because stale beans are missing the $CO_2$ gas that naturally slows down water, they suffer from a lack of resistance. Wetting the puck thoroughly for 10 seconds coaxes the dry grounds into expanding as much as they possibly can, which helps mimic that missing resistance and slows down the actual shot.



A Quick Warning on Flow
Because a 10-second pre-infusion prepares the puck so well, it makes the coffee highly soluble. Once full pressure kicks in, the shot might actually flow even faster than it did with a short pre-infusion.


If you switch to a 10-second pre-infusion and the shot still gushes out too fast:



* Keep the 10-second soak.

* Grind even finer to compensate.


Combining a very fine grind with a long, slow pre-infusion is the best way to force a stale bag of beans to behave like a syrupy, well-behaved espresso.
