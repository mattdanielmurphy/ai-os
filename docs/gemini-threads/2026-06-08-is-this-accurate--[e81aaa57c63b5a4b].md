## Gemini

It is a bit mind-bending at first, but the user was actually conflating two different concepts at the end of that comment.


An accent can change which vowel sound you use, but it doesn't suddenly turn a vowel into a consonant like a /j/ (the "y" sound in yellow).


Here is how it actually breaks down:


1. Vowels vs. Consonants (The Physical Difference)
In linguistics, whether a sound is a vowel or a consonant has nothing to do with the letters written on a page. It is entirely about how your mouth shapes the air:



* Vowels: Created by a completely open, unobstructed vocal tract (like ah, ee, oo).

* Consonants: Created by breaking, squeezing, or blocking the airflow using your tongue, teeth, or lips (like b, t, s).


Because of this, the letter Y is a linguistic shapeshifter. When you say "Yorkshire," your tongue restricts the airflow at the beginning of the word—making it a consonant. When you say "oxygen" or "Lyndsey," the air flows completely unobstructed—making it a vowel.


2. Where the Accent Comes In
The commenter mentioned that in the word "justly," the 'y' sounds like an /i/ (a long "ee" sound, as in just-lee).


If someone has a specific regional accent, they might pronounce "justly" differently—perhaps shortening that final sound so it sounds more like the "i" in "bin."


Crucially, though, it remains a vowel in both accents. The accent just swaps one open vowel sound for another open vowel sound. It does not magically transform the 'y' into a hard consonant sound like the "y" in yo-yo.


3. The Modern Greek Letter Confusing the Issue
The comment also mentioned symbols like /y/ and /i/. These are symbols from the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), not standard English letters:



* /i/ represents the "ee" sound in see.

* /y/ represents a specific vowel sound that doesn't really exist in standard English anymore (it's like making an "ee" sound with your tongue but rounding your lips into an "oo" shape, common in French words like tu or German words like über).


Historically, the letter Y was brought into English to represent that exact /y/ vowel sound. Over centuries, English speakers stopped making that specific sound and naturally filled the gap with standard English vowels.


So while an accent can absolutely dictate which vowel sound you use to finish a word, it won't cross the border into making it a consonant.
