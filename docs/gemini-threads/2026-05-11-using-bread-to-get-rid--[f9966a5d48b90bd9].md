Using bread to get rid of salt in water

## Gemini

Using a piece of bread to "desalt" water or a soup is a common kitchen hack, but the physics behind it are often misunderstood.


How it Works (and How it Doesn't)
The primary mechanism at play is absorption. Bread is a porous, starch-based structure that acts like a sponge. When you place a slice of bread into salty water or an over-salted stew:



* Liquid Intake: The bread absorbs the salty liquid into its pores through capillary action.

* Starch Interaction: There is a minor amount of starch-salt binding, but it is negligible compared to the liquid absorption.

* Net Result: You are removing a portion of the salty solution itself, not filtering the salt out of the remaining liquid.


The Limitation
The bread does not act as a chemical magnet for sodium ions. Because it absorbs the water and the salt at the same concentration currently in the pot, the concentration of the salt in the remaining liquid stays exactly the same.



Analogy: If you have a bucket of red paint and you dip a sponge in to soak some up, the paint remaining in the bucket is still just as red; there is just less of it.



More Effective Alternatives
If you are trying to save a dish that is too salty, consider these methods instead:



* Dilution: The only way to actually lower the salt concentration. Add more unsalted liquid (water, stock, or cream).

* Acid or Sugar: Adding a splash of lemon juice, vinegar, or a pinch of sugar won't remove the salt, but it creates a "sensory distraction" that makes the saltiness less dominant on your palate.

* Increasing Bulk: Adding more solid ingredients (like more vegetables or grains) increases the total volume of the dish, spreading the salt across more surface area.


The Potato Myth
Similar to the bread trick, many people suggest dropping a raw potato into a salty soup. While the potato does absorb some salty liquid, scientific testing (notably by America’s Test Kitchen) has shown that it does not significantly lower the salt concentration of the remaining liquid any better than bread or simply removing a ladle-full of broth.

## Gemini

You're absolutely right that heat management is the biggest factor when you aren't using stabilizers. By keeping the temperature low and slow, you’re preventing the proteins in the cheese from tightening up too quickly and squeezing out the fats—which is exactly what causes that "split" or oily pool.


Here is the breakdown of why sodium citrate is used and why your method still works.


Why Sodium Citrate Matters
Cheese is essentially an emulsion of water and fat held together by a protein network (casein). When you heat an aged cheese like a sharp cheddar:



* The Protein Clumps: The proteins tend to break apart and then re-clump.

* The Fat Escapes: Once the protein structure collapses, it can no longer hold the fat, resulting in a grainy texture and a layer of oil.


Sodium citrate acts as an emulsifier. It changes the chemical structure of the cheese so that the proteins stay suspended in the liquid even when heated. This is why American cheese or a "sodium citrate sauce" stays silky and smooth like lava, even at higher temperatures.


Why Your "Low Heat" Method Works
By using low heat, you are performing a bit of "thermal balancing."



* Gentle Melting: You’re bringing the cheese up to its melting point without hitting the "crash point" where the proteins denature and contract.

* Residual Heat: Low heat allows the bread to toast slowly, giving the cheese time to melt thoroughly from the inside out without the edges of the cheese getting scorched.

* The Moisture Factor: If you cover the pan with a lid for a moment, the trapped steam helps the cheese melt evenly at a lower temperature, further protecting the emulsion.


If You Want to Experiment with Sodium Citrate
If you decide to buy the powder to turn a high-quality aged cheddar or gruyère into a "processed" style melt, here is the standard approach:



* The Ratio: Usually, you use about 2% to 3% of the weight of the cheese in sodium citrate.

* The Liquid: You dissolve the powder in a small amount of warm liquid (water, milk, or even beer) before whisking in the shredded cheese.

* The Result: You end up with a cheese sauce that you can pour into a container, let it solidify in the fridge, and then slice like American cheese—but with the flavor of a sharp, aged cheddar.


A Middle Ground: "The Hybrid"
If you don't want to buy a bag of chemicals, many people just use one slice of American cheese along with their fancy cheddar. The sodium citrate in that single slice is often enough to help stabilize the rest of the cheese in the sandwich.

## Gemini

You're right on both counts: it does increase the sodium content, and the "processed" label usually implies a few other changes to the nutritional profile beyond just the salt.


1. The Sodium Impact
Sodium citrate is a salt of citric acid. While it contains less sodium by weight than table salt (sodium chloride), you are adding it on top of the salt already present in the cheese.



* The Math: Sodium citrate is roughly 27% sodium by weight.

* The Usage: If you use 5g of sodium citrate to turn a block of cheddar into a smooth sauce, you’re adding about 1,350mg of extra sodium to the dish.

* The Comparison: For someone watching their blood pressure or total daily intake (usually capped at 2,300mg), that single addition can be significant.


2. Homemade vs. Commercial "Processed" Cheese
There is a big difference between using sodium citrate at home and buying a pack of "singles."


Commercial Processed Cheese (e.g., American Slices):



* Dairy Content: These often contain less actual cheese and more "whey protein concentrate," "milk protein concentrate," and "modified food starch."

* Emulsifiers: They often use a cocktail of phosphates (like sodium phosphate) in addition to citrates. Some studies suggest that the body absorbs inorganic phosphates from food additives more readily than organic phosphates found naturally in protein, which can be hard on the kidneys if consumed in excess.

* Vegetable Oils: Some lower-end versions (labeled "sandwich slices" rather than "cheese") replace milk fat with vegetable oils to cut costs.


The Homemade "Modernist" Approach:
If you take a high-quality, aged cheddar and use sodium citrate to melt it, you are still eating high-quality cheddar. You aren't losing the vitamins ($A$, $B_{12}$, $K_2$) or the calcium found in the original block. You are simply adding a specific salt to change how the proteins interact with fat and water.


3. Is it "Unhealthy"?
It depends on your definition.



* If "unhealthy" means high-sodium: Yes, adding sodium citrate makes the cheese objectively higher in sodium.

* If "unhealthy" means highly processed fillers: No, using the citrate at home allows you to skip the stabilizers, starches, and oils found in the factory-made versions.

Queso has this citrate in it? And other cheese sauces?

---

## Gemini

Yes, absolutely. Sodium citrate is essentially the "secret" to almost every silky-smooth cheese sauce you’ve ever had at a restaurant or from a jar.


Where You’ll Find It

* Commercial Queso & Nacho Cheese: If you look at the back of a jar of Tostitos Queso or a can of nacho cheese, you will almost always see sodium citrate or sodium phosphate (a similar emulsifying salt). It’s what allows that cheese to stay liquid even as it cools down on a plate of chips.

* Restaurant Dips: Many restaurants that serve "white queso" or beer cheese use sodium citrate to avoid making a traditional roux (flour and butter). A roux-based sauce can feel heavy or "floury," whereas a citrate-based sauce tastes purely like cheese but with a perfectly smooth texture.

* Velveeta: This is effectively just cheese that has already been processed with a high concentration of sodium citrate and extra whey. This is why a block of Velveeta melts so much more reliably than a block of sharp cheddar.


The Two Ways to Make Queso
Most people make queso using one of two "stabilizers" to keep it from splitting:



* The Starch Method (Traditional/Home): You use flour (a roux) or cornstarch. The starch molecules physically get in the way of the cheese proteins, preventing them from clumping together and squeezing out the fat. This works well, but it can dull the flavor of the cheese slightly.

* The Citrate Method (Modernist/Commercial): You use sodium citrate. This doesn't just "block" the proteins; it chemically relaxes them so they can bond with the water in the sauce. This results in a much more intense cheese flavor and a "glossy" look that starch can't quite replicate.


Is there a "Natural" version?
Interestingly, you can get a similar effect by using citric acid (from lemon juice) and baking soda. When they react together in a liquid, they actually create sodium citrate right in your pan. Some "cheater" recipes for Mac and Cheese use a splash of lemon juice and a pinch of baking soda for exactly this reason.
