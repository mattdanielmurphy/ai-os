## Gemini

You've got the core foundation down, but Rocksmith (and its successor, Rocksmith+) uses a pretty extensive visual shorthand to cover almost every physical technique you'd encounter on a real guitar or bass.


Beyond the basics you mentioned, here are the other note types and modifiers you'll see flying down the "noteway":


Expressive Techniques

* Bends: Indicated by a curved upward arrow. The height of the arrow tells you how far to bend (e.g., a half-step vs. a full step). If the arrow loops back down, it's a Pre-bend or a Release.

* Hammer-ons & Pull-offs (HOPOs): These appear as smaller, glowy notes connected to a primary note. You pluck the first and use your fretting hand for the rest.

* Vibrato: Indicated by "waves" or "squiggles" on the note tail. The wider the wave, the more aggressive the vibrato should be.

* Harmonics:

* Natural Harmonics: Represented by a small diamond shape on the string.

* Pinch Harmonics: These usually look like a "shimmering" or glowing version of a standard note, requiring that specific thumb-and-pick technique.





Advanced Chord & Rhythmic Markings

* Arpeggios: You’ll see a semi-transparent "chord zone" (a box on the highway) with individual notes inside it. You hold the chord shape but pick the notes one by one.

* Double Stops: Two notes played simultaneously (essentially a two-string chord), often seen in blues and rock leads.

* Tremolo Picking: Indicated by multiple horizontal slashes across the note tail, signaling you to pick as fast as possible for the duration.

* Tapped Notes: Usually identified by a small "T" or a specific highlight, meant to be struck with your picking hand on the fretboard.


Muting Variations

* String Mutes (X): Different from palm mutes; these are "dead notes" where you rest your fretting hand across the strings to get a percussive "thwack" with no discernible pitch.



Navigation Icons

* Accent Notes: These appear brighter or "larger" than others, indicating you should hit them with more velocity/emphasis.

* Hand Position Shifts: Look for the highlighted numbers on the fretboard lane at the bottom. When those shift, it's the game telling you to slide your whole hand to a new "box" on the neck.

Palm Mutes vs. String Mutes
In Rocksmith’s visual language, they distinguish these based on how much "pitch" is left in the note:



* Palm Mutes (PM): These are usually represented by a flattened or "shortened" note head. Instead of a tall, 3D rectangle, the note looks like a thick, flat slab or has a "muted" texture. It tells the player: "the pitch is still there, but choked."

* String Mutes (X): These are the percussive "dead notes." They use a literal "X" icon on the string. This tells the player: "there is no pitch here, just rhythm."


The "Essential" Implementation Tier
If you're building a Rocksmith-style engine in Godot, here is how I would prioritize the remaining techniques based on effort vs. reward:


1. High Priority (The Essentials)

* Sustains: Essential for rhythm. Simply a mesh trail (ImmediateMesh or a Trail3D) behind the note.

* Bends: Crucial for lead. In Godot, you can represent this by offsetting the Y-axis (or vertical height) of the sustain trail. If the trail moves "up" the highway, the player knows to increase tension.

* Palm Mutes: Easiest to code. Just a boolean flag that swaps the note mesh to a "squashed" version or changes the material to something less "glowy."


2. Medium Priority (The "Feel" Tier)

* Slides: Mechanically, this is just a sustain trail that moves horizontally across strings (or stays on one string but changes fret numbers). It’s visually intuitive but requires your "hit detection" logic to track the finger movement.

* HOPOs (Hammer-ons/Pull-offs): These are vital for fast sections. Visually, you just make the note head smaller/thinner to signal "don't pick this."


3. Low Priority (The "Polished" Tier)

* Vibrato: Looks cool, but it’s just a sine wave applied to the sustain trail.

* Harmonics: Just a shader swap (making the note look like a diamond or ghost-like).


Implementation Tip for Godot
For Bends and Vibrato, don't animate the mesh points manually if you can avoid it. Using a Vertex Shader to wobble or offset the sustain trail based on time and distance from the note head will save you a lot of CPU/GPU overhead.


Since you're focusing on the essentials, starting with a solid Sustain Trail system that can handle Y-axis offsets (Bends) and Horizontal shifts (Slides) will cover about 80% of all rock songs.

## Gemini

It is definitely a bit of a physical contradiction. On a fretted note, a string mute is easy—you just rest your fingers on the string without pressing down. On an open string, you don't have a fret as a reference point, which makes it feel awkward.


When you see an "X" on the 0 fret (open string), here is how you actually play it:


The Technique
You use your fretting hand to lightly touch the string anywhere—usually over the 3rd, 5th, or 7th fret area—just enough to deaden the vibration. Then you pluck it with your hitting hand.



* The Goal: You want a percussive "thud" with no clear pitch.

* The Trap: If you touch the string directly over a harmonic node (like exactly over the 5th, 7th, or 12th fret wire) without enough surface area from your fingers, you might accidentally trigger a natural harmonic (that "ping" sound) instead of a dead mute.


Why does Rocksmith show it that way?
Usually, the game displays an "open string mute" for one of two reasons:



* Rhythmic Consistency: The song has a chugging rhythm, and the artist is "playing" the open string rhythmically but keeping it choked for a percussive effect.

* Fast Transitions: You just finished playing an open note and the next "note" is a rhythmic filler before you move your hand to a new position.


Implementation Tip for Your Project
If you are worried about players finding this confusing in your own app, you can handle it a few ways:



* Visual Grouping: Only show mutes as "fretted" (e.g., at the 1st or 2nd fret) to imply "touch the string here."

* The "Slab" Look: Some players prefer seeing palm mutes (the "squashed" note) instead of string mutes (the "X") for open strings, as palm muting an open string at the bridge is often more intuitive for beginners than a fretting-hand mute.


Since you're focusing on bass, does the "thump" of a string mute feel distinct enough in your current audio engine, or are you still working on the hit-detection feedback?
