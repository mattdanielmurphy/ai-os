## Gemini

Yes, historically, some people absolutely did—specifically when it came to newborn puppies.


While it sounds like a bizarre horror story today, tail biting was an old, crude method of tail docking (amputating a dog's tail) before modern veterinary medicine existed.


Why People Used to Do It
Historically, people docked dogs' tails for several reasons:



* Tax Evasion: In 17th- and 18th-century England, companion dogs were taxed as luxury items, but working herding and hunting dogs were exempt. To prove a dog was a "working" animal and avoid the tax, owners would dock their tails.

* Misguided Medical Beliefs: The ancient Romans believed that removing a puppy’s tail tip (and sometimes part of its tongue) would prevent it from contracting rabies.

* Injury Prevention: For working terriers, herders, and hunting dogs, owners believed a shorter tail prevented it from getting caught in traps, mangled by livestock, or torn up by thick brambles and burrs.


Why They Biting It Off
Before surgical tools or rubber banding methods became standard, breeders and farmers would literally use their teeth to bite off the tips of a newborn puppy's tail.


The historical logic was that a quick, crushing bite from a human would cleanly sever the tiny, soft cartilage of a two- or three-day-old puppy while simultaneously bruising the blood vessels enough to naturally minimize the bleeding.


Thankfully, as veterinary medicine modernized and our understanding of animal pain developed, this brutal practice faded away. Today, cosmetic tail docking is completely banned or heavily restricted in many countries around the world, and where it is legal, it is strictly performed surgically by veterinarians.

However, the practice of using teeth for docking is well-documented in older kennel books, historical folklore, and memoirs of rural life.


The Historical References
The practice is most frequently cited in historical British and American agricultural history, specifically regarding terriers and herding dogs:



* The "Old Wives' Tale" of Rabies: In the 18th and 19th centuries, it was a common folk belief that biting off the "worm" (the tendon or tip) of a puppy's tail with your teeth would permanently protect the animal from rabies.

* The Traditional Breeder's Method: Before the advent of modern veterinary medicine or sterile tools, standard metal blades were prone to causing infections. Some old-school breeders believed that a human bite was actually "cleaner" because the crushing action of the teeth would crimp the blood vessels, preventing the puppy from bleeding out, whereas a sharp knife cut would bleed profusely.


It is a genuinely gruesome piece of history that has largely been scrubbed from modern pet care text for obvious reasons, but it was a very real, primitive custom among certain old-school dog breeders and farmers centuries ago.
