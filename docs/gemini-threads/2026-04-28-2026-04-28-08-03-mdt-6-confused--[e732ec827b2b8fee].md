## Gemini

Your intuition is spot on. To find the center of gravity (CG), you need to treat this as a static equilibrium problem. Since the motorcycle isn't rotating or flying away, the sum of the forces must be zero, and the sum of the torques (moments) must be zero.


1. Find the Total Weight
First, calculate the total gravitational force ($F_g$) acting on the motorcycle. Using $g = 9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$:


$$F_g = m \cdot g = 200 \text{ kg} \cdot 9.8 \text{ m/s}^2 = 1960 \text{ N}$$


2. Determine the Front Wheel Force
As you suspected, the total weight is supported by both wheels. If the rear wheel exerts $1200 \text{ N}$ on the ground, the ground exerts a normal force ($N_r$) of $1200 \text{ N}$ upward on the wheel (Newton's Third Law).


$$N_f + N_r = F_g$$
$$N_f + 1200 \text{ N} = 1960 \text{ N}$$
$$N_f = 760 \text{ N}$$


3. Calculate the Distance (Torque Balance)
To find the distance from the front wheel directly, set the front wheel as your pivot point. This makes the torque from the front wheel zero, leaving you with only one unknown variable ($x$).


In equilibrium, the clockwise torque from gravity must equal the counter-clockwise torque from the rear wheel:


$$\sum \tau_{front} = 0$$
$$\tau_{gravity} = \tau_{rear}$$
$$F_g \cdot x = N_r \cdot 1.8 \text{ m}$$


Now, substitute the values and solve for $x$:


$$1960 \text{ N} \cdot x = 1200 \text{ N} \cdot 1.8 \text{ m}$$
$$1960x = 2160$$
$$x = \frac{2160}{1960} \approx 1.102 \text{ m}$$



The center of gravity is located approximately 1.10 m from the front wheel.


Using the rear wheel as the pivot (as you suggested) also works perfectly; you would just find the distance from the rear wheel ($d \approx 0.698 \text{ m}$) and subtract it from the total wheelbase ($1.8 - 0.698 = 1.102 \text{ m}$).


Does that clarify the "two variables" concern? By picking one of the wheels as the pivot, you effectively "delete" its force from the torque equation.

## Gemini

In physics problems like this, when they mention a force "from the wall on the lever," they are referring to the reaction force at the hinge (the pivot point).


The wall and the lever (the beam) only touch at that single point. While the string is attached to the wall, its force is applied to the beam at the $1.30\text{ m}$ mark, not at the wall.


Because the system is in static equilibrium, the horizontal force at the hinge ($F_x$) must exactly balance the horizontal component of the tension ($T_x$) pulling the beam toward the wall.


Solving for the Weight
To find the weight of the load ($W$), you can use the horizontal force to find the vertical component of the tension, then balance the torques.


1. Find the vertical component of the tension ($T_y$)
Since we know the horizontal force from the wall is $42.0\text{ N}$, the horizontal component of the tension pulling back is also $42.0\text{ N}$. We can use the angle to find the vertical pull:


$$\tan(28^\circ) = \frac{T_y}{T_x}$$
$$T_y = 42.0\text{ N} \cdot \tan(28^\circ)$$
$$T_y \approx 42.0 \cdot 0.5317 \approx 22.33\text{ N}$$


2. Sum of Torques
Set the hinge as the pivot point. The clockwise torque from the weight must equal the counter-clockwise torque from the vertical component of the tension. (We ignore $T_x$ and $F_x$ because they act directly through the pivot or parallel to the beam, creating zero torque).


$$\sum \tau_{hinge} = 0$$
$$(T_y \cdot 1.30\text{ m}) = (W \cdot 1.80\text{ m})$$
$$(22.33\text{ N} \cdot 1.30\text{ m}) = W \cdot 1.80\text{ m}$$
$$29.03\text{ Nm} = W \cdot 1.80\text{ m}$$


3. Final Calculation
$$W = \frac{29.03}{1.80} \approx 16.1\text{ N}$$



The "negligible mass" of the beam is a classic physics-land gift—it means you don't have to worry about the beam's own weight pulling down at the $0.90\text{ m}$ mark.
