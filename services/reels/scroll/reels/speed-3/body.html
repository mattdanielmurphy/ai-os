This is the big one for Midterm 2. Natural deduction means building proofs one small, rule-checked step at a time. The rules are few, and each one is simple. The skill is knowing which one to reach for, so this reel quizzes you on exactly that. Warm-ups first.

01 · The very idea · ch. 16–17.1

Proofs instead of tables

Truth tables check every row, so they double in size with every letter. Twenty letters means about a million rows. A proof instead moves from the premises to the conclusion in small steps, and each step must follow a rule.

A proof is a numbered list of lines. Each line has a sentence and a justification on the right. A line can be a premise, written PR, an assumption, written AS, or follow from earlier lines by a rule, which you cite with the line numbers it uses.

1K ∧ WPR
2K → RPR
3K∧E, 1
4R→E, 2, 3

Every connective gets two rules. An introduction rule, marked with an I, says how to prove a sentence with that connective as its main connective. An elimination rule, marked with an E, says how to use one you already have.

02 · Reiteration and conjunction · ch. 17.2–17.3

The easy ones

Reiteration, R, just copies an earlier available line. And introduction joins two lines you already have into one conjunction. And elimination takes a conjunction and gives you either side.

∧I𝒜, ℬ ⟹ 𝒜 ∧ ℬ
∧E𝒜 ∧ ℬ ⟹ 𝒜, or ℬ

Line 1 is K ∧ W. Line 2 is W. What justifies line 2?

For and introduction, the order you cite matters only for neatness: cite the line of the left side first, then the right side.

03 · The conditional · ch. 17.4

Using an arrow, and proving one

Arrow elimination is the famous one. If you have an arrow, and you have its left side, you may write its right side. You cite the arrow's line, then the left side's line.

𝒜 → ℬ, 𝒜 ⟹ ℬ→E. Only forwards: having ℬ tells you nothing about 𝒜.

You have R → S on line 1 and S on line 2. What can →E give you?

To prove an arrow, you need a subproof. Start a new vertical line and assume the left side. Work inside it until you reach the right side. Then close the subproof and write the arrow, citing the whole subproof as a range of lines.

1H → KPR
2HAS
3K→E, 1, 2
4K ∨ W∨I, 3
5H → (K ∨ W)→I, 2–4

In the proof above, why does line 5 cite “2–4”?

04 · Subproofs · ch. 17.5

What you can cite, and when

You can assume anything at all, at any time, by opening a subproof. The catch is that whatever you prove inside it depends on that assumption. So once the subproof is closed, its lines are locked away. This is called discharging the assumption.

The rule for citing a single line: it must come earlier, and it must not sit inside a subproof that has already closed. A closed subproof can still be cited as a whole range, by rules like arrow introduction, as long as it isn't inside another closed one.

Same proof as above. You add a line 6. Which lines may line 6 cite on their own?

Subproofs can sit inside subproofs. The inner one has to close before the outer one does, and the last line of a proof must be back on the main line, outside every subproof.

05 · Biconditional · ch. 17.6

Two arrows in one

To prove a double arrow you need two subproofs: one assuming the left side and reaching the right, and one assuming the right side and reaching the left. Then double arrow introduction cites both ranges.

Double arrow elimination works in both directions. With the double arrow and either side, you may write the other side.

Line 1 is S ↔ T. Line 2 is T. What can ↔E give you?

Cite the double arrow first, then the side you have.

06 · Disjunction · ch. 17.7

Or: easy in, hard out

Or introduction is generous. From any line, you may write that sentence or anything else, in either order. The added part can be any sentence at all, even a long one you've never seen.

Line 4 is R. Which line can you write next by ∨I, 4?

Or elimination is the hard one. Having K or H doesn't tell you which. So to use it, you show your goal follows either way. You need the disjunction, a subproof assuming the left side that reaches the goal, and a subproof assuming the right side that reaches the same goal.

𝒜 ∨ ℬ, [𝒜 … 𝒞], [ℬ … 𝒞] ⟹ 𝒞∨E cites three things: the disjunction's line and both subproof ranges.

You have K ∨ H and want S by ∨E. What do you need?

Both subproofs must end in exactly the same sentence. That's the whole trick.

07 · Negation and contradiction · ch. 17.8

Not, contradiction, and indirect proof

The symbol that looks like an upside-down T means "contradiction", and it is always false. Not elimination gets you there: if a sentence and its exact negation are both available, write the contradiction symbol. Cite the negation first.

Not introduction runs the other way. Assume something, reach a contradiction, close the subproof, and conclude its negation. Indirect proof, IP, is the mirror image: assume a negation, reach a contradiction, and conclude the sentence without the not.

¬I[𝒜 … ⊥] ⟹ ¬𝒜
IP[¬𝒜 … ⊥] ⟹ 𝒜

A subproof assumed ¬P and reached ⊥, then closed. Which line can you add after it?

Last, explosion, X. From a contradiction, you may write any sentence at all. It's legal, because a contradiction is never true. But it only works when the contradiction symbol is already on an available line. It's not a way out when you're stuck.

Which pair of available lines lets you write ⊥ by ¬E?

Those are all the basic rules. Next, how to actually find a proof.

08 · Constructing proofs · ch. 18

Work backwards from the goal, forwards from what you have

Look at your goal's main connective, and plan to use its introduction rule. Goal is an and? Prove each side. Goal is an arrow? Assume the left side and aim for the right. Goal is a negation? Assume the sentence without the not, and aim for a contradiction.

Your goal is ¬(W ∧ R). What's the natural first move?

Then work forwards. Break up what you have: and elimination on conjunctions, arrow elimination when you have a left side, or elimination when you have a disjunction. Your goal and your premises meet in the middle.

And if nothing works, proceed indirectly. Assume the negation of your goal and hunt for a contradiction, then finish with indirect proof. It always gives you something new to work with.

09 · Additional rules · ch. 19

Five handy extras

Chapter 19 adds five rules that save steps. Learn their names and shapes, because questions often ask which rule justifies a line.

DSDisjunctive syllogism. 𝒜 ∨ ℬ and ¬𝒜 give ℬ (or ¬ℬ gives 𝒜). Cite the or first.
MTModus tollens. 𝒜 → ℬ and ¬ℬ give ¬𝒜. Cite the arrow first.
DNEDouble-negation elimination. ¬¬𝒜 gives 𝒜.
LEMExcluded middle. Two subproofs, from 𝒜 and from ¬𝒜, reaching the same ℬ, give ℬ.
DeMDe Morgan. ¬(𝒜 ∧ ℬ) ⟺ ¬𝒜 ∨ ¬ℬ, and ¬(𝒜 ∨ ℬ) ⟺ ¬𝒜 ∧ ¬ℬ. Works both ways.

Line 1 is R → S. Line 2 is ¬S. Line 3 is ¬R. Justification for line 3?

10 · Proof-theoretic concepts · ch. 20

The single turnstile

The single turnstile means "there is a proof". Some sentences, single turnstile, a conclusion, says a proof exists from those sentences to that conclusion. Compare the double turnstile from reel 2, which is about truth-table rows.

provable𝒜, ℬ ⊢ 𝒞
true on every row𝒜, ℬ ⊨ 𝒞

A theorem is a sentence you can prove from no premises at all. Two sentences are interderivable if each can be proved from the other. A set of sentences is inconsistent if you can prove the contradiction symbol from them.

Notice the lopsidedness. To show something is a theorem, one proof is enough. To show it's not a theorem, you'd have to rule out every possible proof. That's why, in practice, you show "not provable" with a truth table instead.

11 · Derived rules, soundness, completeness · ch. 21–22

Why the system can be trusted

Chapter 21 shows the five extra rules can each be built from the basic rules. So they're shortcuts, not new powers: anything you prove with them, you could prove without them.

Chapter 22 links the two turnstiles. The proof system is sound: whatever you can prove is a real entailment, so proofs never lead you astray. And it is complete: every real entailment has a proof. Together, single turnstile and double turnstile always agree.

soundif ⊢ then ⊨
completeif ⊨ then ⊢

“Every argument that can be proved is valid.” That's…

Soundness, provable means valid. Completeness, valid means provable.
