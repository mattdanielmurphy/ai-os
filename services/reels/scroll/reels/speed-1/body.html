01 · Arguments · ch. 1

Premises and a conclusion

In logic, an argument is a list of sentences. The last one is the conclusion. The others are premises, the reasons offered for it. An argument doesn't need to be angry, and it doesn't need to be good.

Words like "so", "therefore" and "hence" flag a conclusion. Words like "because", "since" and "given that" flag a premise. The conclusion isn't always written last in English, so look for these flags.

“The pond is frozen, since it was minus thirty all week.” Which part is the conclusion?

Only sentences that can be true or false belong in an argument. Questions like "is it cold?" and commands like "shut the door" can't be premises or conclusions.

02 · Validity · ch. 2

No case where the premises are true and the conclusion false

Here is the most important definition in the course. An argument is valid if there is no case where all the premises are true and the conclusion is false. A case is any way the world could be.

valid = no case with all premises T and the conclusion FValidity is about the link between premises and conclusion, not about whether anything is actually true.

So validity doesn't care whether the premises really are true. "All fish can fly. Nemo is a fish. So Nemo can fly." is valid. If the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be.

Can a valid argument have a false conclusion?

The flip side: an argument can have a true conclusion and still be invalid. "Edmonton is in Canada, so the sky is blue." Both are true, but the premise gives no support. There's a case, an imaginable world, where Edmonton is in Canada and the sky is green.

A sound argument is a valid argument whose premises are all actually true. So a sound argument must have a true conclusion.

An argument is valid and its conclusion is false. What do you know for sure?

Two quick tips. Validity can fail because the premises just don't connect to the conclusion. And it can fail because the conclusion only follows "probably", not in every case. Logic here only counts the every-case kind.

03 · Other logical notions · ch. 3

Necessary, contingent, equivalent, jointly possible

A necessary truth is true in every case, like "either it's snowing or it isn't". A necessary falsehood is false in every case, like "it's snowing and it isn't". Anything else is contingent: true in some cases and false in others.

“Matt has a logic midterm on November 4.” This sentence is…

Two sentences are necessarily equivalent if they have the same truth value in every case. "The door is shut" and "the door is not open" are a fair example.

A group of sentences is jointly possible if there is some case where they are all true together. If no case makes them all true at once, they are jointly impossible.

Which group is jointly impossible?

Notice the link to validity. An argument is valid exactly when the premises, together with the opposite of the conclusion, are jointly impossible.

Back to validity: which of these is invalid?

Keep that last pattern in mind. Running the reasoning backwards, from "has feathers" to "is a bird", is the classic invalid move, and it shows up all through the course.

04 · Atomic sentences · ch. 4

Capital letters for whole sentences

Truth-functional logic, or TFL, uses capital letters for whole, simple sentences. These are atomic sentences: they have no logical parts inside them. A symbolization key says what each letter means.

Symbolization keyS: It is snowing
C: The class is cancelled
W: Matt walks to campus

The letters don't carry any meaning on their own. S means "it is snowing" only because the key says so. TFL then builds bigger sentences from these with five connectives, one for each of the next few sections.

¬not (negation)
∧and (conjunction)
∨or (disjunction)
→if … then … (conditional)
↔if and only if (biconditional)

05 · Not, and · ch. 5.1–5.2

Negation and conjunction

The not symbol goes in front of a sentence and flips its truth value. "It is not snowing" is not S. So is "it is false that it's snowing".

The and symbol joins two sentences, and the whole is true only when both parts are true. English has lots of words for and: "but", "although", "yet", "even though", "also". They add a feeling of contrast, but logically they all just mean both.

“It's snowing, but Matt walks to campus.” (key above)

Watch where the not sits. "Not both" puts the not outside the brackets. "Both not" puts a not on each part. They say different things.

not both¬(S ∧ C)
both not, neither¬S ∧ ¬C

“It isn't both snowing and cancelled.” Which symbolization fits?

The part of a sentence a not applies to is called its scope. Brackets are how you control it.

06 · Or · ch. 5.3

Disjunction is inclusive

The or symbol is true when at least one part is true, including when both are. That's called inclusive or. The menu kind of or, "soup or salad but not both", is exclusive, and TFL has no single symbol for it.

(S ∨ C) ∧ ¬(S ∧ C)Exclusive or, built from the symbols you have: at least one, and not both.

"Neither A nor B" means not A and not B. You can also write it as not, then A or B in brackets. Both say the same thing.

“Neither is it snowing nor is the class cancelled.” Which one is wrong?

That pairing is worth memorizing. Not-or matches and-of-nots. Not-and matches or-of-nots.

Quick throwback: in “Matt passed, because he studied”, which word flags the premise?

On to the connective that causes the most trouble.

07 · If, only if · ch. 5.4

The conditional, and which side goes first

The arrow means "if, then". The part before the arrow is the antecedent. The part after is the consequent. "If it snows, the class is cancelled" is S arrow C.

An arrow sentence is false in only one case: the left side is true and the right side is false. In every other case it counts as true.

When is S → C false?

Here is the rule that saves marks. "A only if B" goes A arrow B. The word "if" on its own introduces the left side. The phrase "only if" introduces the right side.

A if BB → A
A only if BA → B

A way to feel it: "you pass only if you write the exam". Writing the exam is necessary, not enough on its own. If you passed, you must have written it. So pass arrow write.

“Matt walks to campus only if it isn't snowing.”

The thing after the arrow is a necessary condition. The thing before it is a sufficient condition. "Sufficient" means enough on its own. "Necessary" means required.

“Snow is sufficient for a cancellation.”

Necessary goes after the arrow, sufficient goes before it. Say that twice.

08 · If and only if, unless · ch. 5.5–5.6

The biconditional, and unless

The double arrow means "if and only if". It's true when both sides have the same truth value, both true or both false. It's like an arrow going each way at once.

S ↔ CSame as (S → C) ∧ (C → S). True when S and C match.

Now unless. "Unless A, B" can be written as not A arrow B, or simply as A or B. Both are correct, and the second is the easiest to remember: unless works like or.

“The class is cancelled unless it's sunny.” Key: C, cancelled; Y, sunny.

People in everyday talk often mean "if and only if" when they only say "if". "You get dessert if you finish your vegetables" usually means only then, too. In this course, symbolize exactly what is written: plain "if" gets one arrow.

09 · Sentences of TFL · ch. 6

How sentences are built, and the main connective

The book defines a sentence of TFL step by step. Every atomic sentence is a sentence. If A is a sentence, so is not A. If A and B are sentences, so are A and B, A or B, A arrow B, and A double arrow B, each wrapped in brackets. Nothing else is a sentence.

(𝒜 ∧ ℬ) (𝒜 ∨ ℬ) (𝒜 → ℬ) (𝒜 ↔ ℬ) ¬𝒜Every two-place connective brings its own pair of brackets. ¬ doesn't.

The main connective, or main logical operator, is the one added last when the sentence was built. It decides what kind of sentence the whole thing is. To find it: if the sentence starts with a not, that's it. Otherwise, count brackets from the left. The first connective you meet with exactly one bracket open is the main one.

What is the main connective of ¬(S ∨ C) → W?

The scope of a connective is the smallest part of the sentence it is the main connective of. In that last example, the not covers just the bracket with S or C, and the arrow's scope is the whole sentence.

Two bracket conventions make life easier. You may drop the outermost pair of brackets. And you may use square brackets instead of round ones, purely to help the eye. Put dropped brackets back whenever the sentence becomes part of a bigger one.

Which of these is not a sentence of TFL, even with the conventions?

If a sentence could be grouped two ways, it isn't finished yet.

10 · Ambiguity · ch. 7

English is ambiguous, TFL isn't

A sentence is ambiguous if it has more than one meaning. Lexical ambiguity comes from one word, like "bank". Structural ambiguity comes from how the parts group together. Vagueness is different: a vague word, like "tall", has fuzzy edges rather than two meanings.

A common structural kind is scope ambiguity. "The soup is hot or spicy and filling" could mean: hot, or else spicy and filling. Or it could mean: hot or spicy, and also filling. TFL forces you to pick, because brackets settle the grouping.

Key: H hot, P spicy, F filling. Which reading says the soup is filling for sure?

So when you symbolize an ambiguous English sentence, you are choosing a reading. The exam may ask you to give each reading separately.

11 · Use and mention · ch. 8

Talking about symbols, not with them

When you talk about a word itself, put it in quotation marks. Edmonton is a city. "Edmonton" is a word with eight letters. Mixing them up gives nonsense, like saying the city has eight letters.

Which sentence is true?

The language being studied is the object language, here TFL. The language used to talk about it is the metalanguage, here English with a few extras.

One of those extras is metavariables: curly script capital letters. They aren't part of TFL. They stand for any sentence of TFL at all, which is how the rules can talk about every sentence at once.

𝒜 → ℬScript letters are metavariables: any sentences. A plain A is one particular sentence letter of TFL.

Which of these does the pattern 𝒜 ∧ ¬ℬ not fit?

Script letters are how every rule in the rest of the course is written, so it pays to read them as "any sentence".
