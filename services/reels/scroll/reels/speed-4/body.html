01 · The pieces · ch. 23

Names, predicates, variables, quantifiers

Names are lowercase a to r, and each picks out exactly one thing in the domain. Predicates are capitals with gaps. Variables are lowercase s to z. The upside-down A means "for all" and the backwards E means "there is at least one".

Symbolization key for this reeldomain: people at a gym
R(x): x runs
L(x): x lifts
T(x, y): x trains y
p: Priya
j: Jun

“Jun trains Priya.”

The order of the gaps is set by the key. Read the key's wording, and fill the gaps in that order.

02 · All and some · ch. 24

Arrow with all, and with some

The two patterns to know cold. "All A are B" uses for-all with an arrow inside. "Some A is B" uses there-is with an and inside.

all runners lift∀x (R(x) → L(x))
some runner lifts∃x (R(x) ∧ L(x))

Why is ∀x (R(x) ∧ L(x)) wrong for “all runners lift”?

And the matching trap on the other side.

Why is ∃x (R(x) → L(x)) wrong for “some runner lifts”?

The rhyme: for-all goes with arrow, there-is goes with and.

03 · Not with quantifiers · ch. 24

A not flips all into some

"Not everyone lifts" means someone doesn't. "Nobody lifts" means everyone doesn't. Moving a not across a quantifier flips the quantifier.

same thing¬∀x L(x)  =  ∃x ¬L(x)
same thing¬∃x L(x)  =  ∀x ¬L(x)

Which one does not say “no runner lifts”?

Two rules about domains help here too. A domain always has at least one thing in it. And a sentence like "all runners lift" is true when there are no runners at all, because there's no runner to break it.

Nobody at the gym runs. Is ∀x (R(x) → L(x)) true?

Next, two quantifiers at once.

04 · Multiple generality · ch. 25

Order matters

With two quantifiers, read left to right, like a game. For-all-x, there-is-y means: whoever you pick for x, I can then find a y, possibly a different one each time. There-is-y, for-all-x means: one single y works for every x.

everyone trains someone∀x ∃y T(x, y)
someone is trained by everyone∃y ∀x T(x, y)

“Somebody trains everyone.”

Going from for-all-there-is to there-is-for-all is called the quantifier shift fallacy. "Everyone has a mother" doesn't mean "someone is everyone's mother".

Does ∀x ∃y T(x, y) entail ∃y ∀x T(x, y)?

The other direction is fine: if one person is trained by everyone, then everyone trains someone.

05 · Identity · ch. 26

Equals, other, only, counting

The equals sign says two names, or variables, pick out the same thing. "Someone other than Priya" needs it: there is an x, and x is not equal to p.

“Priya trains someone other than herself.”

"Only Priya runs" says two things: Priya runs, and anyone who runs is Priya.

R(p) ∧ ∀x (R(x) → x = p)

In that formula, what job does the ∀ part do?

Counting works the same way. "At least two runners" needs two runners and a clause saying they're not the same. "At most one runner" says any two runners are the same one. "Exactly one" is both at least one and at most one.

Why does ∃x ∃y (R(x) ∧ R(y)) not say there are at least two runners?

Different variables don't mean different things. Only the equals sign, negated, can say that.

06 · Sentences of FOL · ch. 27

Free and bound variables

A variable is bound when a quantifier with that variable has it in its scope. Otherwise it's free. A formula with any free variable isn't a sentence: it has no truth value until something fills that gap.

Which of these is a sentence of FOL?

Brackets set a quantifier's scope, just like a not's. Without brackets, a quantifier only reaches the next smallest piece.

In ∀x L(x) → R(x), is the last x bound?

When in doubt, put brackets right after the quantifier around everything it should cover.

07 · Definite descriptions · ch. 28

“The” means three things

Russell's analysis says "the runner lifts" makes three claims. There is at least one runner. There is at most one runner. And that runner lifts.

∃x (R(x) ∧ ∀y (R(y) → y = x) ∧ L(x))At least one, at most one, and it lifts.

On Russell's view, if the gym has two runners, “the runner lifts” is…

"The runner doesn't lift" has two readings. The outer reading puts the not in front of everything: it's not the case that the runner lifts, which is true if there's no runner. The inner reading says there is exactly one runner, and that runner doesn't lift.

Nobody runs. Which reading of “the runner doesn't lift” is true?

08 · Ambiguity · ch. 29

English hides the order

"Every member trusts a coach" can mean each member has some coach they trust, maybe different ones. Or it can mean there is one coach everyone trusts. FOL makes you pick the order of the quantifiers, so you must pick a reading.

Which reading puts ∃ first?

Not and quantifiers make ambiguity too. "Every runner didn't finish" could mean no runner finished, or only that not every runner finished.
