This reel adds one symbol, the equals sign, and shows how much it can do. Then it covers the grammar of FOL: which strings of symbols are sentences, and which ones can't be true or false at all. That's chapters 26 and 27.

01 · The equals sign

Same thing, not just alike

Try writing "Ada likes everyone else". You can't do it yet, because "else" means "everyone who isn't Ada", and nothing so far says two things are the same, or different.

The equals sign fixes that. It's a two-place predicate that always means the same thing: x equals y means x and y are one and the same object. Not two things that look alike. One thing.

Always part of every keyx = y: x is identical to y

So if t names Mark Twain and s names Samuel Clemens, then t equals s says those two names pick out the same person. Which is true: it was his pen name.

t = s“Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens.” Two names, one person.

It's written between its two terms, like in math, instead of out front. And to say two things are different, put a "not" in front: not, x equals a. That reads "x is not Ada".

¬ x = a“x is not Ada.” The ¬ covers the whole x = a.

Now "Ada likes everyone else" works: for all x, if x is not Ada, then Ada likes x.

∀x (¬ x = a → L(a, x))“Ada likes everyone else.” L(x, y): x likes y · a: Ada

02 · Only and except

"Only Ada" and "everyone but Ada"

Symbolization keydomain: people in the class
P(x): x passed
a: Ada

"Only Ada passed" says two things. Ada passed. And anyone who passed is Ada. In symbols: P of a, and, for all x, if x passed, then x equals a.

two partsP(a) ∧ ∀x (P(x) → x = a)
same, shorter∀x (P(x) ↔ x = a)
“Only Ada passed.” The double arrow packs both parts into one.

"Everyone except Ada passed" also says two things. Everyone who isn't Ada passed. And Ada didn't.

two parts∀x (¬ x = a → P(x)) ∧ ¬P(a)
same, shorter∀x (¬ x = a ↔ P(x))
“Everyone except Ada passed.”

The book notes that not everyone agrees "except" means Ada failed. In math, "defined everywhere except at zero" leaves open what happens at zero. For this course, read "except" as including the "and Ada didn't" part unless told otherwise.

03 · Counting

At least, at most, exactly

Identity lets FOL count. We'll count bikes, with B of x meaning x is a bike.

"There is at least one bike" needs nothing new: there is an x, B of x.

∃x B(x)At least one bike.

"At least two bikes" is the tricky one. You might write: there's an x and a y, and both are bikes. But x and y are allowed to be the same bike! So you have to add: and x is not y.

wrong: one bike makes it true∃x∃y (B(x) ∧ B(y))
at least two∃x∃y (B(x) ∧ B(y) ∧ ¬ x = y)

For "at least three", you need three bikes, and every pair of them must be different: x isn't y, y isn't z, and x isn't z. Leaving out one pair is a common mistake.

∃x∃y∃z (B(x) ∧ B(y) ∧ B(z) ∧ ¬ x = y ∧ ¬ y = z ∧ ¬ x = z)At least three bikes. Three pairs, three “not equals”.

The domain has exactly one bike. Is ∃x∃y (B(x) ∧ B(y)) true?

"At most one bike" means: if you pick a bike, and then pick a bike, you picked the same one twice. For all x and all y, if both are bikes, then x equals y. Or simply: it's not true that there are at least two.

pick twice, same one∀x∀y ((B(x) ∧ B(y)) → x = y)
not at least two¬∃x∃y (B(x) ∧ B(y) ∧ ¬ x = y)
At most one bike. Zero bikes also makes these true.

"Exactly one bike" is at least one, plus at most one. The neat way to say it: there is a bike x, and every bike is x.

∃x (B(x) ∧ ∀y (B(y) → x = y))Exactly one bike. Remember this shape: reel 4 uses it for the word “the”.

"Exactly two bikes" works the same way: there are two different bikes, x and y, and every bike is one of those two.

∃x∃y (B(x) ∧ B(y) ∧ ¬ x = y ∧ ∀z (B(z) → (x = z ∨ y = z)))Exactly two bikes.

One last trick. Words like "thing" and "object" apply to everything, so they don't need a predicate. "There are at least two things" is just: there is an x and a y, and x is not y.

∃x∃y ¬ x = y“There are at least two things.” No predicate needed.

04 · The grammar of FOL

Building blocks, then formulas

Chapter 27 is the official grammar. Like in Unit 2 for TFL, it gives exact rules for which strings of symbols count. First, the six kinds of symbols.

A … ZPredicates, plus the special predicate =
a … rNames
s … zVariables
¬ ∧ ∨ → ↔Connectives
( )Brackets
∀ ∃Quantifiers

A term is any name or any variable. An atomic formula is a predicate with the right number of terms in its brackets, or two terms with an equals sign between them. Sentence letters from TFL count too.

atomic formulasF(a)   F(x)   L(x, b)   x = a
A predicate with the right number of terms, or term = term.

From there, formulas are built up. If A is a formula, so is not-A. If A and B are formulas, so are A and B, A or B, A arrow B, and A double-arrow B, each in brackets. And if A is a formula, then for all x A, and there is an x A, are formulas too.

F(x)
(F(x) → G(x))
∀x (F(x) → G(x))
Built up one rule at a time. The last rule used gives the main operator.

The main logical operator is whatever was added last when the formula was built. Its scope is the part of the formula it was added to. That's how you tell exactly what a quantifier covers.

05 · Free and bound

Only sentences can be true or false

Take L of z, z: "z likes z". Is that true? You can't say, because z doesn't name anyone. Put a quantifier in front and it becomes a real claim. There is a z who likes z means someone likes themselves.

So here's the rule. A variable is bound if it sits inside the scope of a quantifier with the same letter. Otherwise it's free. A sentence is a formula with no free variables, and only sentences can be true or false.

free z: not a sentenceL(z, z)
bound z: a sentence∃z L(z, z)

Brackets matter here. In "for all x, F of x", then an arrow, then G of x, the quantifier only covers F of x. So the last x is free, and the whole thing isn't a sentence. With brackets around the if-then, both x's are bound.

last x is free∀x F(x) → G(x)
a sentence∀x (F(x) → G(x))

Which of these is a sentence (no free variables)?

06 · Bracket shortcuts

Brackets you're allowed to drop

The same shortcuts as TFL apply. You may drop the outermost brackets. You may use square brackets to make things easier to read. And in a long chain of only "and"s, or only "or"s, you can drop the inner brackets.

fineA ∧ B ∧ C ∧ D
not allowed: mixes ∨ and ∧A ∨ B ∧ C

You can't drop brackets when "and" and "or" are mixed, or next to an arrow, because then it would be ambiguous.

Wrap-up

What to remember

  1. x equals y means one and the same thing. Not x equals y means different things.
  2. "At least two" needs "not x equals y", or one thing can count twice.
  3. "Exactly one F": there's an F, and every F is that one.
  4. A sentence has no free variables. Check what each quantifier's brackets cover.
