In reel 1, every predicate had one gap, like "blank is a cat". This reel adds predicates with two gaps, and then puts two quantifiers in one sentence. It's chapter 25 of the textbook.

01 · Two gaps

Predicates that relate two things

Some predicates are about how two things relate: "blank likes blank", or "blank is taller than blank". These are two-place predicates. You need two terms to fill them and make a sentence.

Symbolization keydomain: people
L(x, y): x likes y
a: Ada
b: Ben

"Ada likes Ben" is L, with a and b in the brackets. "Ben likes Ada" is L, with b and a. Those are different sentences, because liking doesn't have to go both ways.

Ada likes BenL(a, b)
Ben likes AdaL(b, a)
Ada likes herselfL(a, a)

There's no limit on the number of gaps. "Blank lent blank to blank" has three.

The key tells you which gap is which. The variables in the key are just labels for the gaps: x marks the one who likes, and y marks the one who is liked. In L of b, a, the first name fills the x gap and the second fills the y gap.

Passive voice changes the order of the words, not the gaps. "Ben is liked by Ada" still means Ada likes Ben, so it's L of a, b.

With L(x, y): x likes y, which says “Ada is liked by Ben”?

Connectives work as always. "Ada likes Ben, but not the other way around" is: L of a, b, and not L of b, a.

L(a, b) ∧ ¬L(b, a)“Ada likes Ben, but Ben doesn't like Ada.”

02 · One quantifier, two gaps

Someone, everyone, and a name

Now add a quantifier. "Ada likes someone": there is a y such that Ada likes y.

∃y L(a, y)“Ada likes someone.” Ada fills the liker gap.

"Someone likes Ada": there is an x such that x likes Ada. Same quantifier, but now the variable sits in the other gap.

∃x L(x, a)“Someone likes Ada.” Ada fills the liked gap.

"Everyone likes Ada" puts the upside-down A on the liker. "Ada likes everyone" puts it on the one who is liked.

everyone likes Ada∀x L(x, a)
Ada likes everyone∀y L(a, y)

Which says “Everyone likes Ben”?

03 · Two quantifiers

"Everyone likes someone" has two meanings

Here's the most important idea in the chapter. "Everyone likes someone" can mean two different things.

Meaning one: each person likes somebody or other, maybe a different somebody for each person. For all x, there is a y, such that x likes y.

∀x∃y L(x, y)Each person has someone they like. It can be a different someone for each.

Meaning two: there's one particular person whom everybody likes. There is a y, such that for all x, x likes y.

∃y∀x L(x, y)One single person is liked by everyone.

Picture three people. Ada likes Ben, Ben likes Cal, and Cal likes Ada. Everyone likes someone, so meaning one is true. But no single person is liked by all three, so meaning two is false.

Ada → Ben → Cal → Ada
∀x∃y L(x, y) · true
∃y∀x L(x, y) · false
An arrow means “likes”. Everyone has someone, but nobody is everyone's someone.

A way to read them: whatever comes first gets picked first. With "for all x, there is a y", you pick any x, and then the y is allowed to depend on which x you picked. With "there is a y, for all x", one y has to work for every x at once.

Sliding from the first meaning to the second is a classic mistake, called the quantifier shift fallacy. "Every lock has a key that opens it" does not mean "there's one key that opens every lock".

∀x∃y … does not give ∃y∀x …Every lock has a key, but that doesn't mean there's a master key.

Two quantifiers of the same kind can swap freely. "For all x, for all y" says the same as "for all y, for all x", and two backwards E's work the same way. Order only matters when you mix all with some.

Domain: people. Which says “There is someone who likes everyone”?

04 · Stepping stones

Long sentences, one step at a time

Long sentences get much easier if you go in steps, writing half in English and half in symbols. Those half-way lines aren't real FOL. They're scratch work, like doodles in a margin, and they keep you from getting lost.

Symbolization keydomain: people and cats
C(x): x is a cat
O(x, y): x owns y
F(x, y): x feeds y
a: Ada

Start small. "Ada owns a cat" means: there's a cat that Ada owns. There is an x, such that x is a cat, and Ada owns x.

∃x (C(x) ∧ O(a, x))“Ada owns a cat.”

Now "someone owns a cat". Step one: there is a y such that y owns a cat. Step two: "y owns a cat" has the same shape we just built, with y where Ada was.

step 1, scratch∃y (y owns a cat)
step 2∃y ∃x (C(x) ∧ O(y, x))

Now "every cat owner feeds a cat". Step one: for all y, if y owns a cat, then y feeds a cat. Step two: fill in both halves with the same shape.

step 1, scratch∀y (y owns a cat → y feeds a cat)
step 2∀y (∃x (C(x) ∧ O(y, x)) → ∃z (C(z) ∧ F(y, z)))

See how the second cat gets a new letter, z? One variable can't serve two quantifiers at the same time. While a quantifier's brackets are still open, give the next quantifier a different letter. New letters are always safe.

One variable can't serve two quantifiers at once.

05 · Hidden quantifiers

When English doesn't say which

English often leaves the quantifiers out. "Dogs chase cats" could mean every dog chases every cat, or some dog chases some cat, or something in between. Writing it in FOL forces you to choose.

every dog chases every cat∀x (D(x) → ∀y (C(y) → H(x, y)))
some dog chases some cat∃x (D(x) ∧ ∃y (C(y) ∧ H(x, y)))
D(x): x is a dog · C(x): x is a cat · H(x, y): x chases y

The textbook shows why this matters with Mary Astell, a philosopher writing around the year seventeen hundred. A writer claimed that men are naturally superior to women. Astell asked which claim he meant.

If he meant every man is superior to every woman, then even the Queen would have to obey her footman, which nobody would accept. If he meant some man is superior to some woman, that's no discovery, since some woman is also superior to some man. Pinning down the quantifiers took his claim apart.

Wrap-up

What to remember

  1. The key's variables label the gaps. Check which name goes in which gap.
  2. Whatever quantifier comes first gets picked first.
  3. All-then-some and some-then-all mean different things.
  4. Build long sentences in steps, with a new letter for each quantifier.

Domain: people. Which says “Someone is liked by everyone”?
