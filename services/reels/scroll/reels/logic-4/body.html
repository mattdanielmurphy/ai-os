01 · Definite descriptions

"The thief" isn't a name

Phrases like "the thief", "the tallest student", or "Ada's dog" are called definite descriptions. They aim to pick out one thing by describing it.

You could just give the thief a name, say t. But then you'd lose the information that whoever it is, they're a thief. Worse, names must always pick out something in the domain, and some descriptions pick out nothing, like "the unicorn in my garden".

Bertrand Russell found a way to avoid names entirely. He said: "The F is G" means three things. There is at least one F. There is at most one F. And every F is G.

at least one thief∃x T(x)
at most one thief∀x∀y ((T(x) ∧ T(y)) → x = y)
every thief is tall∀x (T(x) → A(x))
Russell's three parts of “The thief is tall.” T(x): x is a thief · A(x): x is tall

The first two parts together just say "exactly one thief", the shape from reel 3. So the short version is: there is a thief x, every thief is x, and x is tall.

∃x (T(x) ∧ ∀y (T(y) → x = y) ∧ A(x))“The thief is tall.” Exactly one thief, and that one is tall.
The F is G: there's exactly one F, and it is G.

"Ada is the thief" is the same shape, ending with x equals a instead of "x is tall".

∃x (T(x) ∧ ∀y (T(y) → x = y) ∧ x = a)“Ada is the thief.” a: Ada

"The thief is the cook" has two descriptions. There's exactly one thief x, exactly one cook y, and x equals y. That last part has to sit inside the scope of both quantifiers.

∃x∃y (T(x) ∧ ∀z (T(z) → x = z) ∧ C(y) ∧ ∀z (C(z) → y = z) ∧ x = y)“The thief is the cook.” C(x): x is a cook

On Russell's analysis, two people are thieves, both tall. Is “The thief is tall” true?

02 · When nothing fits

Empty descriptions, and two kinds of "not"

Russell's method handles descriptions that pick out nothing. "The unicorn in my garden is friendly" just comes out false, because there's no unicorn there. No name ever had to point at nothing.

Now add "not": "The unicorn in my garden is not friendly". That can mean two things, depending on where the "not" goes.

Outer not: it's not true that the unicorn is friendly. That's true, since there is no unicorn. Inner not: there's exactly one unicorn, and it's unfriendly. That's false, for the same reason.

outer not · true¬∃x (U(x) ∧ ∀y (U(y) → x = y) ∧ F(x))
inner not · false∃x (U(x) ∧ ∀y (U(y) → x = y) ∧ ¬F(x))
U(x): x is a unicorn in my garden · F(x): x is friendly

The inner version promises a unicorn exists. The outer version doesn't. So the inner one implies the outer one, but not the other way around.

03 · Possessives, both, neither

"Ada's dog" is a description too

A possessive like "Ada's dog" is a hidden definite description: the dog that Ada owns. So "Ada's dog barks" is false in three ways. If Ada has no dog. If she has two or more. Or if her one dog doesn't bark.

To write it, paraphrase first: the dog Ada owns barks. Then use the same shape, with "is a dog Ada owns" in the F spot.

∃x (D(x) ∧ O(a, x) ∧ ∀y ((D(y) ∧ O(a, y)) → x = y) ∧ B(x))“Ada's dog barks.” D(x): x is a dog · O(x, y): x owns y · B(x): x barks

"Both" and "neither" work the same way, but with exactly two. "Both F's are G" means there are exactly two F's, and each is G. "Neither F is G" means there are exactly two F's, and neither is G.

∃x∃y (F(x) ∧ F(y) ∧ ¬ x = y ∧ ∀z (F(z) → (x = z ∨ y = z)) ∧ G(x) ∧ G(y))“Both F's are G.” For “neither”, end with ¬G(x) ∧ ¬G(y).

04 · Is Russell right?

Two objections worth knowing

Peter Strawson argued that when nothing fits the description, the sentence isn't false. It's neither true nor false, because what it takes for granted fails. But our logic has only two truth values, so taking his side would mean changing the logic.

Keith Donnellan raised mistaken identity. Someone says "the man drinking a martini is tall", pointing at a tall man. But the tall man's glass holds water, and a short man nearby is the one with the martini. On Russell's analysis, the speaker said something false, even though they clearly meant something true about the man they pointed at.

The book's answer: the speaker had a false belief about the drinks, which is why they said something false. For this course, stick with Russell's analysis.

05 · Ambiguity

FOL forces you to pick a meaning

An English sentence can mean two things. A sentence of FOL can't, because every quantifier and every "not" has an exact scope. So when English is ambiguous, you write one FOL sentence for each meaning.

Take "All the students didn't pass". It might mean no student passed. Or it might mean not all of them passed, so some did and some didn't. The difference is whether the "not" is inside the "all", or outside it.

no student passed∀x (S(x) → ¬P(x))
not every student passed¬∀x (S(x) → P(x))
S(x): x is a student · P(x): x passed

Quantifier order makes ambiguity too. "Every student read a book" might mean they all read the same book, or each read some book or other.

one book for all∃x (K(x) ∧ ∀y (S(y) → R(y, x)))
a book each∀y (S(y) → ∃x (K(x) ∧ R(y, x)))
K(x): x is a book · R(x, y): x read y. In the first, the ∃ has wide scope; in the second, the ∀ does.

"Only" can be ambiguous as well. "Only new phones are expensive" could mean only new phones, and nothing else, are expensive. Or it could mean that among phones, only the new ones are expensive. Stress in speech usually tells you which.

anything expensive is a new phone∀x (E(x) → (N(x) ∧ H(x)))
expensive phones are new∀x ((H(x) ∧ E(x)) → N(x))
H(x): x is a phone · N(x): x is new · E(x): x is expensive

And descriptions add one more kind: "the unicorn is not friendly" from earlier had two readings, with the "not" inside or outside the description. Same idea: wide scope versus narrow scope.

“Every guest brought a gift” where all the guests chipped in on one shared gift. Which reading fits?

Wrap-up

What to remember

  1. "The F is G" means exactly one F, and it is G.
  2. An empty description makes "the F is G" false, and "not" can go inside or outside.
  3. Possessives, "both" and "neither" are descriptions in disguise.
  4. When English means two things, write one FOL sentence for each.
